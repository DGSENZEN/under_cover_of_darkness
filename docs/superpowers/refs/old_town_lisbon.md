# The old town: Lisbon after 1755 (references, Oct 2 2026)

Research pack for the old town district of "the city on the rock": dense houses, stair-lanes, small squares and rooftops climbing from the harbour wall to the cathedral square, with a night watch in the streets and the roofs as the thief's highway. It covers Lisbon from the 1755 earthquake to about 1900: the Pombaline Baixa and its standard house, azulejo facades, the hills around the Baixa, and the city's street life at night. Read-only research, compiled 2026-10-02.

## How to read this

Each number carries a source link after it. "en.wiki" and "pt.wiki" are Wikipedia in English and Portuguese. "SIPA" and "DGPC" are the state heritage inventories. "CML" is Lisbon's city council (its Baixa safeguarding-plan documents). "Thesis" means a Portuguese engineering or architecture dissertation, usually IST (Lisbon) or UNL. Labels: **(not found)** means I looked and found no reliable figure. **(conflict)** means sources disagree, and every value is given. **(derived)** is my arithmetic from sourced numbers, with the working shown. **(design)** is a value I propose for the game where no source exists. **(later, borrowable)** marks things from after 1900. A *palmo* is 0.22 m in Miranda's thesis (60 palmos = 13.2 m) and 0.225 m in Nunes's (conflict, small). Dates sit in brackets after the thing they date. **Model it** and **Play it** are my design notes for a PS2-budget Godot level (metres = Godot units). They build on the sourced facts above them. **Images** are Wikimedia Commons file pages (not downloads). On 2026-10-02 I checked through the Commons API that each one exists, and I matched them to places by their titles and categories without opening the photos. This report does not repeat the earlier `iberian.md` entries for Alfama, the Rua da Prata Roman galleries, the aqueduct or the castle; it adds to them.

## 1. The Baixa Pombalina: the plan and the grid

**What and why:** after the 1755 earthquake, tsunami and fire, the destroyed lower town was rebuilt on its own rubble as a strict, hierarchical grid of streets and standardised blocks. It is the model for a planned, rebuilt quarter at the foot of the old town.

**Features**
- The engineer Manuel da Maia wrote a *Dissertação* (Dec 1755-Mar 1756) setting out the options, from leaving things as they were to rebuilding on a new site. Three teams drew 6 plans. The plan chosen was Captain Eugénio dos Santos's, with Carlos Mardel ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). The law regulating the rebuilding was the alvará of May 1758. A dedicated office, the *Casa do Risco*, managed the works: Santos ran it until his death (1760), then Mardel (to 1763), Miguel Ângelo Blasco (1763-70), Reinaldo Manuel dos Santos (1770-91) and Manuel Caetano de Sousa (1792-1802) (SIPA, ibid.).
- **Blocks:** north of Rua da Conceição the centre is **30 rectangular blocks, c. 70 m north-south by c. 25 m east-west**. South of it the blocks turn east-west ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). Most blocks were set north-south, which Miranda's thesis says was the usual direction of the shaking, so only the short ends faced it ([Miranda 2011, UNL thesis](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). The block, not the building, was the unit of design: a continuous front hid how many plots it held, and plots were sized in modules ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)).
- **Street hierarchy:** SIPA gives three classes, main streets, *travessas* and secondary streets, at **60, 40 and 30 palmos** ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). (Conflict) Miranda gives main 60 palmos (13.2 m) and secondary 40 palmos (8.8 m), with a 10-palmo (2.2 m) pavement each side ([Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). Nunes quotes the 1758 law: main streets 60 palmos (40 for vehicles plus 10 each side for pedestrians), secondary 40 (20 plus 10 plus 10) ([Nunes 2017, UNL thesis](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/23393/1/Nunes_2017.pdf)). en.wiki rounds it to "40 ft (12 m)" ([en.wiki Pombaline style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pombaline_style)).
- **Named streets:** main streets Áurea (do Ouro), Augusta, Bela da Rainha (da Prata from 1910) and Nova da Princesa (dos Fanqueiros from 1910). Secondary streets Crucifixo, Sapateiros, Correeiros and Douradores. Cross streets Santa Justa, Assunção, Vitória and São Nicolau ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966); [Toponímia de Lisboa](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/2014/12/23/a-rua-da-prata-que-foi-bela-da-rainha/)). Rua Augusta is **550 m** long ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_Augusta_(Lisboa))).
- **Trades by street (decree of 5 Nov 1760, which named 14 streets):** Rua Augusta for silk and wool merchants ([Toponímia de Lisboa](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/)). Rua Áurea for goldsmiths, with watchmakers in the leftover shops ([Toponímia](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/2014/01/09/a-rua-aurea-dos-ourives-pombalinos/)). Rua Bela da Rainha for silversmiths, with booksellers in the leftovers ([Toponímia](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/2014/12/23/a-rua-da-prata-que-foi-bela-da-rainha/)). Rua Nova da Princesa for cloth, linen and small hardware dealers ([Lisboa Secreta, secondary](https://lisboasecreta.co/6-curiosidades-sobre-a-rua-augusta/)). Rua dos Correeiros for strap-makers, saddlers and turners ([Toponímia](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/2015/11/18/a-rua-dos-correeiros-seleiros-e-torneiros/)). The noble trades took the main streets and the manual trades the secondary ones ([Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
- **Infrastructure:** the town was rebuilt on a raised platform of earthquake rubble against tidal flooding. A *cloaca* (main sewer) ran down the middle of the main streets: **10 palmos wide by 14 high** (1758 law; about 2.2 x 3.1 m, derived). Secondary streets had none, and carts collected refuse there ([Nunes 2017](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/23393/1/Nunes_2017.pdf); [Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). pt.wiki adds a well in every block and the fire walls between buildings ([pt.wiki Baixa](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baixa_Pombalina)).
- **Phases:** under Pombal, building concentrated on Rua Augusta (partly paid for by the state) and the blocks next to Praça do Comércio. Most of the central grid went up under Maria I. After 1808 work moved to the edges. From about 1780 an extra floor above the cornice was allowed, which changed the original types. The inspection office closed in 1808 ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). The protected Pombaline area covers about 70 ha ([UNESCO tentative list](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6226/)).

**Model it**
- Lay a block as a 70 x 25 m rectangle made of 2-6 building shells per long side. Use corner buildings (four-pitch roofs) at each end. The middle of the block is a thin service void (the *saguão*, section 4).
- Streets: 13.2 m main (a 8.8 m roadway plus two 2.2 m pavements), 8.8 m secondary. Use the narrower 30-palmo (6.6 m) class as alleys.
- One shared texture set per street class (types A/B/C, section 4) carries the hierarchy cheaply.

**Play it**
- Wide, straight, lamplit streets are long sightlines for the watch. The thief lives on the roofs and in the block's inner slot.
- Every block is the same height (section 4), so the rooftops form a level plateau broken only by fire walls: a highway with a rhythm of low walls to vault.
- The *cloaca* under the main streets, about 2.2 x 3.1 m (derived), is a ready-made underground route. The secondary streets have none, which gives a natural limit to the network.
- Trade streets set the loot: gold and watches on one street, silver and books on the next, cloth on a third. A district map tells the player where to rob.

## 2. Praça do Comércio and the Arco da Rua Augusta

**What and why:** the royal square on the river, rebuilt as a *place royale* with arcades on three sides. It is the model for a monumental, exposed square at the foot of the old town.

**Features**
- (Conflict) c. 36,000 m², 180 x 200 m, bounded by **79 arches** ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_do_Com%C3%A9rcio)), against 175 x 175 m (30,600 m²) in en.wiki (see `iberian.md` 1.6).
- The arcades were reserved for this square alone. The equestrian statue of José I, by Machado de Castro, was unveiled on 6 June 1775 ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966); [pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_do_Com%C3%A9rcio)).
- The buildings were yellow, painted pink after 1910 and recently yellow again. Marble steps at the Cais das Colunas lead down to the water ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_do_Com%C3%A9rcio)).
- The **Arco da Rua Augusta** was first designed as a bell tower and finished as an arch in **1873** ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_Augusta_Arch)), to a design by Veríssimo José da Costa ([Toponímia](https://toponimialisboa.wordpress.com/)). It has six columns of about **11 m** and a top cornice over **30 m** high. The figure of Glory on top is **7.0 m** tall ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_Augusta_Arch)). It is a later monument, so borrow its 1870s form.

**Model it:** a single arcade-bay module (pier, arch, vault) is repeated along three sides. The arch is one hero mesh. The square itself is a flat plane with a pavement texture, fog and vertex-lit pools around the lamps.

**Play it:** the arcade is the covered, shadowed route around a square no one can cross unseen. The arch is a climbable landmark tower with a view down Rua Augusta. The water stairs are a boat landing.

## 3. Rossio and Praça da Figueira

**What and why:** the town's two northern squares. Rossio was the place of crowds, cafés and executions; Figueira was the market. Together they suggest a "people's square" and a market square for the old town.

**Features**
- **Rossio** was rebuilt to Mardel's plan as a rectangle of **166 x 52 m** ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_D._Pedro_IV)). Mardel is credited with the double (mansard) roofs there, later used elsewhere ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). The Arco do Bandeira on the south side leads into Rua dos Sapateiros (SIPA, ibid.).
- On Rossio, the Inquisition palace burned in 1836 and the Teatro D. Maria II replaced it (opened 1846) ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_D._Pedro_IV)). The square was paved in black-and-white wave mosaic in 1848 ([en.wiki Portuguese pavement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_pavement)). Bronze fountains from France and the Pedro IV column followed. (Conflict) en.wiki gives the column's date as 1874 but captions its unveiling 1870 ([en.wiki Rossio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossio)). Café Nicola dates from the 18th c. (ibid.). The first kiosk opened here in 1869 (section 14).
- **Praça da Figueira** was given to the city on 23 Nov 1775: **380 x 440 palmos** (about 84 x 97 m, derived) on condition it held the sale of fruit, vegetables and poultry. It began as an open-air market on beaten earth, then gained small stalls and a well dug where a fig tree stood. Trees and lighting came in 1834 and iron railings in 1849. A covered iron market of **8,000 m²**, with 3 naves and 4 domed corner towers, opened in 1885 and was auctioned off in 1949 ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_da_Figueira)). The square measures 12,000 (units not stated; m² assumed) (ibid.).

**Model it:** stalls are kit-bashed from 3-4 parts (trestle, awning card, crates, baskets) on a dirt texture. Use the 1849 railing as a perimeter fence module. The 1885 hall is a later hero piece (borrowable).

**Play it:** a market by day becomes an empty square of stalls and awnings at night: plenty of low cover, crate stacks to climb, and a well. Iron railings close it off and channel movement through gates.

## 4. THE POMBALINE BUILDING, in depth

**What and why:** the standard rental building of the rebuilt city: a stone shell around a three-dimensional braced timber cage, with a shop floor in vaulted masonry, flats above and an attic in the roof. It is the backbone of the old town's housing kit.

### 4a. Structure: the *gaiola* (cage)

- The *gaiola* is a three-dimensional braced timber frame set inside the walls, built **from the first upper floor**. The ground floor is all masonry: stone arches and brick groin vaults. That kept fire from the shops' hearths away from the timber and kept the timber off the high water table ([Cardoso, Lopes & Bento 2004, WCEE paper](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf); [Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). Mardel tested a model by having troops march out of step on a platform in the Terreiro do Paço ([Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). The cage was built first, then the masonry infill and outer walls, then the stone frames. Gaiola construction was abandoned by about the 1880s ([WCEE 2004](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)).
- **Three wall types** ([Miranda 2011](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf); [Fernandes 2020, IST](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1126295043838046/Extended%20Abstract_AnaMargaridaFernandes_81156.pdf)):
  - **Outer walls:** rendered rubble with a simple frame of uprights and rails inside and no diagonals. Thickness: **0.90 m at ground level, decreasing upward** ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). (Conflict) Other values are 0.80 m ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)), about 1 m constant ([Valério 2011](https://scispace.com/pdf/caracterizacao-de-edificios-antigos-edificios-pombalinos-48z681kr4o.pdf)), 0.90-1.10 m ([Ferreira 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/h3dthwlv4s3)) and a minimum of 90 cm ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). The cage sits on sill beams (*frechais*) of 0.14 x 0.10 m set about 0.05 m back from the inner face. Iron cramps tie the stone window frames to the timber so they cannot fall into the street ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
  - ***Frontal* walls** (inner, structural): a timber lattice of St Andrew's crosses, infilled with lime mortar, small stones and rubble brick, rendered both sides. They are 15-20 cm thick ([search summary of the ResearchGate literature, unverified](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271630345_Simple_and_complex_modelling_of_timber-framed_masonry_walls_in_Pombalino_buildings)) or 18-25 cm ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)).
  - ***Tabiques*** (partitions): vertical boards with nailed laths, rendered, about 10 cm thick ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)), or 10-15 cm ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)).
  - **Party walls (*paredes meeiras*):** rendered stone, **no openings, about 0.5 m thick**, rising from the ground floor **to above the roof** as fire walls ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf); [CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). Ferreira gives 0.50-0.70 m ([Ferreira 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/h3dthwlv4s3)). The gable walls were heightened so fire could not jump from roof to roof ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6226/)). Height above the roof: **(not found)**.
- **Timber sections:** uprights 12 x 10 cm; rails 12 x 10 cm on upper floors and 15 x 12 cm on lower; diagonals 7 x 10 cm upper and 10 x 10 cm lower; floor beams 12 x 16 cm ([Mascarenhas Mateus via Nunes 2017](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/23393/1/Nunes_2017.pdf)). CML gives averages of 15 x 13 cm and 10 x 13 cm ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). Wood is oak or holm oak, with lighter *casquinha* (Baltic pine) in later buildings ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf); CML).
- **Floors:** joists of *casquinha* or oak, **13 x 18 cm at 40-60 cm centres**, perpendicular to the street front. Pine floorboards are **16 x 2.2 cm** and run as a border frame round the edge of the reception rooms. Ceilings are lapped boards nailed straight to the joists (later, stucco on laths) ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
- **Foundations:** green-pine piles about **1.5 m long and 0.15 m in diameter, 0.40 m apart** in two rows under the main walls. A timber grid ties their heads and a rubble raft tops it about 0.5 m below the door sill ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
- **Ground floor:** stone pillars and walls (about 0.90 m) carry cut-stone arches and **quadripartite brick groin vaults**, backfilled with rubble. It is fireproof and open-plan. Shops on the main streets had big stone slab floors; stables and stores on the secondary streets had cobbles or earth floors and the more solid vaulted variant ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf); [Fernandes 2020](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1126295043838046/Extended%20Abstract_AnaMargaridaFernandes_81156.pdf)). There is an air gap between the first-floor boards and the rubble over the vaults ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). There are cellars, with vents behind wrought-iron grilles (ibid.).

### 4b. The facade

- **Storeys:** Maia's first elevations allowed 3 storeys (shops plus 2). The final projects have **5 levels: shops, 3 floors and an attic** ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). The ground floor was to have a clear height of 16 palmos and the rest of the height was to be divided equally. No building could rise above the buildings of the Terreiro do Paço (1758 law; [Nunes 2017](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/23393/1/Nunes_2017.pdf)). (Conflict) The WCEE paper says 3 storeys was the limit but most buildings got 4 ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)).
- **Storey heights:** ground floor and first (noble) floor are generally **16 palmos, about 3.70 m**. Upper floors are lower, and the attic stays **above 2.50 m** ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). Elsewhere in the same thesis the stone ground floor is **about 4 m** (conflict, small). Exact heights of the 2nd and 3rd floors: **(not found)**.
- **Openings:** vertically aligned. **Each opening is as wide as the pier between openings**, except on the ground floor ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). There are **3 to 6 openings per storey**, depending on width ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)), most often 3 or 4 ([Fernandes](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1126295043838046/Extended%20Abstract_AnaMargaridaFernandes_81156.pdf)). Plot fronts were multiples of the facade's base module ([Valério 2011](https://scispace.com/pdf/caracterizacao-de-edificios-antigos-edificios-pombalinos-48z681kr4o.pdf)). One real example, a corner building in Rua da Assunção: **12.70 m with 4 openings per floor, and 15.60 m with 5** on the Rua dos Sapateiros side, on a 192 m² plot ([Espaço de Arquitetura](https://espacodearquitetura.com/projetos/edificio-pombalino-a-rua-da-assuncao/)). Measured window and door sizes: **(not found)**; see the derived module in the kit section.
- **Opening types:** first floor *janelas de sacada* (French-window balconies, two-leaf casements with a fixed or bottom-hinged transom). Upper floors *janelas de peito* (sill windows) with sashes. Dormers also have sashes. Cellar vents have wrought-iron grilles ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). Windows first had wooden shutters with small hinged flaps (*postigos*); the first glazing was in sashes. Balcony guards are cast-iron balusters ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). Type A fronts have stone balconies (*sacadas de pedra*) with iron guards ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)). Balcony projection and rail height: **(not found)** (the Porto report `houses_ribeira.md` gives up to 0.5 m and 0.885-0.98 m).
- **Facade types by street** (França 1965, in [SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5966)):
  - **A, main streets** (Augusta, Prata, Ouro, per [EcoArkitekt](https://ecoarkitekt.com/construcao-sustentavel/o-design-das-fachadas-no-chiado/)): shaped (*recortadas*) stone frames on every opening, with jambs dropping below the sill like consoles. The 3rd-floor window arch has a keystone set into the cornice. A stone band separates the first floor, whose stone *sacadas* are linked by a low plinth. Every ground-floor door has a transom light, and the dormers carry scroll ornaments (EcoArkitekt).
  - **B, e.g. São Julião, Fanqueiros:** balconies but no band; plain frames.
  - **C, e.g. Douradores:** no balconies, plain rectangular frames.
  - Ground-floor shop doors mix curved, straight and lightly notched lintels. Secondary streets alternate straight-with-chamfer and wider curved heads, the wider ones for workshops and coach houses ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). On steep streets a mezzanine (*sobreloja*) takes up the slope ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)).
- **Street door:** vertical tongue-and-groove planks, braced inside with iron-riveted rails. Strap hinges (*taleiras*) on pintles, a cut-iron lock plate, and a ring knocker ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)).
- **Materials and colour:** stone frames, balconies and sills in **lioz limestone**. Lime render, painted ([Espaço de Arquitetura](https://espacodearquitetura.com/projetos/edificio-pombalino-a-rua-da-assuncao/)). Above each opening there is a flat brick relieving arch, with a thin brick panel under the sill ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). Today's safeguarding rules, which follow tradition: **white window frames; street doors and their frames all one dark colour, never white; light-well walls white unless tiled; no saturated colours, with a limewash-like sheen**. They also protect the iron grilles on door transoms and *postigos* ([CML regulation, annex 3](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/regulamento_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_regulamento_anexo_III.pdf)). The original render colour palette: **(not found)** (Praça do Comércio was yellow, section 2).

### 4c. The stair, the flats and the services

- **Stair:** reached from the entrance hall through **two cut-stone arches**. It has two flights, stone up to the first landing and timber above. There is **no open well (*sem bomba*)**. The balustrade is masonry with a timber cap. Hall and stair have an azulejo skirting and dado ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). The ground-floor flights are stone for fire safety; above them, timber strings rest on beams at the landings ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). (Conflict) Light: CML says through openings in a facade wall. Miranda says the stairs were originally inside the building with no daylight except a **skylight over the stairwell**: glass set in lead on a metal frame, clad outside in sheet metal, rising above the roof. Fernandes puts the stairs next to the front or back facade for light ([Fernandes](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1126295043838046/Extended%20Abstract_AnaMargaridaFernandes_81156.pdf)). In most buildings the stair is on the building's centre line, except in corner buildings and very narrow ones ([IST thesis 62881](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1970719973966279/DISSERTACAO_62881.pdf)). Stair width: **(not found)**.
- **Flats:** each landing serves **two symmetrical flats, one on each side of the stair**. Each flat has **two entrance doors**: a wider one into the reception rooms and a service door ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). The WCEE paper gives one or two flats per floor ([WCEE](https://www.iitk.ac.in/nicee/wcee/article/13_918.pdf)). The rooms are many and small, divided by *frontal* walls and *tabiques*. Reception rooms face the street (the *sacadas* light the noble floor). Service rooms lie at the back towards the yard, or are **interior rooms with no window**. Glazed fanlights over the inner doors pass light inward ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). CML: **no distribution corridor**; rooms open into each other; rooms are square or nearly square. Window bays sometimes have masonry seats with stone tops, the *conversadeiras* ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). Miranda notes modest corridors appearing (conflict, minor). Tall double-leaf doors link the rooms ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). The attic was lived in, with dormers reached along a narrow corridor (ibid.).
- **Kitchen:** at the back. A cut-stone hearth on a brick arch base, with a narrow, tall brick flue against the party wall or the outer wall ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf); [Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). A **stone slop sink** drains through a fired-clay trap to a clay downpipe inside the outer wall. The pipe is vented above the roof and empties into a channel to the sewer (Miranda). **Only kitchens had water**, kept in ceramic or wooden vessels and fetched from the public fountains. **There were no toilets**; WCs were added later on the light-well walls (Miranda). Kitchens are often lined with leftover tiles (CML). Chimneys on the roof are plain rectangles, topped with tiles set on dabs of mortar (Miranda).
- **The light well (*saguão*):** a narrow, open court inside the building or block that lights and ventilates rooms with no street window (Miranda's glossary). In Maia's scheme each building had an inner court, the *alfurge*, **5-6 palmos wide** (about 1.1-1.35 m, derived). It had **windows onto it but no doors**. Residents emptied waste out of these windows, and the court drained to the street sewer, as did rainwater ([Nunes 2017, citing Maia's Dissertação](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/23393/1/Nunes_2017.pdf)). Later, store sheds filled the ground level of light wells and WCs were stuck onto their walls ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).

### 4d. The roof

- Most roofs are **two-pitch, in canal tiles (*telha de canudo*) on battens**. The eaves tiles are bedded in mortar so the wind cannot throw them into the street. The trusses bear on wall plates over a cornice of paired brick (*ladrilho*), which also throws water clear of the walls ([Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). **Corner buildings have four-pitch roofs** ([IST thesis 62881](https://fenix.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/downloadFile/1970719973966279/DISSERTACAO_62881.pdf)).
- **Mansard ("German") roofs** on the important streets and squares give headroom over the whole attic. The mansard's steep pitch needed nailed tiles ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf); [Miranda](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
- **Dormers (*trapeiras*)** on lesser streets come in a rhythm of **one to two or one to three** bays. They have a masonry front and rendered *frontal* cheeks, and a three-pitch canal-tile roof (CML). They line up with the trusses so their cheeks bear on the rafters (Miranda).
- Roof pitch: **(not found)**. Modern canal-tile manufacturers ask for at least about 36-70% (about 20-35°) depending on exposure, which is modern guidance only ([Cerâmica Torreense](https://www.ceramicatorreense.pt/media/filer_public/08/c3/08c3ce41-492f-4409-b36f-ee280018d066/torreense_-_fichas_de_aplicacao_-_telhas_ceramicas_e_telhados.pdf)).

### 4e. How the type changed, 1780-1900 (the borrowable variants)

All from [CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf):
- **Neoclassical (last quarter of the 18th c. to the 1870s):** still built on the cage, with storeys added (**six storeys common**) and wider plots of **6 or more bays**. Dormers grow into big *trapeirões*. The facade rises into a parapet with gutters and downpipes, and running balconies appear above the cornice. Wrought-iron guards curl into arabesques. The street door gets a cross-rail, then an iron grille above. The stair is lit from a skylight round a square well. Flats gain a small corridor. Walls carry tiled dados with painted plaster above, kitchens are tiled to the ceiling, and **tile saints' panels sit over the entrance arches**.
- **Gaioleiro (last quarter of the 19th c. to the early 20th):** **6-7 storeys**, Marseille tiles and zinc mansards, and a parapet with an inner gutter. Lighter timber, thinner piers. Cast-iron balcony guards, often set flush inside the openings. **The facade is often tiled.** The stair winds round an elliptical well under a skylight. Two flats per floor along a **long corridor**, with one independent room opening straight off the stair.

**Model it**
- The shell (front, back and fire walls) is geometry. The *frontal* lattice exists only where you cut a building open or ruin it (one alpha-free mesh panel per 3 m wall).
- Stone frames, sills, *sacada* slabs and cornice are geometry. Shaped jamb consoles and keystones go in the texture or a normal-free baked AO.
- Vault the ground floor with one groin-vault mesh per bay. Mark the building's age with stair and door variants.

**Play it**
- Sound map: stone floors (ground floor and first flight) are quiet but echo; timber floors and stairs above creak. The thief climbs from stone into timber.
- Every flat has two doors (reception and service): two ways in, and a guard must choose which to watch.
- Interior windowless rooms and alcoves are dark hiding places. Glazed fanlights leak candlelight between rooms, a cheap light-detection gradient.
- The skylight over the stair is a roof entry that drops straight into the stair well (in the neoclassical and later types, an open well).
- The light well, about 1.1-1.35 m wide in Maia's scheme (derived), is a slot to climb up or down by bracing (design). Kitchen and service windows open onto it. Waste was thrown into it, so it is foul and noisy underfoot.
- Fire walls stand above the roofs: low parapets to vault, and cover from a watchman on the next roof.

## 5. Azulejo facades (1838-1900)

**What and why:** from the late 1830s Lisbon began cladding street fronts in mass-produced pattern tiles, framed around every opening. This is the strongest "Lisbon" signal for a 19th-century layer of the old town.

**Features**
- **Dates:** the earliest dated tiled street front is Rua Nova da Trindade (now the Cervejaria Trindade), with **1838** on its balcony ironwork. Its tiles were recycled 18th-c. tiles, because factory facade tiles did not exist yet ([Mimoso 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5swzkdnn83s)). Count Raczyński mentions fully tiled fronts by **1845** ([Mimoso & Pereira, LNEC 2011](http://www-ext.lnec.pt/AzTek/download/Rel%20303_11.pdf)). Facade tiling spread from the 1830s-40s and grew huge after 1850 ([Patrimónios](https://patrimonios.pt/en/azulejo-de-fachada/)).
- **Why:** to avoid the regular cleaning and repainting that rendered fronts required (LNEC, ibid.). The traditional story of returning emigrants from Brazil after 1822 is now nuanced ([Patrimónios](https://patrimonios.pt/en/azulejo-de-fachada/)). Tiles were easy to clean and gave a dull front a bright new face ([Qualicer 2010 paper](https://www.qualicer.org/recopilatorio/ponencias/pdfs/2010097.pdf)).
- **Factories:** Roseira, which made architectural tiles from **1840** and was perhaps the first in Lisbon ([Mimoso 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5swzkdnn83s)). **Viúva Lamego**, founded **1849** at Largo do Intendente ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A1brica_de_Cer%C3%A2mica_da_Vi%C3%BAva_Lamego)). **Sacavém**, founded 1850 by its own claim but working from 1856, English-run after 1861-63, at 70,000 m² at its peak ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A1brica_de_Loi%C3%A7a_de_Sacav%C3%A9m)). Constância ([Patrimónios](https://patrimonios.pt/en/azulejo-de-fachada/)). **Sant'Anna**, which dates itself to 1741 ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A1brica_Sant%27Anna)).
- **Technique:** from about 1850 tiles were moulded semi-industrially and painted by hand through paper stencils (*estampilha*). Tiles are **7-10 mm thick**, with edges bevelled 5-30°. A front usually uses one design, turned 0/90/180/270° to build the pattern, plus a second, simpler pattern as the edging ([LNEC 2011](http://www-ext.lnec.pt/AzTek/download/Rel%20303_11.pdf)). In the late 19th c. Sacavém made stone-powder tiles, some pressed in relief. **Relief tiles are rare in Lisbon** but common in Porto (ibid.). Transfer printing came in, and later creamware blanks ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azulejo)).
- **Size:** Lisbon tiles are **14 cm** square (as against 13-13.5 cm elsewhere, in the 17th-18th c.) ([Casimiro & Almeida](https://research.unl.pt/ws/portalfiles/portal/15691943/Portuguese_tiles_16th_18th_century_.pdf)). Pattern repeats are **2x2, 4x4, 6x6 and 12x12** tiles ([Museu Nacional do Azulejo, Google Arts](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/the-azulejo-in-portugal-an-identitary-art-a-world-heritage-national-azulejo-museum/agUB70vTvN7aIg?hl=en-GB)). The biggest, "Marvila", is 144 tiles ([Casimiro & Almeida](https://research.unl.pt/ws/portalfiles/portal/15691943/Portuguese_tiles_16th_18th_century_.pdf)).
- **How much of a front, by period** ([Mariz, "As primeiras fachadas azulejadas"](http://azulejos.lnec.pt/AzuRe/links/01%20Primeiras%20fachadas%20azulejadas.pdf)):
  1. Late 1830s to the 1840s: not the whole front. The shop floor wears trompe-l'oeil stone: blue ground with purple-brown imitation panels of red Negrais limestone. Upper floors carry reused 18th-c. borders.
  2. Period patterns on the upper floors, sometimes different on each floor.
  3. Late 1860s to the early 1900s: pattern from top to bottom, sometimes along whole blocks, varied only by friezes and frames. Advertising cartouches in tile appear on shops, mostly from the 1860s.
- **Frames and friezes:** early fronts (the 1840s to the late 1850s) have **wide frames of full-size tiles** in cobalt blue and a **double row of tiles as a frieze under the cornice**. **A frieze on an intermediate floor means the building was heightened later.** Narrow frames came in by the late 1850s with a wider palette, peaking in the 1870s-80s ([Mimoso 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5swzkdnn83s)). **Lisbon frames doors, windows and corners; Porto mostly does not** ([Qualicer](https://www.qualicer.org/recopilatorio/ponencias/pdfs/2010097.pdf)). Some fronts have a fake stone corner made of white tiles ([Mimoso 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5swzkdnn83s)).
- **Social arc:** cheap stencilled tiles spread to the middle classes, so the upper classes dropped full cladding by the end of the century. Critics attacked industrial tiles from the 1880s ([Qualicer](https://www.qualicer.org/recopilatorio/ponencias/pdfs/2010097.pdf)).
- **Examples:** the **Viúva Lamego building, Largo do Intendente 23-27** (built 1849-1865), 2 storeys with 2 windows each and a balcony between the upper windows. A triangular gable holds an oculus framed by garlands. The whole front is figurative tile by Luís Ferreira, "Ferreira das Tabuletas", including allegories of Commerce and Industry at the door ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A1brica_de_Cer%C3%A2mica_da_Vi%C3%BAva_Lamego)). The "Casa das Bolas" (1871-72) has a trompe-l'oeil sphere pattern on its ground floor and over 50 Roseira patterns inside ([Mimoso et al. 2015](https://exa.ai/library/publication/vvflwfpvxfn)). Rua da Mouraria, Rua das Janelas Verdes and Campo de Ourique: **(not found)**, no sourced specifics; the Commons photos below are generic Lisbon tiled fronts.

**Model it**
- Tile is texture, never geometry. One 128 px texture holds a 4x4-tile module (56 cm), so 32 px per tile; it tiles across the wall.
- Frames are a trim sheet: a 1-tile (14 cm) narrow-frame strip and a 2-tile wide frame. Use UV-mapped quads around each opening and the corners.
- The frieze is a 2-tile strip under the cornice. A shop cartouche is a single 256 px decal panel.
- Vertex-paint grime under sills and at the base; tiles gleam, so give them a slightly higher specular than render.

**Play it:** a tiled front reflects lamplight and is too slick to climb (design), whereas rendered fronts with stone frames are climbable. Tiled shopfronts with painted signboards are readable landmarks in the dark. The frieze-on-a-middle-floor detail lets you tell the "this storey was added later" story in the level art.

## 6. Indoor tiles: Pombaline dados and the *figuras de convite* (18th c.)

**What and why:** older blue-and-white tile panels line palace halls, stairs and kitchens, and life-size cut-out tile servants greet visitors on the stairs. They are ideal set dressing for the key buildings.

**Features**
- **Pombaline houses:** dados of pattern tiles with shells, stars, flowers, vases, halberds and fake marble on the walls; leftover tiles in kitchens ([CML](https://www.lisboa.pt/fileadmin/portal/temas/urbanismo/planos_pormenor/baixa_pombalina/pecas_escritas/relatorio_anexos/pp_baixa_pombalina_relatorio_07_tipologias_arquitetonicas_construtivas.pdf)). A dado (*lambril*) is the bottom band of the wall, about **0.90 m** high ([Miranda glossary](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)). After 1755, small devotional tile panels on buildings were meant to protect them from new disasters ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azulejo)).
- ***Figuras de convite*:** invented by the Master "PMP", they are life-size cut-out figures (halberdiers, squires, footmen, rarely ladies or janissaries) placed in entrances, patios and stair landings to welcome guests ([Carita, A Casa Senhorial](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/casos-de-estudo/casosdeestudo/588-figuras-de-convites); [en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azulejo)). They were used from about the 2nd quarter of the 18th c. to the 1st quarter of the 19th ([MNAz via Google Arts](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/the-azulejo-in-portugal-an-identitary-art-a-world-heritage-national-azulejo-museum/agUB70vTvN7aIg?hl=en-GB)). One halberdier of about 1730-40 measures **212.5 x 133 cm** ([Augusto 2024](https://exa.ai/library/publication/08r04d78n2c)). Yellow touches imitate gold embroidery ([Carita](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/casos-de-estudo/casosdeestudo/588-figuras-de-convites)).
- **Stair programmes:** the Marquises of Minas palace in Rua da Rosa (Bairro Alto) has an L-shaped stair with a dado **10 tiles high** on the stair and **8** in the vestibule, about 1.4 m and 1.12 m (derived at 14 cm per tile). The tiles are cut on the diagonal to follow the steps, with sponged blue tiles imitating stone in the triangle under the flight. Painted balustrades are peopled with nobles, children, dogs, cats, monkeys and parrots, and a welcoming figure stands on the top landing ([Carita](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/casos-de-estudo/casosdeestudo/568-escadarias-do-palacio-de-minas)). In a noble house in Rua de São Boaventura there are **five squires, one on each landing**, guiding the visitor upstairs. In the Barbacena palace one figure invites the visitor up and a servant waits on the upper landing ([Carita](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/casos-de-estudo/casosdeestudo/588-figuras-de-convites)). The Palácio Centeno stair (1715-25) tells Ovid's *Metamorphoses* in blue and white ([Carita, PDF](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/artigos?file=pdf&id=26&task=download)).

**Model it:** a figure is one alpha-cut quad (or a thin slab with a cut silhouette) at 2.1 m, carrying a 256 px blue-white texture. Stair dados are a diagonal-cut trim texture. Vertex light the stair so the figure is the brightest thing on the landing.

**Play it:** life-size painted servants on dark landings are perfect false alarms at night. Seen out of the corner of the eye, they read as guards (design). They also mark the route to the noble floor, where the loot is.

## 7. Chiado and the Carmo ruin

**What and why:** on the hill west of the Baixa, the great Gothic Carmo church was left roofless after 1755. Across its square stands a fountain baldachin, and from 1810 the convent next door was a barracks of the night watch. It is a ready-made set piece of ruin, fountain and guardhouse.

**Features**
- **Carmo church** (founded 1389; built to about 1423). In 1755 the stone vault and roof of the nave fell, the fire took the contents, and a library of about 5,000 volumes was lost. The 126 clerics left ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmo_Convent_(Lisbon))). Rebuilding in an experimental neo-Gothic began in 1756 and stopped in 1834. In the mid-19th c. it was decided to leave the nave **open to the sky** as a romantic ruin. The archaeological museum opened in 1864 ([Museu Arqueológico do Carmo](https://www.museuarqueologicodocarmo.pt/mac.html); [pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convento_do_Carmo_(Lisboa))).
- **Dimensions:** originally **72 m** long; central nave **24.64 m** high, aisles **18.70 m**; transept **33 m** long; apse about **15.40 m** high ([Augusto 2024, citing Pereira 2016](https://exa.ai/library/publication/t45x48s87r8)). (Conflict) Others give "almost 70 m" ([RTP Ensina](https://ensina.rtp.pt/artigo/convento-do-carmo-o-gotico-monumental-de-lisboa/)) and 73 m ([Norberto de Araújo via Lisboa de Antigamente](https://lisboadeantigamente.blogspot.com/2021/09/largo-e-convento-do-carmo.html)). Plan: three aisles, a main chapel and four side chapels. **Five flying buttresses** on the south side (1399). The rose window is broken. Only the transverse pointed arches remain over the nave ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmo_Convent_(Lisbon))). Of the five nave arches, only the two next to the portal are original (Araújo, ibid.).
- **The watch next door:** from 1810 the convent housed the **Guarda Real da Polícia**. There was a sharpshooter battalion in 1814 and militia in 1831; municipal-guard infantry companies and, in 1845, a cavalry squadron. In 1835 the church was rented out as a sawmill ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmo_Convent_(Lisbon))). It is still the national guard's headquarters ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convento_do_Carmo_(Lisboa))).
- **Chafariz do Carmo** (begun 1769-70 by Miguel Ângelo Blasco, redesigned 1786 by Reinaldo Manuel dos Santos) ([Discover Baroque Art](https://baroqueart.museumwnf.org/database_item.php?id=monument%3BBAR%3Bpt%3BMon11%3B27%3Ben&pageT=N)). It is a lioz baldachin of **four round arches on Tuscan pillars** under a false dome with an urn, standing on a **two-step circular platform**. Inside, an octagonal water box (with a door) feeds **four spouts into two curved tanks** under a pyramid of **four dolphins** ([SIPA-derived description](https://www.municipiosefreguesias.pt/ponto-de-interesse/3089/chafariz-no-largo-do-carmo)). It had the largest flow of all the aqueduct fountains ([APS blog](https://aps-ruasdelisboacomhistria.blogspot.com/2014/05/largo-do-carmo-iv.html)). In **1851: 4 spouts, 7 companies of water-carriers, 7 foremen, 231 carriers**. In 1875 the council voted to demolish it because of fights between carriers and residents; it was not demolished ([Lisboa de Antigamente](https://lisboadeantigamente.blogspot.com/2015/11/chafariz-do-carmo-o-chafariz-das-pombas.html)).
- The Santa Justa lift (1901-02) bridges from the Baixa into this square (section 15, later).

**Model it:** the nave is a few pillar-and-arch modules with broken wall-tops. Leave the floor as grass and rubble with tomb props, and cut the window tracery as alpha cards. The fountain is one hero mesh with four arches. The convent wing is a plain barracks block with a guarded gate.

**Play it:** a moonlit, roofless nave is open to the sky but full of pillar shadows and fallen stone. The watch's barracks across the square means patrols start here. The fountain's running water masks noise, and its arches give cover in the middle of an open square.

## 8. Bairro Alto and Príncipe Real

**What and why:** a 16th-century grid on the hill above Chiado, of long narrow lots. In the 19th c. it held taverns, printers and newspapers. It is the model for an older, tighter grid quarter on a slope, with nightlife.

**Features**
- First lots were laid out around 1513. By **1527** there were **408 buildings and 1,600 inhabitants**. Streets run perpendicular to the river and lanes parallel to it. The houses sit on **long narrow lots, 3-4 storeys high**, with asymmetric fronts and the stairs along the side. Pombaline fronts are less common ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bairro_Alto)). The quarter was not badly hit in 1755. Between 1760 and 1780 Rua de São Roque and others were widened (ibid.). Rua da Rosa, Atalaia, dos Calafates (now Diário de Notícias), das Gáveas and do Norte belong to the 1530s phase (ibid.).
- In the 19th c. the main newspapers and print shops were here, alongside **sailors' taverns, coal sellers and houses of ill repute** ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bairro_Alto)). Ground floors have quadripartite vaults ([Miranda, fig. 25](https://run.unl.pt/bitstream/10362/5964/1/Miranda_2011.pdf)).
- **Príncipe Real:** under the square lies the **Reservatório da Patriarcal**, designed 1856 and built **1860-64**: **880 m³** of water and **31 pillars, about 9.25 m** high (the pt.wiki sentence is garbled) ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservat%C3%B3rio_da_Patriarcal)).

**Model it:** narrow lot modules 2-3 bays wide (about 5.4-8.1 m, derived from the module in the kit section) and 3-4 storeys. Use the Pombaline door, sacada and peito kit, with less ornament and asymmetric bay spacing.

**Play it:** a grid of narrow streets climbing a slope makes many parallel routes with cross-lanes. Lit tavern doors spill noise and light, and drunks are a moving hazard. The underground reservoir is a pillared, flooded chamber for a secret route or meeting.

## 9. Alfama: becos, pátios, escadinhas

**What and why:** a hillside maze of dead ends, stair-lanes, arches and small squares between the castle and the river. It is the organic counterpart to the grid.

**Features**
- (Conflict) **What 1755 did.** Most buildings survived, says [pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfama). The quake and fire destroyed almost all of Alfama, and the cleared plots rehoused the poor, says [Costa, UCP](https://revistas.ucp.pt/index.php/povoseculturas/article/download/15779/15187). A study of one area classed nearly all its buildings as post-quake gaiola construction ([RCAAP thesis](https://comum.rcaap.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/89128807-454f-4f64-927c-f4b8cad15f65/content)). All three agree that **the street pattern survived** ([Benis 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/dh6z8vyqp9b)).
- **Houses:** on average **3-5 storeys**. Facade walls are **0.50-0.60 m**, about 0.60 m at street level and thinner above. Some 6-storey cases come from later additions ([RCAAP thesis](https://comum.rcaap.pt/server/api/core/bitstreams/89128807-454f-4f64-927c-f4b8cad15f65/content)).
- **Lanes:** the only real street is **Rua dos Remédios**, straight and wider. You enter the quarter through tight arches, lanes or *escadinhas*. In a 20th-c. description, the dark *becos* (dead ends) are dotted at night with wrought-iron lamps ([Costa, UCP](https://revistas.ucp.pt/index.php/povoseculturas/article/download/15779/15187)). The **Largo do Chafariz de Dentro**, where **seven lanes** meet, is the quarter's hub ([Benis 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/dh6z8vyqp9b)). Beco do Carneiro is **29.5 m** long with 8 addresses ([RuaGuia](https://ruaguia.com/lisboa/beco-do-carneiro/)). Lane widths: **(not found)**.
- **Chafariz d'El-Rei** (known since at least the 13th c.): its front collapsed in 1744 and was rebuilt in **1747 with 9 spouts** (up from 6). The present classical front was finished in **1864**. Each spout was reserved for one social group (rules from 1551). In **1851** it had **9 spouts, 10 companies, 10 foremen and 330 water-carriers**, and the overflow ran to the river ([Lisboa de Antigamente](https://lisboadeantigamente.blogspot.com/2016/04/palacete-do-chafariz-del-rey-ou.html); [pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chafariz_de_El-Rei)). The **Chafariz de Dentro**, locally the "Tanque dos Cavalos", was where horses and donkeys drank ([Benis 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/dh6z8vyqp9b)).
- **Pátios** (courtyards behind walls) on the castle hill and in Alfama, from [e-cultura](http://www.e-cultura.pt/artigo/19375):
  - Pátio de D. Fradique: on two levels, entered by a 17th-c. portal, built against the Moorish wall and one of its towers.
  - Pátio do Curvo: reached through an elbow-shaped passage.
  - Pátio José Pedreira: behind a high wall and gate, with a well, a vegetable garden and an animal pen.

**Model it:** use 6-10 house shells of 2-3 bays, 3-5 storeys, with 0.5-0.6 m walls, built from the Pombaline kit but plainer. Stair-lane modules: straight flights, landings and switchbacks with iron handrails. Add arch-over-lane modules and laundry-line cards. Pátios are walled boxes holding a well, garden and pen.

**Play it:** dead ends trap both thief and guard. Stair-lanes give height changes and sightline breaks. The fountain square is a hub where seven lanes meet, with water noise to hide footsteps. Walled pátios are pockets to hide in or rob, entered over the wall or through a gate.

## 10. Mouraria, Graça and the *vilas operárias*

**What and why:** the Mouraria is the old Moorish quarter, the birthplace of fado. Graça is the hill above it, with its convent, lookouts and late 19th-c. workers' housing built as gated private streets and courtyards. It gives the poor quarter of the old town.

**Features**
- **Mouraria:** the Moors' quarter after 1147. The fado singer Maria Severa lived in Rua do Capelão by the Beco dos Três Engenhos ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouraria)). Its lower streets were demolished in the 20th c. to make Martim Moniz square (ibid.).
- **Graça:** the parish had **1,993** inhabitants in 1864 ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gra%C3%A7a_(Lisboa))). Lanes named Caracol da Graça, Escadas do Monte and Escadinhas Damasceno Monteiro show the climb (ibid.).
- **Pátios:** courtyard tenements behind or under existing buildings, often in basements. Most had **no water or sewer, damp and no sun**. An official survey of **1902 counted 130 in 18 of the city's 29 parishes**, so over 200 in all ([Teotónio Pereira](https://nunoteotoniopereira.pt/multimedia/patios-e-vilas-de-lisboa-1994/)).
- **Vilas** were the planned successor from the 1870s-80s. Types: rows (*correntezas*) of 2-4 dwellings; vilas round a central court; vilas hidden behind a bourgeois street building and entered by a side passage or an arch with an iron gate and a name plaque; and private streets. Iron **galleries and stairs** replaced interior stairs to save space. Typical touches were symmetry, enamelled name plaques and decorated roof edges. A 1930 regulation defined vilas and banned new ones ([Teotónio Pereira](https://nunoteotoniopereira.pt/multimedia/patios-e-vilas-de-lisboa-1994/)).
- **Vila Sousa** (Graça, **1889**): built into a former palace, a **five-storey** block round a spacious courtyard, clad in blue tiles ([Teotónio Pereira](https://nunoteotoniopereira.pt/multimedia/patios-e-vilas-de-lisboa-1994/)).
- **Vila Berta** (Graça), by the architect-builder Joaquim Francisco Tojal. (Conflict) Built **1902-1908** ([DGPC](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=72359)) or 1909-1911 ([vilaberta.pt](https://www.vilaberta.pt/vila-berta/)). Layout:
  - **Two parallel rows facing an inner street.** The west side has the larger **3-storey** houses with garden terraces and square balconies on **cast-iron pillars** set at the street edge. The east side has **2 storeys plus a cellar** and smaller balconies, under **one continuous two-pitch roof** ([DGPC](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=72359); [SIPA-derived](https://www.municipiosefreguesias.pt/ponto-de-interesse/7511/vila-berta)).
  - **Two flats per floor** on shared stairs, **14 buildings** ([vilaberta.pt](https://www.vilaberta.pt/vila-berta/)) and **81 dwellings** today ([Visão](https://visao.pt/visao-arquivo/2024-02-20-na-vila-operaria-ao-som-dos-tuck-tucks/)).
  - The main entrance is a passage through the ground floor of Rua do Sol à Graça 55-59, under an Art Nouveau tile frieze reading "Villa Bertha". It was **closed by gates at the north and south ends**. A high tiled plinth runs under the windows, and cellars housed the builder's workers ([DGPC](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=72359)).
  - It is 1900s, so borrowable as late as the setting allows.

**Model it:** a vila is a private street of one or two repeated row modules (3 x 2 bays), with an entrance arch module carrying a tile name-frieze. Iron balconies on thin pillars are cheap geometry: four pillars, a slab and a rail card. The iron gallery is a walkway module with a stair.

**Play it:** a gated private street gives a single watched entrance and a locked gate at the far end. The pillar balconies are easy climbs to the upper floors. The galleries are exposed walkways that ring underfoot. A pátio entered through someone's building is a hidden courtyard of poor dwellings: witnesses, dogs and washing lines.

## 11. Castelo de São Jorge and Santa Cruz do Castelo

**What and why:** the castle on the summit, and a small walled village of narrow lanes inside its outer walls. It is the model for the transition from the old town to the castle.

**Features**
- **Castle after 1755:** apart from the old walls, the soldiers' hospital and the Recolhimento were left in ruins. The Casa Pia charity school worked inside the citadel from **1780 to 1807**, with soldiers still garrisoned there. Portugal's first geodetic observatory went up on a tower in **1788**. The keep (*castelejo*) has **ten towers** at the citadel's high north-west corner. An inner wall splits the courtyard. A **barbican** guards the south and east, and a now-dry **moat** is crossed by a stone bridge at the main gate ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Jorge_Castle)). The look of the castle today comes from the 1940s restoration (later). The 1373-75 city wall had **77 towers, 34 or 38 gates and a 5,400 m perimeter** (ibid.).
- **Santa Cruz do Castelo:** the parish covers **0.06 km²** ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castelo_(Lisboa))). After 1755 it was rebuilt without a formal plan, along the old streets. In **1776** it had **eleven narrow, irregular streets inside the walls**, with Rua de Santa Cruz do Castelo as the spine to the **only gate**, near the Chão da Feira ([pt.wiki, church](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igreja_de_Santa_Cruz_do_Castelo)).
- **The church** was rebuilt after 1755 to a design by João Paulo. Its bell tower stands on a tower of the citadel wall: **50 steps**, called the tallest in old Lisbon (ibid.).
- **Lanes and courts:** Beco do Forno do Castelo (officially named 1859); Rua do Recolhimento, after a home for orphaned noble girls destroyed in 1755; Rua das Cozinhas; Beco do Leão; and the Pátio da Pascácia, Pátio das Cozinhas, Pátio do Sequeiro and Pátio José Pedreira ([pt.wiki, street list](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lista_de_arruamentos_do_Castelo_(Lisboa))). The **Pátio das Cozinhas** is a former Pombaline mansion with tiles from the second half of the 18th c., an inscription of **1765** and a black-and-white pebble pavement ([e-cultura](http://www.e-cultura.pt/artigo/19375)).

**Model it:** a walled village has one gate and one spine lane with 10 short branch lanes, using low 2-3 storey houses from the Alfama kit. The church tower grows out of a wall tower. Use the castle kit from `castles.md` and `iberian.md`.

**Play it:** a single gate is a chokepoint, so the walls themselves become the entry: climb over, or use a drain or postern (design). The bell tower on the wall is a lookout to take or avoid. The courtyards are pockets of loot and witnesses.

## 12. The miradouros (lookout terraces)

**What and why:** terraces on the hill edges with railings, benches and a view. In the game they are exposed, lit balconies over the roofs below.

**Features**
- **São Pedro de Alcântara:** under João V a **20 m** high wall was built here for a planned reservoir; after 1755 the site became a dump for dead animals. In 1830 the Guarda Real da Polícia used it as a vegetable garden. The public garden of **1864** is on **two terraces, 0.6 ha**, with a pond and a tile map at the balustrade. The Glória funicular climbs to it ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miradouro_de_S%C3%A3o_Pedro_de_Alc%C3%A2ntara)).
- **Santa Luzia:** built over remains of the Moorish walls (see `iberian.md` 2.1). It looks over Alfama to the river. Its south wall carries two **modern** tile panels by António Quaresma from Viúva Lamego: the pre-1755 Praça do Comércio and the Christians attacking the castle ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miradouro_de_Santa_Luzia)). The pergola's date is (not found). Size: (not found).
- **Portas do Sol:** named after the Porta do Sol of the Moorish wall, ruined in 1755. It is a balcony-like space looking east over Alfama and São Vicente. The São Vicente statue is from 1949, later ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miradouro_das_Portas_do_Sol)).
- **Santa Catarina (Adamastor):** a public garden of **1877**, made after the council refused a returned emigrant's plan for a big building there. The Adamastor statue is from 1927, later. pt.wiki gives an area of "8 hectares", which seems too big for what it calls a small garden (doubtful) ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miradouro_de_Santa_Catarina)).
- **Senhora do Monte:** the forecourt of a hermitage traditionally founded in 1147, at the highest point of Graça, looking over the Mouraria to the castle. It was remodelled twice in the 20th c. ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miradouro_da_Senhora_do_Monte)).

**Model it:** a terrace is a flat slab on a tall retaining wall, with a balustrade, benches, a tree or two and one or two lamps. The view is a skybox plus far LOD rooftops. A pergola is a beam grid with a vine card.

**Play it:** exposed and lit, with long sightlines over the roofs, a guard posted here sees half a district. They are also orientation beacons for the player. The retaining walls drop straight onto the roofs below: a fast, risky descent.

## 13. Water: chafarizes and water-carriers

**What and why:** public fountains were the only water supply for most houses, and water-carriers organised in companies crowded round them. They give hubs of life and noise.

**Features**
- The Águas Livres aqueduct was complete by **1748**, bringing about **1,300 m³** a day. It entered Lisbon from the west at an elevation of **95 m**, which allowed a whole network of gravity-fed fountains. The Mãe d'Água reservoir was only finished in **1834**. Two main galleries, Loreto and Esperança, leave its Casa do Registo ([pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduto_das_%C3%81guas_Livres)). The Loreto gallery fed the Carmo and Loreto fountains ([APS blog](https://aps-ruasdelisboacomhistria.blogspot.com/2014/05/largo-do-carmo-iv.html)).
- **Chafariz do Loreto** (Neptune, by Machado de Castro; stood from about 1780 to 1858-59): 4 spouts, 6 companies, **198 carriers** (date of the count not stated) ([Lisboa de Antigamente](https://lisboadeantigamente.blogspot.com/2021/04/chafariz-do-loreto-de-neptuno-ou-dos.html)). For Carmo and El-Rei see sections 7 and 9.
- In the heat the spouts ran thin, and the carriers waited **sitting on their barrels** and quarrelled ([APS blog](https://aps-ruasdelisboacomhistria.blogspot.com/2014/05/largo-do-carmo-iv.html)). Water was carried to houses in barrels on the shoulder, with street cries ([Benis 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/dh6z8vyqp9b)).

**Model it:** a fountain is one mesh: a back wall or baldachin, spouts and a trough. Add barrel props (carrier barrel, crate) and a wet-floor decal.

**Play it:** fountains are loud spots where footsteps are masked. By day the barrel queues are crowds to blend into. A fountain square is also a hub: the watch posts guards at several (section 15).

## 14. Night: street lighting, 1780-1900

**What and why:** the history of Lisbon's lamps gives the night its light rules.

**Features**
- **Before 1780:** in 1383 residents of streets on the watchmen's route had to keep candles lit all night ([GEO, Lisbon](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/a-iluminacao-noturna-de-lisboa-da-idade-media-a-atualidade)). Until public lighting arrived, the only lights were the small lamps in **street niches of saints and wayside crosses** ([Cardoso, quoting Cruz 1840](https://exa.ai/library/publication/6l2kk5g4dcm)).
- **17 December 1780:** the police intendant Pina Manique lit the main streets with **774 olive-oil lamps**, in Rossio, Rua Augusta, the riverside streets and squares. **A crew of 100 men** lit them **only on the 17 darkest nights of the month**. Residents paid in kind, a measure (*quartilho*) of cheap oil every four weeks ([GEO](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/a-iluminacao-noturna-de-lisboa-da-idade-media-a-atualidade); [Cardoso](https://exa.ai/library/publication/6l2kk5g4dcm)). Every master tinsmith had to make six lamps free. The lamps were shot at by those who profited from the dark. Oil prices and unpaid costs ended the service in **1792**, when the 770 lamps were put out and people went back to carrying torches ([Cardoso](https://exa.ai/library/publication/6l2kk5g4dcm)).
- **1801-1802:** relit together with the founding of the Guarda Real. A traveller, writing in January 1802, describes lamps hung **high at street corners on a curved iron arm** reaching over the street. Each held **two lights** under very clear glass, bright enough to read by (Ruders and Cardoso, in [GEO](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/a-iluminacao-noturna-de-lisboa-da-idade-media-a-atualidade); [Cardoso](https://exa.ai/library/publication/6l2kk5g4dcm)). By **1834** there were **2,328 lanterns** (GEO).
- **Gas from 30 July 1848**, first in Chiado ([FCSH](https://maislisboa.fcsh.unl.pt/luz-na-noite-lisboeta/)), run by the Companhia Lisbonense de Iluminação a Gás ([GEO](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/a-iluminacao-noturna-de-lisboa-da-idade-media-a-atualidade)). Lamps had a rectangular-based frame and were **lit by hand by lamplighters (*vaga-lumes*)**. Incandescent gas mantles came from 1889. Electric arc lamps of 8-10 m (Avenida da Liberdade) are later, and general replacement by electricity began in 1919 ([EDP Foundation](https://www.colecoesfundacaoedp.edp.pt/Nyron/Library/catalog/winlibimg.aspx?doc=187198&img=178883&skey=FA075D6CCF7247B1BFD1846E6E53BA2C)). Lisbon had 442 private gas customers by 1850 ([Matos 2009](https://exa.ai/library/publication/th93k7sgzzq)).

**Model it:** use one corner-bracket lantern mesh (curved iron arm plus a glass box) at each street corner, high on the wall. It carries an OmniLight3D for one or two lamps and a vertex-painted warm pool below. Gas posts (later) are a post variant. Put saint niches in the lanes, each with one faint flicker light.

**Play it**
- **Light depends on the moon.** In 1780-92 the lamps burned only on the 17 darkest nights, so on a moonlit night the street lamps are out and the moon lights the streets instead. That gives two lighting states for the mission.
- Corner lamps light the crossings and leave the middle of each block darker: cross the street mid-block.
- Lamps were shot out by criminals, so the bow can shoot them out too, and the watch reacts (design).
- Niche lamps are tiny light sources deep in the lanes, which a thief can snuff.

## 15. The night watch: the Guarda Real da Polícia (1801-1834)

**What and why:** Lisbon's first military police corps, foot and horse, raised against night crime. It is the template for the game's watch.

**Features**
- Created by decree of **10 December 1801**, modelled on the Paris watch (the *Guet*) ([arqnet](https://www.arqnet.pt/exercito/policia.html)). It answered to the general of the province and to the Intendant of Police, with daily reports on the day and night before ([Revista Militar](https://www.revistamilitar.pt/artigo/1232)). Recruits were the best soldiers, chosen from the army: robust, single and **aged 30 or under** (ibid.).
- **Strength:** **8 infantry and 4 cavalry companies**. (Conflict) **424 men** (8 commanders, 16 sergeants, 8 furriers, 32 corporals, 32 lance-corporals, **8 drummers**, 320 privates) ([Army Historical Archive](https://ahm-exercito.defesa.gov.pt/details?id=87955)), or 642 of all ranks ([arqnet](https://www.arqnet.pt/exercito/policia.html)). There were 160 more men in 1802. Reorganised in 1805 into 10 companies, split into **sections and squads (*esquadras*)**, with the suburbs added. Raised to **1,300** in 1810 ([AHM](https://ahm-exercito.defesa.gov.pt/details?id=87955); [arqnet](https://www.arqnet.pt/exercito/policia.html)). First commander: the émigré Count of Novion (1801-08). The corps lasted to 1834 (arqnet).
- **Uniform:** a short **blue** coat buttoned all the way down, with **red** facings, collar and cuffs, yellow buttons and 8 bars of yellow braid. Coarse twill breeches in winter and white in summer. A **black waxed-leather cap with the company number**, and a blue cloak ([AHM](https://ahm-exercito.defesa.gov.pt/details?id=87955)).
- **Barracks and posts in 1805** ([arqnet](https://www.arqnet.pt/exercito/policia.html)): the 3rd company at Loios, posted at Praça da Figueira, Mouraria and others. The 4th at Loios, posted at Chafariz de Dentro and the quays. The **7th at Largo do Carmo**. The **8th at Largo da Graça**, posted at Senhora do Monte among others. The 9th at Boa-Hora do Chiado, posted at the Cais das Colunas, São Carlos and Chagas. The four cavalry companies had their own quarters. Its later headquarters were the Carmo convent from 1810 (section 7).

**Model it:** a guard model in blue with red facings and a black leather cap with a number; a cloak for the night variant. Drummers exist (8 in 1801), so use the drum as an alarm. Use a guardhouse module (a door, a lantern, a bench).

**Play it:** fixed posts at fountains, squares, quays and lookouts, plus roving squads by district, are exactly the game's patrol-and-post layering. The corps' drummers make a natural alarm sound. Cavalry patrols the wide Baixa streets but cannot enter the lanes.

## 16. Street furniture and street life

**What and why:** the small things that fill a street at night.

**Features**
- ***Calçada portuguesa*:** first laid **1840-46** in the castle courtyards, a black-and-white zigzag, under the engineer Eusébio Pinheiro Furtado. In **1848** he paved Rossio with waves, and from then it spread through Lisbon ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_pavement)). The stones are bedded on gravel over compacted clay. Patterns include the classic 45° diagonals, linear, circular, fans and peacock tails (ibid.). It is slippery when wet (ibid.).
- **Kiosks:** approved in Nov 1867 and first installed in **1869** in Rossio (the "Elegante", nicknamed "Boia"). They sold water, *capilé* syrup, orgeat, wine by the glass, coffee, newspapers, tobacco and lottery tickets ([D de Delta](https://dddelta.com/quiosques-de-refrescos/)). Lisbon had **34**, **six of them in Rossio**. They were built by the J. Lino joinery ([Olisipo 1960 via Paixão por Lisboa](https://paixaoporlisboa.blogs.sapo.pt/quiosques-de-lisboa-51286)). The 1870s regulations standardised their size and form ([D de Delta](https://dddelta.com/quiosques-de-refrescos/)).
- **Taverns and *casas de pasto*:** a tavern sold wine, olive oil and a little food "by the small measure" (Bluteau). They were mostly male: card games, gambling and fights, and rules from 1592 against gambling and prostitution, renewed in 1797. After 1755 tents and taverns spread along the waterfront ([GEO, O Taberneiro](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/o-taberneiro)). A *casa de pasto* was an eating house at fixed prices, open all day ([Gomes 2018](https://exa.ai/library/publication/pph57t192q0); [pt.wiki](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_pasto)). The 1899 trade directory shows a jump in eating houses and inns (75 listed in one of these categories; the source's wording is ambiguous). The city grew from about **240,000 to over 350,000** people by 1900 ([Gomes 2018](https://exa.ai/library/publication/pph57t192q0)).
- **Shop signs:** painted signboards (*tabuletas*) were common enough in the Baixa that a writer could count seven on one short street ([GEO, O Taberneiro](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/o-taberneiro)). Tiled advertising cartouches spread from the 1860s (section 5).

**Model it:** use a pavement trim texture (diagonal or waves) for the squares and plain setts in the lanes. The kiosk is a later, octagonal hero prop. A tavern is a ground-floor shop module with a counter, barrels and a bench, plus a signboard decal.

**Play it:** wet *calçada* is slick and noisy, while earth lanes are quiet. A lit tavern door is a pool of light and noise with drunks spilling out. Kiosks are cover in open squares.

## 17. The *elevadores* (later, borrowable)

**What and why:** funiculars and a lift built 1884-1902 to climb the hills. They are borrowable late-period landmarks for a city on a rock.

**Features**
- **Lavra** (opened 19 Apr 1884): **188 m**, an average grade of **22.9%**, 900 mm gauge, first water-balance then steam ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevador_do_Lavra)).
- **Glória** (24 Oct 1885): **275 m** at **17.7%**, water-balance, then steam from 1886 with a Riggenbach rack for braking; electric from 1915 ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevador_da_Gl%C3%B3ria)).
- **Bica** (28 Jun 1892): **245 m** at **11.8%**, steam-driven; the lower station hides behind a street front ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevador_da_Bica)).
- **Santa Justa:** a **45 m** iron tower; the bridge opened 31 Aug 1901 and the car in 1902 ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Justa_Lift)).
- All four are by Raoul Mesnier du Ponsard (ibid.).

**Model it:** two counterweighted cars on a sloped track with a cable slot, a top machine house and a bottom station hidden behind a facade.

**Play it:** the track is a straight, exposed climb between districts. The cars are moving cover that can also crush. The machine house is a sabotage target that stops the line.

**Images:** [Lavra, 1909](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Elevador_do_Lavra_1909.jpg) · [Bica](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ascensor_da_Bica_01.JPG) · [Glória](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lisbon_Elevador_da_Gl%C3%B3ria_BW_2018-10-03_12-43-43.jpg) · [Santa Justa](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:062_Elevador_de_Santa_Justa_%2848821257101%29.jpg)

## 18. Stealth affordances, gathered

- **Roofs:** level block-wide plateaus of canal tile, crossed every 2-6 buildings by **fire walls about 0.5 m thick rising above the roof** (sections 1, 4). Mansards are steeper and need nailed tiles (4d). Dormers in a 1:2 or 1:3 rhythm are the attic entries. Stair skylights are drops into the stairwell.
- **Light wells (*saguões*):** a narrow slot (5-6 palmos in Maia's scheme) that windows open onto but no doors, foul underfoot (4c).
- **Underground:** the *cloaca* under the main streets (10 x 14 palmos, section 1); cellars with grilled vents (4a); the Patriarcal reservoir (section 8).
- **Arcades:** around the great square (section 2).
- **Fountains:** noise-masking hubs and guard posts (sections 13, 15).
- **Lighting:** corner lamps lit on the dark nights; moonlight on the bright nights; saint niches in the lanes (section 14).
- **Exposed spaces:** miradouros and squares, lit and watched (sections 2, 12).
- **Interiors:** two doors per flat, windowless interior rooms, glazed fanlights, a stone-then-timber stair (4c); painted tile servants on the landings (section 6).

## The Pombaline house: a kit specification

Sourced values carry their section reference. Values marked (design) are mine. The scale is metres.

**The facade module.** Openings equal piers (4b). The derived proportion that fits the Rua da Assunção corner building is an opening of **1.35 m**, a pier of **1.35 m** and end piers of **1.6 m**. Check: 4 x 1.35 + 3 x 1.35 + 2 x 1.6 = 12.65 m against the measured 12.70 m, and 5 openings + 4 piers + 2 ends = 15.35 m against 15.60 m. So: **bay pitch 2.70 m**, end-pier module 1.6 m (derived). A building is 3-6 bays wide (4b).

**Storeys** (sourced unless marked):

| Level | Height | Openings |
|---|---|---|
| Ground (shop) | 4.0 m (3.7 m in the 16-palmo rule; 4b) | Shop doors and the stair entrance door; arched or straight heads by street (4b) |
| Sobreloja (slopes only) | Takes up the street slope (4b) | Small windows inside the shop-door frame (design) |
| 1st, noble floor | 3.7 m (4b) | *Sacadas* on type A/B fronts; *peito* windows on type C and *travessas* (4b) |
| 2nd | 3.4 m (design; exact height not found) | *Peito* windows |
| 3rd | 3.1 m (design) | *Peito* windows; type A has a keystone arch into the cornice |
| Attic | ≥ 2.5 m clear (4b) | Dormers 1 per 2-3 bays, or a mansard with a window in every bay (4d) |

**Opening sizes (design; measured sizes not found)**
- *Peito* window: 1.35 x 2.2 m, sill at 0.9 m (the 0.90 m dado height of section 6 fixes the sill line).
- *Sacada* door: 1.35 x 2.9 m.
- Shop door: 1.35-1.6 x 3.0 m. The wider arched variant is 1.8 m, for workshops and coach houses.
- Dormer: 1.0 x 1.4 m sash, with a three-pitch roof.

**Frames and balconies**
- Lioz stone frames 0.2 m wide and proud by 3 cm (design; compare the Porto report). Type A has shaped heads and consoles; types B and C have plain rectangles.
- *Sacada* slab: 0.15 m thick projecting 0.45 m (design), with a cast-iron baluster rail 0.95 m high (design; the material is from 4b).
- Window frames white; street door dark (4b).

**Walls**
- Facade 0.9 m at the ground floor tapering to 0.6 m at the top. Model it as 0.9 / 0.75 / 0.6 steps hidden in the reveal depth (design from 4a).
- Party/fire wall 0.5 m (4a). It rises **0.6 m above the roof surface** with a tile-capped top (design; height not found).
- *Frontal* 0.2 m; partition 0.1 m (4a).

**Stair**
- On the building's axis; at the side for corner and narrow buildings (4c).
- Two flights per storey with a half-landing: stone to the first landing, timber above, no well, a masonry parapet with a timber cap (4c).
- Flight width 1.0 m (design). A skylight box 1.2 x 1.2 m over the stair rises 0.6 m above the ridge line (design).

**Light well**
- At the back between the two rows of a block: 1.2 m wide in the Pombaline scheme (derived from 5-6 palmos), or a 3 x 4 m court in later or larger buildings (design).
- White walls unless tiled (4b). Kitchen and service windows only, no doors (4c).

**A flat's plan** (half a floor, mirrored on the other side of the stair; 4c)
- Front strip, 2-3 bays: reception room (*sala*), with the *sacada* on the noble floor; a second room; a corner room.
- Middle: windowless alcoves and bedrooms lit through glazed fanlights.
- Back: kitchen with a cut-stone hearth on a brick arch and a tall flue against the party wall, a stone slop sink to a clay downpipe, water jars, and the service door from the landing.
- No corridor (Pombaline) or a short corridor (neoclassical); a long corridor in the gaioleiro type (4e).
- Rooms are square: 2.7 m (one bay) or 5.4 m (two bays) wide by about 4.5 m deep (design).
- Ceilings of lapped boards; pine plank floors (4a).

**Roof**
- Two-pitch canal tile at 27° (design; pitch not found). Corner buildings four-pitch (4d).
- Mansard on squares and main streets: lower slope 65°, upper slope 25° (design).
- Paired-brick cornice with mortared eave tiles (4d). Rectangular chimneys with a tile cap (4c).

**Azulejo cladding options** (section 5)
1. None: limewashed render (1758-1830s).
2. 1840s: blue faux-stone shop floor, a blue pattern on the upper floors, full-tile frames and a double frieze.
3. 1860s-80s: polychrome stencilled pattern from top to bottom, with narrow contrasting frames and a frieze.
4. A tile cartouche signboard over a shop.
5. Interior: a 0.9 m pattern dado in the hall and on the stair, and a kitchen tiled to the ceiling (neoclassical).

All use a 14 cm tile, 4x4 modules on a 128 px texture, and trim strips for the frames.

**Variants**
1. **Baixa block corner:** type A, 4-5 bays on each face, a four-pitch or mansard roof, shop doors on both streets, the stair at the side, a corner quoin strip.
2. **Mid-block building:** type B or C, 3-4 bays, two-pitch roof with dormers at 1:2, fire walls each side, stair on the axis.
3. **Narrow hill-street house** (Bairro Alto or Alfama): 2-3 bays, 3-4 storeys, walls 0.5-0.6 m (section 9), side stair, asymmetric bays, a *sobreloja* stepping with the slope, no ornament, canal tile.
4. **Building with a ground-floor shop:** a vaulted stone shop (groin-vault module per bay), a counter, a door to the stair hall through the two stone arches, a cellar hatch with a grilled vent to the street, and a tile cartouche sign.
5. **Vila operária row** (section 10): an inner street with two rows. One side has 2 storeys plus cellar under a continuous roof; the other has 3 storeys with balconies on cast-iron pillars. Two flats per floor, an arched entrance with a tile name-frieze, and gates at both ends.

**Props for the rooms** (sourced where marked)
- **Hall and stair:** two stone arches, a tile dado, a timber-capped parapet, a lantern; an invitation figure in grand houses (sections 4c, 6).
- **Reception room:** *conversadeira* window seats (sourced), interior shutters with *postigos* (sourced), a chair row, a table, a mirror, a wall sconce (design).
- **Bedroom and alcove:** bed, chest, chamber pot, devotional image (design); fanlight glow from the next room (sourced).
- **Kitchen:** stone hearth with pots, slop sink and downpipe, water jars (all sourced); bench, table, hanging herbs (design).
- **Attic:** low sloped room, dormer, straw mattresses, a servant's trunk (design).
- **Shop:** counter, shelves, scales, a strongbox for the goldsmith, clocks for the watchmaker, bolts of cloth, saddles and straps in the Correeiros (trades sourced in section 1; props design).
- **Street:** corner lamp, saint niche, fountain with barrels, kiosk (later), signboard, *calçada*, laundry lines (sections 13-16).
