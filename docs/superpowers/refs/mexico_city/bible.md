# Build the sinking city on real stone

A small team can make an alternate 1920s Mexico City feel unmistakably real by building on four free, period-correct pillars and inventing only a few rules on top. The first pillar is the photographic record of the 1880–1930 city by Guillermo Kahlo, C.B. Waite, W.H. Jackson and the Casasola studio. The second is a public-domain image bank: Posada and Manilla's broadsides, Julio Ruelas's Symbolist etchings and colonial devotional painting. The third is Luis González Obregón's *Las calles de México* (1922), which pins dozens of ghost and crime legends to named downtown streets in the game's own decade. The fourth is the documented politics of the 1920s: the Calles Law and its silent churches, Obregón's murder by a sketch artist, a foreign company that owned the city's light. The look rests on three materials, oxblood **tezontle** stone, grey **chiluca** trim and lime wash, with Talavera tile and hand-painted pulquería fronts over them. CC0 libraries cover the generic surfaces, but every Mexico-specific surface has to be painted. Licensing limits everything. Commit only CC0 or self-made textures. Treat work by anyone who died in 1952 or later as reference only. Invent the Aztec gods rather than copying INAH-held sculpture, because commercial reproduction of Mexican monuments needs an INAH permit and INAH has gone after commercial users. Grim Fandango, Deadlock and Dishonored all used one method: study one real city hard, then bend it with a few large, consistent lies. This bible proposes five such lies and ends with a ranked list of what to collect and make first.

How to read the tables: **Status** is the licence position for a public repo, using the ladder in the first section. **Period** flags anything that did not exist, or was not visible, before 1930. Wikimedia Commons categories are licensed file by file, so every Commons link is *reference only* unless the individual file is tagged CC0 or public domain.

## Three licence filters decide what can enter the public repo

Every resource below passes through three filters before any of it can be committed.

**The repo rule.** Poly Haven ([licence](https://polyhaven.com/license)) and ambientCG ([licence](https://ambientcg.com/license)) are CC0 and safe to commit. textures.com must stay out of git ([licence](https://www.textures.com/license)). Many Commons files are CC BY-SA, which brings attribution and share-alike duties if their pixels are baked into a committed texture.

**Mexican copyright.** Under the Commons analysis of Mexican law, **authors who died before 1952 are public domain in Mexico**. The 1982 extension to life+50 did not apply retroactively, and the current term is life+100. Photographs also get life+100. **Anonymous works stay protected "until the author or the owner of the rights are identified"** (Art. 153) ([Commons: Copyright rules/Mexico](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/Mexico)). That last rule matters more than it looks. Anonymous ex-votos, retablos and pulquería murals are the most "Mexican" images there are, yet they sit in a grey zone, so paint new ones rather than copying old ones. In the US, works published before 1931 are in the public domain as of 1 January 2026. That is a general rule and was not re-verified in this research.

**INAH monument law.** Commercial reproduction of archaeological, historical or artistic monuments needs authorisation from the competent institute under Art. 17 of the federal monuments law, applied for on form INAH-01-004. The tests are that the heritage is not harmed and its image is not distorted, and fees are set by Art. 288-D of the Ley Federal de Derechos ([INAH trámite form](https://tramites.inah.gob.mx/Descargables/INAH_01_004_A_y_B_REPRODUCCION_MONUMENTOS.pdf)). This is enforced. INAH and the Secretaría de Cultura took action against MrBeast in 2025 ([Expansión](https://expansion.mx/tendencias/2025/05/15/inah-demanda-mrbeast-que-paso)) and threatened Max over its use of Chapultepec Castle in 2024 ([El Imparcial](https://www.elimparcial.com/espectaculos/2024/06/12/inah-podria-demandar-a-max-por-uso-indebido-del-castillo-de-chapultepec/)). The Chapultepec case shows that historic *buildings* are covered as well as Aztec sculpture. **No legal opinion was found on whether a fictional video-game depiction counts as "commercial reproduction"**, so get legal review before release. A 2022 law protecting Indigenous and Afro-Mexican cultural heritage may require community consent for traditional designs such as masks; it was not verified. The practical answer is the alternate universe itself: invent gods inspired by Coatlicue rather than replicas of her, and remix real buildings rather than modelling registered monuments one-to-one.

## Four architectural layers stacked on a sinking lake bed

The city is four layers on top of one another, and the game should make that literal. At the bottom is **Tenochtitlan**: an island city of 200,000–400,000 people on a compass-aligned grid laced with canals. Three causeways joined it to the shore, cut by bridges that could be pulled away, and the ceremonial precinct was a walled square 500 m a side ([Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)). The Spanish kept the main roads. The colonial traza covered 180 ha in 100 blocks with eight principal canals ([Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)). Then they drained the lake. Tunnelling by forced Indigenous labour began after the floods of 1604 and 1607. In 1629 a flood came that stayed for years: most people fled, buildings collapsed, and the crown proposed moving the capital ([Wikipedia: Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe)). The British contractor Weetman Pearson finished the job for Díaz with five steam dredges, and the Gran Canal opened in 1900 as a symbol of modernity. It also carried sewage and tannery waste ([Wikipedia: Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe)). The sources disagree on the cost, 13 or 16 million pesos. The drained clay is still sinking. **Parts of the city drop about 2 cm a month and have fallen more than 12 m in under a century** ([AP via 10News](https://www.10news.com/news/mexico-city-is-sinking-so-quickly-it-can-be-seen-from-space)), and **Bellas Artes has sunk about 4 m since 1904** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes)). That sinking is the city's most distinctive real-world uncanny fact. Leaning churches and doorways reached by steps going *down* need no invention at all.

The **colonial layer** is a grid of blood-red, porous tezontle walls framed in grey chiluca. The stone was chosen partly because it is light enough to slow sinking into the lakebed ([Wikipedia: Tezontle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezontle)). Palaces were built as fortresses. The Casa de los Condes de Santiago de Calimaya has a portal resting on clawed feet, **cannon-shaped gargoyles that "could be fired, but only with permission of city authorities"**, and an Aztec feathered-serpent head set into its corner as a cornerstone ([Wikipedia: Museum of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Mexico_City)). Churches carry Churrigueresque façades built on the **estípite**, a pilaster shaped like an inverted pyramid ([Wikipedia: Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque)). The **Porfirian layer** laid imported skins over the sinking grid: Carrara marble, Florentine bronze, German iron halls shipped over and reassembled, and New York steel frames. Boari's Bellas Artes already put **serpent heads and Maya Chaac masks on a Beaux-Arts palace around 1904** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes)). That is the real start of "occult deco". The **1920s layer** brought a neocolonial revival, helped by a 1917–1920 tax exemption for viceregal-style building ([El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/el-tezontle-desde-la-arquitectura-virreinal-la-neocolonial/)). It also brought "Aztec Deco" with stepped setbacks, and the Estridentistas' imagined **Estridentópolis**, "absurd, vertiginous, seat of multitudinous modernity" ([es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estrident%C3%B3polis)). Estridentópolis is the most period-authentic licence there is for a stylised, other-world Mexican modernity.

The 1920s city was also full of **unfinished and stalled buildings**, and those make good gothic sets. Bellas Artes, begun in 1904, stopped in 1913 and was finished only in 1932–34 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes)). The Federal Legislative Palace stood as an abandoned Porfirian skeleton until it was turned into the Monumento a la Revolución in 1938 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumento_a_la_Revoluci%C3%B3n)). In the game's decade both should be shells.

## Tezontle, lime wash and Talavera carry the material identity

Three materials make up the street look. **Walls are faced in tezontle**, a vesicular, iron-red volcanic scoria that also comes in black. It has been called "the most representative building stone of Mexico" ([IUGS Geoheritage](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=11830)). **Openings, cornices and quoins are cut from chiluca or cantera**, a grey-white stone fine enough to carry Baroque relief ([Wikipedia: Cantera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantera_(stone))). **Lime plaster and lime wash cover everything else.** The tezontle story has a twist that matters for a 1920s game. The San Carlos Academy (1781) and Porfirian French taste hid tezontle under plaster. The post-Revolution revival put it back on show: the third storey of the Palacio Nacional was added in 1926–28 with a tezontle façade ([El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/el-tezontle-desde-la-arquitectura-virreinal-la-neocolonial/)). So in the game's decade, red stone reads as *new nationalism*, not only as old empire.

The ruin layer goes over all three. Humidity drives salts into porous masonry, so damage concentrates at the base of exterior walls as flaking and scaling ([INAH, El Correo del Restaurador](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/elcorreodelrestaurador/article/download/21217/22612/44410)). Talavera poblana supplies the ceramic. It is tin-glazed earthenware, and the 1653 guild ordinances reserved cobalt blue for the finest pieces ([LOC Law blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2023/09/talavera-ceramics-at-the-intersection-of-art-history-and-law-in-mexico/)). Its permitted palette is blue, yellow, black, green, orange and mauve. **In 1922 Enrique Ventosa and Ysauro Uriarte designed Talavera blending pre-Columbian and Art Nouveau motifs** ([Wikipedia: Talavera pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talavera_pottery)), a real "neo-prehispanic" ceramic from the game's exact decade.

Floors in Porfirian houses were unfired, unglazed, hydraulic-pressed cement tile (*mosaico de pasta*), which reached Mexico at the end of the 19th century ([Wikipedia: Cement tile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement_tile); [Posta](https://www.posta.com.mx/yucatan/mosaicos-de-pasta-tesoro-yucateco-de-arte-y-tradicion-bajo-tus-pies/vl2072390)). Its matte surface needs a different shader from glossy Talavera. On the street, two finishes mark the period. One is the **hand-painted rótulo** sign, which grew out of 19th-century sign painting ([Wikipedia: Rotulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotulo)). The other is the **Talavera street plaque**: the 1920s city government put up tile plaques giving the *old* colonial street names after most streets were renamed around 1921 ([AP via Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/world/reminders-of-centuries-past-emerge-along-mexico-city-streets)). Every street therefore has two names, which is ready-made lore for an investigation game.

Two colour claims are easy to get wrong. **"Rosa mexicano" as a named colour dates from 1949**, although the hot pink itself is pre-Hispanic ([Wikipedia: Mexican pink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_pink)). Avoid the name in-world. **Cempasúchil**, the marigold, is recorded in the Florentine Codex in Aztec feasts of the dead and is used in petal paths that guide souls ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/10/30/1050726374/why-marigolds-or-cempasuchil-are-the-iconic-flower-of-dia-de-los-muertos)). Public lighting went from gas to electric between 1896 and 1905 ([Biblat abstract](https://biblat.unam.mx/en/revista/letras-historicas/articulo/de-empresa-de-alumbrado-a-empresa-de-servicio-de-energia-electrica-la-cmgle-en-la-ciudad-de-mexico-1896-1905)). The 1920s night is therefore warm lamp pools on wet volcanic stone. No source was found on the mix of arc and incandescent lamps.

## Posada's broadsides and Ruelas's etchings form the free image bank

**The Vanegas Arroyo broadside world is the richest public-domain source the game has.** From 1882 Manuel Manilla, and later José Guadalupe Posada, made cheap sheets on green, magenta and yellow paper: skeletons, crimes, miracles, monster births and the end of the world. The calavera theme was "probably invented" by Manilla and made iconic by Posada ([Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649)). The Library of Congress holds **over a hundred Posada prints that it presents as rights-free** ([LOC Picture This](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2020/10/revisiting-rights-free-popular-graphic-art-prints/)), along with the zinc blocks he used for some of his most famous images ([LOC blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2023/10/jos-guadalupe-posadas-lively-calaveras-and-enduring-legacy/)). The Met's Open Access sheets may be reused even commercially. The broadside layout is a template that can be used as it stands: a woodcut or zinc image on top, rhyming text below, a sensational headline, coloured paper. It suits in-world wanted posters, crime sheets, miracle sheets and mock-epitaph calaveras that "kill" living people in verse. Posada's **Calavera Garbancera** (c.1910–13; another source says 1912) mocked Indigenous women who copied French fashion. The name "Catrina" came only with Rivera's 1947 mural ([El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/cultura/artes-visuales/quien-invento-la-catrina/)), so in-world she is the *Garbancera*.

**Julio Ruelas (1870–1907) is the gothic heart of the high-art register.** He was the principal illustrator of *Revista Moderna*, and his etchings show satyrs, drowned women, a bug-creature drilling into the artist's skull (*La crítica*) and a self-portrait as the damned ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_Ruelas); [Commons, 22 files, checked](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Julio_Ruelas)). His student **Saturnino Herrán** (d. 1918) left studies for *Nuestros dioses*, an unrealised mural for the Teatro Nacional (now Bellas Artes) that sets Coatlicue against Christ ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturnino_Herr%C3%A1n); [UIC](https://aaa.digital.uic.edu/s/ancientamericasappropriated/item/6308)). It is the single best period image of syncretic mysticism, and it is public domain. The colonial devotional genres supply the religious-horror vocabulary. Crowned nuns were painted at profession and again on their deathbeds, wearing paper-flower crowns ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/?p=54313)). Sixteenth-century Hell murals at Actopan and Xoxoteco were found in the 1970s under whitewash, and 18th-century churches replaced Hell with ánimas (purgatory) paintings ([UNAM](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cielo/ciyp_04cap.pdf)). Casta paintings, sets that encode a hierarchy of "purity", are on LACMA's open-access shelf of about 20,000 public-domain images ([LACMA](https://collections.lacma.org/node/220040)).

For photography, **Hugo Brehme is the best mood source but cannot be reproduced**. He died in 1954, so his work is under Mexican copyright until 2055; his pre-1931 postcards are public domain in the US only ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Brehme)). C.B. Waite (d. 1927) is clean. He photographed street scenes, Guadalupe and the Guanajuato catacomb mummies ([UC Riverside finding aid](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8zg6qnh/)). The Casasola archive, the founding collection of INAH's Fototeca Nacional, holds **325,290 glass negatives and photographs** ([INAH Alquimia](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/alquimia/article/download/3192/3082/5081)). It is searchable on the [Mediateca INAH](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/islandora_74/islandora/search/casasola), but scans carry INAH terms that were not confirmed.

## Folklore supplies a casebook already pinned to real streets

The decisive fact is timing. **Mexico City's colonial ghost and crime legends were fixed in print between 1882 and 1922**, the generation just before the game. Riva Palacio and Peza serialised *Tradiciones y leyendas mexicanas* in verse from January 1882 ([UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/tradiciones-leyendas-mexicanas-peza-riva-palacio/)). González Obregón's *Las calles de México* appeared in 1922 and was reissued in two volumes in 1927 ([TAMU record](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000680341)). A 1920s character could be reading it as a new bestseller. The full texts are on archive.org: [*Las calles de México*](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015), [*México viejo*](https://archive.org/details/mexico-viejo-luis-obregon), and a [1937 English translation, *The Streets of Mexico*](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-EGN-299). Many of the legends attach to real people and buildings, and González Obregón himself debunks some of them. That makes them ideal investigation cases, where the legend and the record disagree. Valle-Arizpe's *Leyendas mexicanas* is from 1943, so it is POST-1930 ([Lecturalia](https://www.lecturalia.com/libro/50370/leyendas-mexicanas)).

Three patterns run through the corpus and could become game rules. One is **revenant justice**: the dead punish oath-breakers and murderers. Don Juan Manuel is executed "by angels", a skeleton priest strangles his killer, a corpse warns a man who broke a vow. The second is **two names per street**, which follows from the 1921 renaming. The third is **migrants' monsters**. Most famous Mexican bogeymen are regional, and post-Revolution migration plausibly brought them into the vecindades, so each district can carry its own imported beliefs.

Grim Fandango used Aztec cosmology loosely. It made the afterlife depend on merit, which is closer to European than Aztec belief ([Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/exhuming-grim-fandangos-mexican-folklore-inspirations/)). A CDMX game should decide on purpose where it is faithful and where it is loose. Obsidian is the natural thread for a single magic school covering seeing, reflecting and cutting: it links Tezcatlipoca, Dee's mirror and Mictlán's obsidian mountain.

### Church horror, Inquisition and the Cristero decade

The Holy Office ran a formal tribunal from 1571 to 1820 and executed about 50 people. Its main targets were crypto-Jews, witchcraft, new science and homosexuality rather than Indigenous people ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Inquisition)). The burning place was beside the Alameda at San Hipólito ([HMdb](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=121057)). The present palace (1732–36) had hearing rooms, offices of "El Secreto", secret chambers and perpetual prisons ([CDMX tourism](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/palace-school-medicine/?lang=en)). In the 1920s it housed the medical school. That gives a ready level: an anatomy theatre above, sealed inquisitorial cells below.

**The 1920s supply their own gothic events in the capital.** On 14 November 1921 dynamite hidden in flowers exploded at the Guadalupe altar. It twisted a 34-kg bronze crucifix and left the tilma untouched ([Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/el-cristo-que-protegio-a-la-virgen-de-guadalupe-se-cumplen-hoy-100-anos-del-atentado)). Public worship was suspended from 31 July / 1 August 1926 ([EWTN](https://www.ewtnnews.com/world/americas/100-years-since-the-cristero-war-in-mexico-what-you-should-know)). Under the Calles Law, wearing a cassock in public cost a 500-peso fine ([Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches)), so **a priest in clerical dress is more visible to guards, not less**. Miguel Pro ran secret masses in disguise until he was shot without trial on 23 November 1927. Calles had the execution photographed as a warning, and it backfired ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Pro)). On 17 July 1928, José de León Toral, posing as a sketch artist drawing guests at La Bombilla in San Ángel, shot president-elect Obregón. A nun, Madre Conchita, was accused of inciting him ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_Le%C3%B3n_Toral)). In San Ángel itself, Zapatista troops digging for treasure c.1916–17 broke open the Carmelite crypt and exposed mummies that are still on display ([INAH](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4241)). That crypt was opened less than a decade before the game.

### Spiritism, Theosophy, Masonry and the reburied idol

**Spiritism was mainstream among the liberal elite.** *La Ilustración Espírita* was published in Mexico City from 1872, and Juan de Dios Peza, co-author of the legends, was an adherent ([Analéctica](http://portal.amelica.org/ameli/journal/251/2511186004/html/)). A national spiritist congress was held in the capital in 1906 with Madero taking part ([Revista Argos](https://revistaargos.cucsh.udg.mx/index.php/argos/article/view/5)). **Francisco I. Madero, who launched the Revolution, practised automatic writing.** His spirit guides included Benito Juárez, signing "BJ", who by Madero's account urged the 1910 revolution. Madero wrote a *Manual espírita* under the pseudonym Bhîma, and Calles reportedly attended séances ([Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/president-talked-to-ghosts-madero-spiritism/); [SPR Psi Encyclopedia](https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/francisco-i-madero/)). Theosophy's first lodge, "Aura", opened in 1906, and the Mexican Section was recognised in 1919 ([theosophy.world](https://theosophy.world/es/node/2191)). Calles was a Scottish Rite Mason ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry_in_Mexico)). Spiritism and anticlericalism were allied, so **séance-going Masons running a state that persecutes the Church is historically grounded**. The healer Niño Fidencio, whom Calles visited in 1928, adds the popular counterpart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%B1o_Fidencio)).

The best "idol that will not stay buried" story is real. The Coatlicue statue was dug up in the Zócalo in 1790. When Indigenous people came at night with candles, flowers and incense, the university reburied it, then dug it up again for Humboldt and buried it again ([UNAM Big Bang Mex](https://bigbangmex.unam.mx/en/coatlicue/antecedentes/)).

## The 1920s city ran on generals, silent bells and a foreign light company

The best single academic source is **Pablo Piccato, *City of Suspects: Crime in Mexico City, 1900–1931*** ([Duke UP](https://www.dukeupress.edu/City-of-Suspects)). It reads the city as "contested territory" where the urban poor negotiated public space with the police and settled wrongs outside the courts ([Aguirre review](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~caguirre/Aguirre_Piccato.pdf)). Power in the decade was a three-way knot. The victorious Sonoran generals were on one side. The Church, driven underground, was on another. The third was organised labour bought by the state: Morones's CROM, run by an inner clique called the Grupo Acción ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Confederation_of_Mexican_Workers)). Around the edges sat foreign capital and the fading Porfirian elite. **The Anglo-Canadian Mexican Light and Power Company literally owned the city's light and trams** ([UB Geocrit](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/Electr-y-territorio/GomezMartinez.pdf)). For a stealth game, a foreign company that decides which streets go dark is the most useful political fact in the record.

The watch had two layers. **Gendarmes** of the Inspección General de Policía were frequently in violent conflict with residents ([Historia Mexicana](https://revistas.colmex.mx/Record/oai:oai.historiamexicana.colmex.mx:article-4682)). The older **serenos** worked 11 pm to 5 am with a lantern, an iron-tipped *chuzo* and a whistle. They called the hour and kept the neighbourhood's house keys: "Las doce en punto y sereno" ([Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/)). Whether serenos still worked alongside gendarmes in the 1920s is not established, which is a good thing for an alternate universe to decide. The decade's crime legend is the 1915 **Banda del Automóvil Gris**: fake soldiers raided rich houses and escaped in a grey car. The 1919 film about them played in 19 cinemas at once ([Morelia FF](https://www.moreliafilmfest.com/en/peliculas/el-automovil-gris)). Crime news, the *nota roja*, was a mass genre ([INAH Diario de Campo](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/7804/8661)).

Night life was stratified by class, which gives ready social-stealth levels. There were over a thousand painted pulquerías ([historicalmx](https://historicalmx.org/items/show/93)). In 1926 Diego Rivera and Edward Weston published essays and photographs on pulquerías and their names in *Mexican Folkways* ([Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-last-word-in-direct-naive-realism-diego-rivera-edward-weston-/)). **Salón México opened on 20 April 1920 with three dance floors classified by the dancers' social class** ([Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ciudad/lo-que-la-modernidad-se-llevo/)): one building, three class layers. Carpa tent theatres bred Cantinflas's *pelado* by 1930 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantinflas)). Revue star María Conesa was courted by Díaz, Villa and Zapata ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Conesa)). In July 1924 medical and preparatory students attacked bobbed-haired *pelonas* as a "national duty" ([Rubenstein](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-modern-women-and-their-enemies-mexico-city-1924/)).

Technology dated the city precisely. Electric trams ran from 1900 ([Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/mientras-tanto/ese-momento-glorioso-cuando-la-cdmx-tenia-tranvias/)), and the first radio station went on air on 8 May 1923 ([Wikipedia: Radio in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_in_Mexico)). The church bells fell silent for the sacraments in 1926–29, which suits a mood device: a city where the bells have stopped, and someone is still ringing one.

Street trades double as gameplay roles, each with its own cry, or *pregón* ([Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/351/35145982008.pdf)). Scribes, newsboys and quack talkers are information brokers. Water carriers, porters and boatmen know the routes. Organ grinders and street criers provide cover noise. Curanderas are the occult healers. For language there are three registers: underworld *caló* ([Masaryk University PDF](https://digilib.phil.muni.cz/cs/_flysystem/fedora/pdf/125851.pdf)), Frenchified elite speech and revolutionary officialese. No verified 1920s word list was found, so Piccato's prisoners' letters are the next place to look. For dress, only a few looks are sourced: the pelona (bob, red lips, short dress), the pelado (baggy trousers, rope belt) and the sereno's hat, hood and overcoat. Gendarme uniforms, charro dress and rebozos need a dedicated pass through the Casasola holdings.

## Literature, film and music: pre-1930 for the world, post-1930 for the craft

The rule is simple. **Works from before 1930 can exist inside the game; later works shape structure and tone from outside it.** In-world books for a 1920s bookish NPC come from the Porfirian layer. José María Roa Bárcena's "Lanchitas" (1877–78) is an urban ghost story about a priest transformed by supernatural events ([Cervantes Virtual](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra/relatos/)). Bernardo Couto Castillo's *Asfódelos* (1897) has Lady Death wandering the city streets ([Snuggly Books](https://snugglybooks.co.uk/asphodels)). In Amado Nervo's *El donador de almas* (1899) a soul is given as a gift ([UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/el-donador-de-almas-de-amado-nervo/)). The *Revista Moderna* circle survives into the 1920s as aging decadents, a natural in-world aesthete cult.

Rulfo, Fuentes, Garro and Moreno-Garcia are all POST-1930. They supply the five structures an investigation game needs: the chorus of unreliable dead (*Pedro Páramo*), the house that remembers (*Aura*), the town as narrator (*Los recuerdos del porvenir*), the double, and the bargain with death. Antonio Helú's detective Máximo Roldán has a name that is an anagram of *ladrón*, "thief" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Hel%C3%BA)). He is the closest Mexican precedent for a Thief-style protagonist who solves crimes while lifting the loot.

Film is all POST-1930. The 1933–34 cycle is the nearest to period-accurate visual reference. *Dos monjes* (1934) tells one shared past twice, from two unreliable viewpoints, in German-expressionist style ([Criterion](https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/7119-dos-monjes-expressionism-a-la-mexicana)). That is the model for an interrogation system where physical evidence contradicts a witness and unlocks the next layer of testimony.

For sound, the period city was **barrel organs**: German Wagner & Levien instruments from 1884, about 500 organilleros in the Porfiriato against about 50 today ([Xataka](https://www.xataka.com/magnet/organilleros-parte-tejido-cultural-ciudad-mexico-problema-que-no-hay-quien-fabrique-instrumentos)). Alongside them were típica orchestras, danzón, corridos and alabados, and from 1927 Agustín Lara's cabaret bolero ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agust%C3%ADn_Lara)). Silvestre Revueltas's dissonant street-band ostinatos (1931–40) are the best reference for a distinctly Mexican horror score ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvestre_Revueltas)). Use the archives to *listen* for period timbre, and keep the project's approved, licensed packs as the actual sound source.

## The case studies teach one method: research hard, then lie big

**Grim Fandango** set two unrelated sources against each other and gave each a single job. Folklore supplied who people are and how the cosmos works. Noir and Mayan-Revival Art Deco supplied tone, buildings and speech. Schafer described how noir and folklore "crash into each other and you'll realize that they accentuate each other" ([Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/exhuming-grim-fandangos-mexican-folklore-inspirations/)). Its architecture took a San Francisco building, 450 Sutter Street, a monument of the Mayan Revival branch of Art Deco. **A CDMX game can use the local originals instead**: Bellas Artes's Chaac masks, the Frontón, La Nacional. The game was split into four Day-of-the-Dead years in four locations, each with its own industry, and the puzzles were mapped as dependency charts in a design document from April 1996 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grim_Fandango); [VGHF](https://gamehistory.org/?p=1594)).

**Deadlock** is still a playtest, "in early development with lots of temporary art" ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1422450/Deadlock/)). Treat its look as a moving target. Its method is visible all the same:

- One real city frozen at a cut-off year (New York to 1932).
- One rupture, the Maelstrom, that makes the occult public ([Deadlock Wiki](https://deadlock.wiki/Storyline)).
- Gods embodied as civic monuments mapped onto real class geography: the Liberty-masked Archmother on the white-stone Uptown, the wicker-man Hidden King on the red-brick Downtown ([Deadlock Wiki](https://deadlock.wiki/Hidden_King)).
- Districts that, in the October 2026 update, reportedly each get their own creature family, such as sentient book stacks in a haunted university. This rests on a search summary ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/moba/deadlocks-gigantic-new-update-gives-its-city-a-spooky-new-coat-of-paint-and-debuts-6-new-heroes-including-the-one-and-only-rat-king/)).

No Valve-authored art-direction statement was found. "Valve's principles" are analysis, not a quotation.

**Dishonored** is the most transferable for a small team. Arkane made photo trips and studied mug shots and regional faces. It worked from paintings and sculpture rather than games. It wrote a whole continent while building only what the player sees ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/dishonoreds-art-director-talks-inspiration-cut-levels-5953087); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-dishonoreds-artists-created-an-oppressive-world-from-scratch/)). Each city runs on one invented economy that explains its technology, its class split and its stealth spaces: whale oil in Dunwall, silver dust and windbreakers in Karnaca ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/23269-dishonored-2-sebastien-mitton-art-director-arkane-studios-interview)). Karnaca is the nearest analogue to a Latin-American city. It mixed Cuban reference photos with research in Barcelona because Cuba was out of reach, and its history reportedly took about a year to write ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dishonored_2)). Mitton's reason for dropping a medieval Japan setting carries a warning: "we do not know this culture" ([Game Informer](https://Www.Gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2013/03/28/dishonored-almost-took-place-in-japan.aspx)). Jorge Gutiérrez rejected the "tourist version" of Mexico ([Cartoon Brew](https://www.cartoonbrew.com/?p=107509)). Together they argue for **hiring or consulting Mexican artists and writers**.

## Five big lies for an alternate Mexico City

No published source sets out a "3–5 big lies" rule. The pattern comes from comparing the case studies: one geography, one rupture, one economy, one cosmology, one twisted institution. Each lie below is anchored in a cited real fact and justified by what it gives the stealth and investigation design. Everything not named here stays as it really was in the 1920s: Calles politics, trams, cabarets, Estridentistas, muralists, tezontle, vecindades, pulquerías.

| # | The lie | Real anchor | What it gives the game | Model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The lake came back.** Pearson's Gran Canal failed in 1900, or a 1629-style flood returned and stayed | The 1629 flood lasted years and the crown proposed moving the capital ([Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe)); the city sinks unevenly ([AP](https://www.10news.com/news/mexico-city-is-sinking-so-quickly-it-can-be-seen-from-space)) | Canal streets, drowned ground floors, life moved up to azoteas (rooftop routes), garita tolls, trajineras as moving cover, districts at different tilts | Karnaca's single geographic logic |
| 2 | **Madero's spirits were real.** The Revolution was started by the dead, and since 1910 they are publicly present: they testify, and the state keeps séance registries | Madero's automatic writing, "BJ" urging revolution, the *Manual espírita*, Calles at séances ([Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/president-talked-to-ghosts-madero-spiritism/)) | Ghost witnesses in a *Dos monjes* / *Pedro Páramo* testimony system; spirit photography as a detective tool; the Kardecist, Theosophist and Masonic factions | Deadlock's Maelstrom |
| 3 | **Light is owned, and light guides the dead.** The Light Company's lamps are also wards; a street gone dark belongs to the dead | Mexican Light and Power owned the lamps and trams ([UB Geocrit](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/Electr-y-territorio/GomezMartinez.pdf)); marigold paths guide souls ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/10/30/1050726374/why-marigolds-or-cempasuchil-are-the-iconic-flower-of-dia-de-los-muertos)); the New Fire put out every fire every 52 years ([World History](https://worldhistory.org/article/866)) | **The stealth core.** Dousing a lamp hides you from the living but opens the street to spirits and Tzitzimimeh. The New Fire night is the climax. Obsidian is the magic school | Dunwall's whale oil |
| 4 | **Mictlán is the basement.** The underground is stratified: drains, crypts, colonial cellars, the six Templo Mayor shells, then Mictlán's nine trials | Templo Mayor rebuilt six times ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templo_Mayor)); foundations hit idols at Casa Boker and a carved sacrum at the Gran Canal ([es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_Boker)); Coatlicue reburied ([UNAM](https://bigbangmex.unam.mx/en/coatlicue/antecedentes/)) | A third play layer under the same footprint; La Nacional as a deco "seventh shell"; idols that will not stay buried | *Coco*'s history by height, turned downward; Grim's journey of the soul |
| 5 | **The Holy Office never closed. The anticlerical state inherited it.** "El Secreto", in the Santo Domingo palace, registers ghosts, confiscates relics, reburies idols and issues ward permits | Tribunal 1571–1820 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Inquisition)); Calimaya's gargoyles fired "with permission of city authorities" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Mexico_City)); the Calles Law ([Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches)) | A morally grey antagonist bureaucracy that sits between the Silent Church and the Masonic state; permits and papers as an investigation economy | Grim's Department of Death |

Lies 3 and 4 do the most work for gameplay, and lie 3 should be locked first. A stealth game's lighting has to have a reason, and whale oil proved that the resource which explains the light also explains the economy and the class map. Candidates the case-study notes considered and set aside for the main slot were copal, pulque, lake silt and Hidalgo silver. Each could return as a district industry.

**Deliberate anachronisms, to label in the lore bible.** Alebrijes (1936), the public Santa Muerte cult (2001), the Isla de las Muñecas, lucha libre (1930s), La Planchada and the name "rosa mexicano" (1949) can each be moved earlier on purpose, as Deadlock allows anachronistic heroes, but never by accident.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Most of the "gothic" a Mexico City game needs is not invention but retrieval. Here is a capital that is physically sinking. Its Inquisition palace housed a medical school. Its churches went silent in 1926. Its president took advice from Juárez's ghost. A foreign company owned its light, and its authorities kept reburying a goddess who drew night worshippers. Those facts are stranger than most invented settings, and nearly all are documented in sources a small team can read for free. The invention budget should therefore be spent narrowly: on the five lies, mainly on the light rule that drives stealth, and on painting the five Mexico-specific surfaces that no CC0 library will ever supply.

The second shift is legal and cultural, not artistic. The binding risks for a public-repo game are not about style. They are Mexico's life+100 copyright on anonymous and post-1952 work, and INAH's monument law, which has been applied to commercial media. Both point to the same design answer that Grim Fandango and Deadlock reached for creative reasons: recognisable but remixed, real in its materials and fictional in its gods. A team that writes its licence policy and its lies before modelling a single wall will end up with a city that is both safer to ship and more clearly another universe.
