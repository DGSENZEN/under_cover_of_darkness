## 1. Politics and power: the post-Revolution state, Church, unions, assassinations

### Takeaway
The 1920s city was run by victorious Sonoran generals (Obregón, then Calles) who ruled through patronage, a captive labour federation (Morones's CROM) and a cultural-education crusade (Vasconcelos's SEP), while fighting the Catholic Church outright from 1926. The decade's defining capital-city event was the assassination of president-elect Obregón at La Bombilla (San Ángel) on 17 July 1928 by a Catholic sketch artist, an act tied to a nun's circle of devotees.

### Cited Findings
- **Obregón's assassination.** On 17 July 1928, two weeks after his re-election, Obregón was shot at La Bombilla restaurant in San Ángel by José de León Toral, a sketch artist who drew the guests, then showed Obregón a portrait sketch and fired a .32 Star pistol; six shots hit the face and torso — [Wikipedia: José de León Toral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_Le%C3%B3n_Toral); [El Universal: crónica del juicio y fusilamiento](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/cultura/cronica-del-juicio-y-fusilamiento-de-leon-de-toral-asesino-de-obregon/). **Game use:** the "artist who sketches the room before the kill" is a perfect investigation hook — a sketchbook as evidence; portrait-drawing as a social-stealth disguise.
- Toral was allegedly incited by the nun Concepción Acevedo de la Llata, "Madre Conchita"; Toral insisted the plan was his alone. She got 20 years on the Islas Marías penal colony; Toral was shot by firing squad on 9 Feb 1929 inside Lecumberri — [Wikipedia: Toral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_Le%C3%B3n_Toral); [El Universal: la monja que colgó el hábito](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/la-monja-que-colgo-el-habito-despues-de-la-muerte-de-obregon/); [Excélsior: el fusilamiento que cerró una era](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/fusilamiento-jose-leon-toral-1929); a popular ballad exists: [El Corrido de Toral (UT Austin)](https://laits.utexas.edu/jaime/cwp5/crg/english/toral). **Game use:** a clandestine convent-in-a-private-house led by a charismatic abbess; penal-island exile as a threat NPCs fear.
- **Calles Law and the Cristero War.** In June 1926 President Plutarco Elías Calles signed the reform of the Penal Code ("Calles Law"): fines (500 pesos) for wearing clerical garb in public, up to five years' prison for a priest criticising the government; monastic orders banned and convents dissolved — [Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches); [Guadalajara Reporter](https://theguadalajarareporter.net/index.php/featured/64875-the-loaded-gun-calles-fired-the-cristero-war-at-100-when-the-state-went-after-the-church). (Note: both are Catholic-leaning or popular sources; facts match the standard account.)
- The bishops suspended all public worship from 31 July / 1 August 1926 — churches stayed open but without sacraments; the Cristero War (1926-29) killed roughly 90,000 people — [Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches); [EWTN News](https://www.ewtnnews.com/world/americas/100-years-since-the-cristero-war-in-mexico-what-you-should-know). **Game use:** empty, silent churches with bare tabernacles; secret house-Masses; priests in civilian disguise (a priest wearing a cassock is breaking the law — a visible tell for guards); smuggled sacraments as contraband.
- **CROM and Morones.** Luis N. Morones (1890-1964), "the most important union leader of the 1920s", headed the Regional Confederation of Mexican Workers (CROM) and was Calles's Secretary of Industry/Economy 1924-28; the CROM was run by an inner clique, the *Grupo Acción*, which backed the government; Morones was known for unseemly displays of wealth — [Wikipedia: Luis N. Morones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_N._Morones); [Wikipedia: CROM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Confederation_of_Mexican_Workers). Contemporary leftist critiques: [Diego Rivera, "The Mexican Labor Movement", Daily Worker 1928 (reprint)](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2025/06/12/the-mexican-labor-movement-by-diego-rivera-from-the-daily-worker-vol-5-no-14-january-18-1928/). **Game use:** a union boss faction that is half labour movement, half protection racket, with a secret inner circle.
- **Vasconcelos and the cultural mission.** The SEP was created in 1921 under José Vasconcelos with three departments (Schools; Libraries and Archives; Fine Arts); the Misiones Culturales (travelling teacher missions) were founded October 1923; he commissioned Rivera, Orozco and Siqueiros (Rivera at San Ildefonso, 1921) and printed cheap "green books" of Greek and other classics for new readers — [Redalyc (Vasconcelos/SEP)](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/600/60048431005.pdf); [Revista de la Universidad PDF](https://mia.revistadelauniversidad.mx/downloads/94bd64b4-0f20-415e-9fdd-2a954f9e2b1c/rum-OCT.2012-art%c3%adculos-la-reforma-inaplazable.pdf); [México Desconocido on Orozco](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/revolucion-social-o-reconstruccion-jose-clemente-orozco.html). **Game use:** "missionary" teachers and muralists as a faction; murals as in-world storytelling surfaces (and places to hide messages); green books as collectible readables.
- **Foreign capital.** The Anglo-Canadian Mexican Light and Power Company ran Necaxa hydro power and, via its Tramways subsidiary, the city's electric trams and lighting — [Universitat de Barcelona (Gómez Martínez)](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/Electr-y-territorio/GomezMartinez.pdf); [Sopitas: cuando la CDMX tenía tranvías](https://www.sopitas.com/mientras-tanto/ese-momento-glorioso-cuando-la-cdmx-tenia-tranvias/). **Game use:** a foreign utility company literally owns the light — the stealth player's enemy; substations and power cut-outs as stealth tools.
- **Porfirian high society in decline.** The Spanish tiple María Conesa performed for Porfirio Díaz, counted Villa and Zapata among admirers, and was "a constant in the parties of the survivors of high Porfirian society" — [Wikipedia: María Conesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Conesa); [México Desconocido](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/maria-conesa-la-gatita-blanca-de-la-revolucion-mexicana.html).

### Inferences
- Power in the 1920s city is a three-way knot: revolutionary generals/state, the Church driven underground, and organised labour bought by the state. Foreign capital (power, trams, oil) and the faded Porfirian elite sit around the edges. That maps cleanly onto game factions (see section 8).
- The Obregón killing (a sketch artist, a nun, a restaurant garden party) is a ready-made template for a set-piece investigation.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session (general knowledge; orientation links not opened): the "Sonoran dynasty" (Obregón 1920-24, Calles 1924-28, the Maximato 1928-34); the De la Huerta rebellion 1923-24; Obregón lost his right arm in 1915 and it was later displayed in a jar at the La Bombilla monument (labelled; check) — [Wikipedia: Álvaro Obregón (not opened)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81lvaro_Obreg%C3%B3n); the execution of Jesuit Miguel Pro by firing squad in Mexico City, Nov 1927, photographed for the press — [Wikipedia: Miguel Pro (not opened)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Pro); the Huitzilac massacre of General Francisco Serrano (1927); the Bucareli Agreements (1923) on US oil interests and Article 27 — [Wikipedia: Bucareli Treaty (not opened)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucareli_Treaty).
- Suspicion that Morones/CROM was behind Obregón's murder is commonly reported but I did not find a source this session.
- Morones's famous diamond rings: not confirmed by the sources found (Wikipedia only says "displays of great wealth").

## 2. Law, crime and policing

### Takeaway
The city's police were gendarmes of the Inspección General de Policía, frequently in violent conflict with the urban poor; night streets also had the older sereno (lantern-carrying night watchman) tradition. The Porfirian panopticon Lecumberri ("Palacio Negro") was the central prison. The defining crime legend is the 1915 Banda del Automóvil Gris (fake soldiers raiding rich houses in a grey car), and crime news (nota roja) was a mass genre.

### Cited Findings
- **Gendarmes and police violence.** Historian Alejandro Ponce Hernández studies "generalized violence" between gendarmes of the Inspección General de Policía and residents of 1920s Mexico City, from judicial files and newspapers — [Historia Mexicana (COLMEX) record](https://revistas.colmex.mx/Record/oai:oai.historiamexicana.colmex.mx:article-4682); [SciELO PDF](https://www.scielo.org.mx/pdf/hm/v73n2/2448-6531-hm-73-02-797.pdf). UNAM study of the ideal of a "modern, hierarchical, militarised and technical" police: [IIH-UNAM "Los policías modernos" PDF](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/807/807-05-03-policiasmodernos.pdf). **Game use:** guards are not neutral — crowds may side against them, insult them, or shield the player.
- Piccato describes the city as "contested territory" where a growing urban poor negotiated public space with more powerful citizens and the police, and studies post-revolutionary corruption and organised crime, prisoners' letters and communities settling transgression outside the courts — [Duke UP](https://www.dukeupress.edu/City-of-Suspects); [Aguirre review PDF](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~caguirre/Aguirre_Piccato.pdf). **Game use:** neighbourhood "justice" (vecindad courtyards settling their own scores) as an alternative to police.
- **Serenos (night watchmen).** Shift 11 p.m.-5 a.m.; carried a *chuzo* (iron-tipped staff), a lantern and a whistle; wore wide-brimmed hat, hood and overcoat; watched streets, checked public lights, kept neighbourhood house keys, and called the hour and weather — e.g. "Las doce en punto y sereno" ("Twelve o'clock and all clear") — [Chilango: oficios chilangos extintos](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/); [Posta: oficio virreinal del toque de queda](https://www.posta.com.mx/cdmx/el-antiguo-oficio-que-ya-no-existe-en-cdmx-y-nacio-en-la-epoca-virreinal-para-dar-el-toque-de-queda/vl2076316). A statue honours them at the Glorieta de Insurgentes (per the same Chilango article). **Game use:** the sereno is a ready-made Thief-style watchman: audible hour-calls the player can time against, a lantern (light radius), a whistle (alarm), and — crucially — he holds the keys to the houses. Caveat: the sources describe the trade generally from the colonial period; how many serenos survived into the 1920s alongside gendarmes is not established (see Gaps).
- **Lecumberri, "Palacio Negro".** Penitentiary 1900-1976, inaugurated by Porfirio Díaz; construction from 1888; design by Miguel S. Macedo (himself later jailed there for months during the Revolution); based on Bentham's panopticon; built for 800 men, 180 women and 400 children, 804 cells, workshops, nursery, bakery; became the national archive (AGN) in 1980 — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Lecumberri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Lecumberri); [INAH monument catalogue PDF](https://catalogonacionalmhi.inah.gob.mx/consulta_publica/generarPdfDetallePublico/14139); [SciELO article](https://www.scielo.org.mx/pdf/hg/n38/n38a5.pdf). **Game use:** a radial panopticon with a central watch tower is a superb stealth level ("you never know when the guard is looking").
- **Banda del Automóvil Gris (1915).** During the Revolution's chaos, a gang robbed the houses of the richest families, posing as military men and escaping in a grey car (a Fiat, per the film literature); the 1919 film *El automóvil gris* was based on reporter Miguel Nocoechea's records and the notes of detective Juan Manuel Cabrera, played in 19 cinemas at once, and was re-cut with sound in 1937 — [SensaCine: El automóvil gris](https://www.sensacine.com.mx/peliculas/pelicula-2765/secretos/); [Morelia Film Festival](https://www.moreliafilmfest.com/en/peliculas/el-automovil-gris). **Game use:** criminals in borrowed uniforms with forged search warrants — perfect for a faction that blurs police and crooks; the grey car as a recurring sighting.
- **Monte de Piedad (pawnshop).** Founded by Pedro Romero de Terreros, Count of Regla, opened 25 Feb 1775 just off the Zócalo; tezontle façade with the Count's arms; modelled on the Italian *Monte di Pietà*; declared a national charity in 1927 — [Wikipedia: Nacional Monte de Piedad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacional_Monte_de_Piedad); [HMdb marker](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=122602). **Game use:** the pawn vault as the city's "memory of debts" — stolen loot surfacing in pledges; a heist location; pawn tickets as clues.
- **Nota roja.** For much of the 20th century the *nota roja* (red/crime news) was one of Mexico's most important journalistic genres, focused on urban crime, especially in Mexico City; from 1925-1955 police fiction also ran in the supplements of *El Universal*, *La Prensa* and *Excélsior*; *La Prensa* was built on police news with witness and investigator testimony — [INAH Diario de Campo PDF](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/7804/8661); [UNAL article](https://revistas.unal.edu.co/index.php/achsc/article/view/83158); [UNAM thesis PDF](https://ru.dgb.unam.mx/bitstream/20.500.14330/TES01000618851/3/0618851.pdf). **Game use:** a crime-reporter NPC who competes with the player to solve cases; in-world tabloid front pages that react to the player's crimes.

### Inferences
- Two layers of watch: the modern gendarme (uniformed, armed, tied to the state) and the older sereno (lantern, keys, hour calls) suit two guard archetypes with different senses and rhythms.
- The real detective of the 1915 case (Cabrera) plus a crime reporter (Nocoechea) give an authentic template for a detective/journalist pairing.

### Gaps
- Gendarme uniforms of the 1920s (colour, kepi or helmet, sabre/pistol): not found in a citable source this session; check the Casasola archive (section 7 / photo archives) under "gendarme".
- Whether serenos still worked in 1920s Mexico City (vs. being replaced by gendarmes and electric light) is not established by my sources.
- Names of the 1915 gang members (e.g. Higinio Granda) and the alleged protection by General Pablo González: widely repeated, not verified this session (Spanish Wikipedia URL attempted returned 404).
- *La Prensa*'s founding date (commonly given as 1928) not confirmed by a fetched source.
- Rateros (pickpockets) and contraband: covered in depth by Piccato but I had no direct page excerpt to cite specifics.

## 3. Trades and street figures

### Takeaway
The street was a theatre of trades, each with its own cry (*pregón*), tool and posture: water sellers (fading after Porfirian plumbing), public letter-writers under the Santo Domingo arcades, candle and firewood sellers, milkmen, charlatans, newsboys and food sellers. These give a loud, readable soundscape and a cast of informants.

### Cited Findings
- **Evangelistas (public scribes).** Under the "Portal de los Evangelistas" beside Plaza de Santo Domingo (corner of República de Brasil), scribes wrote letters and official petitions from dictation or composed them for clients; recorded since the 17th century, most important in the 19th, when most people could not write; after the Revolution illiteracy still exceeded 60%; the arcade gradually turned into small print shops — [Chilango: oficios antiguos de Santo Domingo](https://www.chilango.com/agenda/oficios-antiguos-plaza-santo-domingo/); [HMdb marker](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=122238); photo essay (modern): [Ewan Telford, "The last scribes and printers"](https://ewan-telford.format.com/the-last-scribes-and-printers). **Game use:** the scribe knows everyone's secrets (love letters, debts, petitions) — an ideal information broker; forged documents and printing for the player's disguises.
- **Aguadores (water carriers).** Drew and sold water from fountains; entry to the trade required another worker's recommendation; disappeared as Porfirian plumbing and drainage arrived — [Chilango: oficios extintos](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/). **Game use:** in an alternate city, aguadores who still know the old aqueduct and fountain network = route-finders.
- **Other street trades.** Firewood sellers with donkey carts; milkmen by bicycle or horse collecting glass bottles; candle sellers carrying wares on long poles — [Chilango: oficios extintos](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/).
- **Pregones.** Cries of water sellers, sweet sellers, snack sellers, medicine hawkers, *merolicos* (quack talkers), beggars and *voceadores* (newsboys) flooded the colonial and Porfirian city; sellers used distinctive intonation and cadence and witty phrases; street sellers offered cocadas, cinnamon rosquillas, alfajores, buñuelos, tamales and tacos — [Chilango: dos siglos de chilanguismo](https://www.chilango.com/general/dos-siglos-de-chilanguismo-3/); [Redalyc PDF on pregones](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/351/35145982008.pdf); [INAH Boletín de Monumentos PDF](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/boletinmonumentos/article/download/2587/2492/4295). **Game use:** each trade gets a recognisable call as audio landmarks (and as cover noise for stealth).
- **Pulque (and implicitly tlachiqueros, the sap gatherers).** Murals in pulquerías show the extraction of agave sap — [historicalmx: Pulquerías in Mexico City](https://historicalmx.org/items/show/93).
- **Organilleros, cargadores, voceadores, tamaleras, fotógrafos ambulantes, curanderas, trajinera boatmen.** Photographic coverage of these exists in the Casasola archive (street trades, markets, pulquerías, cafés, shops, theatres) — [Plano Informativo on the Casasola archive](https://planoinformativo.com/996097/el-archivo-casasola-germen-del-fotoperiodismo-en-mexico); [INAH Mediateca (search portal)](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/). See also [Winfield Scott street-vendor photographs c.1900-1920 (UNM)](https://nmarchives.unm.edu/repositories/20/resources/4889).

### Inferences
- Trades sort neatly into gameplay roles: info brokers (evangelistas, voceadores, merolicos), route-knowers (aguadores, cargadores, trajineros), cover/noise (organilleros, pregoneros), healers/occult (curanderas).
- The scribe arcade turning into print shops is a lovely real "transition" detail — old trade vs. new technology — that suits the alt-1920s tone.

### Gaps
- Verbatim period pregones ("¡Tamales calientes!" type cries) were not found in a fetched source; the Redalyc and INAH PDFs above likely contain them (not opened).
- Specific sourced detail on organilleros (barrel organs, mostly German-made), cargadores with tumplines (mecapal), trajinera boatmen at Xochimilco/Santa Anita and the Canal de la Viga, curanderas and fotógrafos ambulantes: not gathered this session.

## 4. Nightlife and leisure

### Takeaway
Leisure divided by class and by modernity: over a thousand painted pulquerías for the poor (already in decline against beer), downtown cantinas like La Ópera, dance halls like Salón México (opened 1920, floors divided by class), carpas (tent theatres, nurturing the pelado comic Cantinflas by c.1930), and the glamorous teatro de revista with Spanish tiples like María Conesa. The 1924 "war on the pelonas" shows modern women in public space provoking violence.

### Cited Findings
- **Pulquerías.** By the early 20th century the city had over a thousand; their decline came from beer competition and smear campaigns; interiors and façades carried murals (Mayahuel, Virgin of Guadalupe, drinking monks, sap-gathering scenes) — [historicalmx](https://historicalmx.org/items/show/93). Pulque and pulquerías became symbols of national identity for 1920s-30s muralists (Orozco, Rivera) — [Philadelphia Museum of Art object note](https://philamuseum.org/collection/object/346296). In 1926 Diego Rivera and Edward Weston published essays on pulquerías, with photographs, in the bilingual *Mexican Folkways*, including a section "Los nombres de las pulquerías" — [Princeton: "The last word in direct naive realism"](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-last-word-in-direct-naive-realism-diego-rivera-edward-weston-/); [revistas-culturales.de digitised *Mexican Folkways*](https://www.revistas-culturales.de/es/node/351247). **Game use:** pulquerías with absurd, poetic names and painted walls are perfect hubs and safe-houses; the murals can hide clues.
- **Carpas.** Carpa theatre flourished in the 1920s-30s: comic sketches, political satire, acrobatics, dance; the government encouraged it as an alternative to the pulque hall and brothel; by 1930 Cantinflas worked the Ofelia, the Sotelo carpa of Azcapotzalco and the Valentina — [Wikipedia: Cantinflas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantinflas). The *pelado* stock figure is the penniless underdog who improvised on the cost of living and political scandals — [Wikipedia: Pelado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelado). **Game use:** a carpa comic whose patter is coded news; the tent as a stealth arena with canvas walls (light and shadow play).
- **Teatro de revista and tiples.** María Conesa, "La Gatita Blanca" (b. 1892 Vinaròs, Spain; d. 1978 Mexico City), tiple cómica and star of revue; admirers included Díaz, Villa and Zapata — [Wikipedia: María Conesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Conesa); [Museo del Estanquillo exhibition "Escenas de pudor y liviandad"](https://artsandculture.google.com/exhibit/escenas-de-pudor-y-liviandad/FgLS4rX32KCLJg). **Game use:** a star singer moving between generals and old aristocrats = a perfect double-agent character.
- **Salón México.** Opened 20 April 1920 at Pensador Mexicano 16, colonia Guerrero; called the "cathedral of danzón"; three dance floors classified by the social class of the dancers (nicknamed floors, e.g. "de cebo" — "tallow", the cheapest) plus a mirror room; later inspired Emilio Fernández's film *Salón México* (1949, labelled later) — [Chilango: lo que la modernidad se llevó](https://www.chilango.com/ciudad/lo-que-la-modernidad-se-llevo/); [UNAM Archipiélago PDF](https://revistas.unam.mx/index.php/archipielago/article/download/79275/70046). **Game use:** one building, three class layers — a ready-made level with social stealth per floor.
- **Cantinas: La Ópera.** Began as a French bakery (1876), moved in 1895 to 5 de Mayo and Filomeno Mata, a cantina from 1906; the bullet hole in the ceiling is attributed to Pancho Villa — accounts conflict (Villa shooting at the ceiling in 1910 or 1914, vs. a failed attempt on Villa's life) — [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/mexico-citys-best-cantinas/); [Chilango: Bar La Ópera centenario](https://www.chilango.com/ciudad/bar-la-opera-un-negocio-chilango-centenario/amp/); [CDMX Secreta](https://cdmxsecreta.com/en/oldest-restaurants-mexico-city/). **Game use:** contested legends are good dialogue — each NPC tells the bullet-hole story differently.
- **The pelonas (flappers), July 1924.** Male students from the National Medical School and the National Preparatory School attacked several *pelonas* (bobbed-hair modern women), soaking them and trying to shave their heads; *Excélsior* (23 July 1924) quoted students calling it a "national duty", linking bobbed hair to the "taxicab", the "rapid lunch" and the "jazz-band" — [Anne Rubenstein, "The War on Las Pelonas" (York U)](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-modern-women-and-their-enemies-mexico-city-1924/); [Wikipedia: Mexican-American women's fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican-American_women%27s_fashion). **Game use:** a gang of reactionary students as street antagonists; a pelona faction (typists, shopgirls, cinema-goers) as modern, mobile allies.

### Inferences
- Nightlife locations give natural social-stealth arenas stratified by class (pulquería → cantina → dance hall floors → revue theatre → aristocratic salon).
- The pulquería's decline (beer, hygiene campaigns) is a usable conflict: brewers and reformers vs. pulque sellers.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session: El Tenampa (Garibaldi, commonly dated 1925, mariachi), the frontón/jai alai (Frontón México opened 1929 — check), bullfights at the Toreo de la Condesa, cockfights, the Lotería Nacional, cinema palaces (e.g. Olimpia), revue star Lupe Rivas Cacho. The famous Salón México sign about not throwing cigarette butts so the ladies don't burn their feet is often quoted; not confirmed here.
- "Fifís" and "lagartijos" (dandies of Plateros/Madero street): my search confirmed Plateros/Madero as the Porfirian promenade "mandatory passageway for all social classes" ([Wikipedia: Madero Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madero_Street); [UNAM IIB "Viajes por la Avenida Plateros"](https://sigloxix.iib.unam.mx/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/2-7_-Comentario-de-%E2%80%9CViajes-por-la-Avenida-Plateros%E2%80%9D.pdf)) but did NOT find a source defining "lagartijo"/"fifí".

## 5. Dress and appearance

### Takeaway
Sources found confirm the pelona look (bob, red lips, short dresses) and the pelado/Cantinflas look (baggy trousers, rope belt, sparse moustache), and the sereno's hat-hood-overcoat-lantern kit; broader costume (charro, china poblana, rebozo, sarape, clergy) is best taken from the photo archives.

### Cited Findings
- Pelonas: bobbed hair, scarlet lips, short dresses; usually women aged 15-25 — [Wikipedia: Mexican-American women's fashion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican-American_women%27s_fashion); [Rubenstein (York U)](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-gender-and-the-revolutionary-state-in-mexico-city-1924/).
- The pelado (Cantinflas's version): poor slum-dweller, baggy trousers, rope for a belt, a distinctive thin moustache — [Wikipedia: Cantinflas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantinflas).
- Sereno: wide-brimmed hat, hood, overcoat, chuzo, lantern, whistle — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/).
- Clergy dress was itself illegal in public after June 1926 (500-peso fine) — [Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches). **Game use:** a cassock is a disguise that makes you MORE visible; a priest in a lay suit with a hidden stole is the authentic look.
- Photo references for dress (gendarmes, soldiers, charros, market women in rebozos, street children, nuns): [INAH Mediateca / Fototeca Nacional (Casasola)](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/); [INAH article: El Archivo Casasola](https://biblat.unam.mx/en/revista/antropologia-revista-interdisciplinaria-del-inah/articulo/el-archivo-casasola-experiencia-fundacional-de-la-fototeca).

### Inferences
- A visual language of class in silhouettes: rope belt and huaraches (pelado), rebozo and braid (the "dark braid" the students defended), bob and short hem (pelona), frock coat and bowler (old regime), khaki and Stetson-style hats (revolutionary generals).

### Gaps
- No fetched source on charro/china poblana as 1920s urban dress, rebozo/sarape use, overalls, military uniforms of the 1920s federal army, or gendarme uniforms. Recommend a pass through the Casasola holdings (Mediateca) with searches "gendarme", "charro", "china poblana", "rebozo".

## 6. Daily rhythm, sound and technology

### Takeaway
The 1920s city ran on electric trams (since 1900, powered by foreign hydroelectricity), newspapers, a brand-new radio (May 1923), and the older sounds of church bells (silenced liturgically 1926-29), hour-calling night watchmen and street cries.

### Cited Findings
- **Trams.** Porfirio Díaz inaugurated electric trams on 15 Jan 1900 (first run Chapultepec-Tacuba); 11 electric lines for passengers and freight opened 1900-1902, replacing mule-drawn cars — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/mientras-tanto/ese-momento-glorioso-cuando-la-cdmx-tenia-tranvias/); [Posta: 125 años de electromovilidad](https://www.posta.com.mx/mexico/125-anos-de-electromovilidad-en-cdmx-desde-el-tranvia-hasta-el-cablebus/vl1744403); [Chilango photo history](https://www.chilango.com/noticias/transporte/del-tranvia-al-tren-ligero-asi-ha-cambiado-el-transporte-electrico-en-cdmx-fotos/amp/); [UNAM PUEC "Transporte urbano" PDF](https://puec.unam.mx/pdf/libros_digitales/Publicaciones/transporte_urbano.pdf). Electricity from the Necaxa falls lit public streets and ran industry and transport — [UB Geocrit PDF](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/Electr-y-territorio/GomezMartinez.pdf). **Game use:** tram bells as an audio clock; riding on the back of trams as traversal; overhead lines as hazards/shortcuts.
- **Radio.** 8 May 1923: "El Universal / La Casa del Radio" (later CYL) went on air in Mexico City with 50 W, co-owned by *El Universal* and Raúl Azcárraga's La Casa del Radio shop chain; Andrés Segovia and Manuel Ponce attended the inauguration; 500 W from September; off air in 1928 — [Wikipedia: Radio in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_in_Mexico); [UNAM CCH PDF "Radio 1920"](https://portalacademico.cch.unam.mx/repositorio-de-sitios/historico-social/historia-de-mexico-2/HMIICultura_Vida/Radio1920.pdf); [Crónica: primeras radiodifusoras](https://www.cronica.com.mx/nacional/emociones-novedad-sonido-primeras-radiodifusoras-comerciales.html). **Game use:** a radio broadcast as an in-world news channel and a mystery (voices on the wrong frequency is a natural gothic hook).
- **Newspapers.** *El Universal*, *Excélsior* and *La Prensa* carried crime news and police fiction (1925-55) — [INAH Diario de Campo PDF](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/7804/8661).
- **Church silence.** From 1 Aug 1926 public worship was suspended — no sacraments in churches — [EWTN News](https://www.ewtnnews.com/world/americas/100-years-since-the-cristero-war-in-mexico-what-you-should-know).
- **Night.** Serenos worked 11 p.m.-5 a.m. calling the hours — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/).
- **Food.** Street foods recorded: tamales, tacos, buñuelos, cocadas, alfajores, cinnamon rosquillas; aguas frescas of lemon, orange, pineapple, chía with horchata — [Chilango: dos siglos de chilanguismo](https://www.chilango.com/general/dos-siglos-de-chilanguismo-3/).

### Inferences
- A day/night sound map: morning trams and newsboys, midday market cries, evening organ grinders and dance halls, night sereno calls — useful for audio-led stealth timing.
- The 1926-29 bell silence (worship suspended) is a powerful mood device: a city where the bells have stopped, and someone is still ringing one.

### Gaps
- Not verified this session: Buenavista station; Café de Tacuba (opened 1912, check); Sanborns in the Casa de los Azulejos (from c.1919, check); dates of *El Universal* (1916) and *Excélsior* (1917); gas vs electric street lamps by district; market hours (La Merced, Lagunilla); Día de Muertos, Semana Santa and 12 December Guadalupe observances under the 1926-29 worship suspension.
- Hemeroteca Nacional Digital de México (HNDM, UNAM) is the primary newspaper archive; I did not open it this session: [hndm.iib.unam.mx (not opened)](https://hndm.iib.unam.mx/).

## 7. Language: slang, caló, pelado speech

### Takeaway
"Caló" in Mexican Spanish is underworld / thieves' jargon, enriched up to the early 20th century by Spanish Gypsy slang; the 1920s "pelado" (penniless urban migrant) became both a comic type (Cantinflas's nonsense-speech) and, in Samuel Ramos (1934, later), a theory of national character.

### Cited Findings
- Caló = criminals' (especially thieves') jargon, very expressive, feeding colloquial Mexican Spanish; the late 19th and early 20th century was the last phase in which Spanish Gypsy slang could influence it — [Masaryk University study (PDF)](https://digilib.phil.muni.cz/cs/_flysystem/fedora/pdf/125851.pdf); [MUNI publication record](https://muni.cz/en/research/publications/1812437).
- "Pelado" named a 1920s class of penniless, uprooted, un- or under-employed urban slum-dwellers; Samuel Ramos (1934 — after the period) called the pelado "the most elemental and clearly defined expression of national character" — [Wikipedia: Pelado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelado).
- Pelado comedy improvised on the cost of living and political scandal — [Wikipedia: Pelado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelado); Cantinflas's character — [Wikipedia: Cantinflas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantinflas).
- Pachuco caló began in US border cities in the 1920s (flourished 1940s-50s, later and elsewhere — use with care) — [Revista Bicentenario PDF "Bato pachuco"](https://revistabicentenario.com.mx/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/BiC_47_05_Bato_pachuco.pdf).
- Insult language around modernity: 1924 students tying bobbed hair to "taxicab", "rapid lunch" and "jazz-band" — [Rubenstein](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-modern-women-and-their-enemies-mexico-city-1924/).

### Inferences
- "Cantinflear" (to talk a lot and say nothing) is a natural dialogue mechanic: a character who evades interrogation by verbal fog.
- Underworld caló vs. elite Frenchified speech vs. revolutionary officialese gives three registers for writers.

### Gaps
- No verified period word list (e.g. "chamba", "lana", "cuates", "chale", "ñero", albures, "jefe/jefecito", "ustedes" vs "su merced") was found this session. Recommended sources not opened: Piccato's *City of Suspects* (prisoners' letters), and period caló glossaries in UNAM theses.

## 8. Factions and character archetypes (grounded seeds for an alternate-universe version)

### Takeaway
Real 1920s structures translate directly into factions: a revolutionary general-state, a hidden Church, a bought union, foreign light-and-power capital, fading aristocrats, a crime press, an older night watch, and the urban poor with their own justice. Each seed below points to its historical source.

### Cited Findings (each seed = historical basis + alternate twist)
1. **The Sonoran Generals (the State).** Basis: Obregón/Calles rule, assassination at La Bombilla — [Toral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_Le%C3%B3n_Toral). Twist: the dead president-elect's relics (an embalmed arm?) are kept in a shrine-monument and guarded; the generals rule by them.
2. **The Silent Church / Cristeros of the City.** Basis: Calles Law, suspension of worship 1926-29, Madre Conchita's circle — [Catholic Answers](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/when-the-bishops-closed-the-churches); [El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/la-monja-que-colgo-el-habito-despues-de-la-muerte-de-obregon/). Twist: a hidden abbess who "blesses" assassins; underground Masses in pulquería cellars.
3. **The Grupo Acción (the Union).** Basis: Morones's CROM and its inner clique — [CROM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Confederation_of_Mexican_Workers). Twist: a labour brotherhood whose dues buy protection; strikes as city-wide blackouts.
4. **The Light Company (foreign capital).** Basis: Mexican Light and Power and the Tramways Company — [UB Geocrit](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/Electr-y-territorio/GomezMartinez.pdf). Twist: an Anglo-Canadian company that owns every lamp — and decides which streets go dark.
5. **The Serenos (old night watch).** Basis: lantern, chuzo, whistle, house keys, hour calls — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/oficios-chilangos-extintos/amp/). Twist: a guild older than the Republic that never stopped walking — the keys they hold open every house in the old city.
6. **The Gendarmerie (Inspección General).** Basis: violent, contested 1920s policing — [Historia Mexicana](https://revistas.colmex.mx/Record/oai:oai.historiamexicana.colmex.mx:article-4682). Twist: bureaucratic, militarised, hated; standard stealth guards.
7. **The Grey Car (crooks in uniform).** Basis: Banda del Automóvil Gris, 1915 — [SensaCine](https://www.sensacine.com.mx/peliculas/pelicula-2765/secretos/). Twist: a gang that wears real uniforms with forged warrants, protected by someone high up; their grey car appears before every crime.
8. **The Evangelistas (scribes and forgers).** Basis: Santo Domingo arcade scribes and printers — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/agenda/oficios-antiguos-plaza-santo-domingo/). Twist: they keep copies of every letter they ever wrote — a secret archive of the city's sins.
9. **The Nota Roja reporter.** Basis: crime-news genre, *La Prensa*, police fiction in supplements — [INAH](https://www.revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/7804/8661); the 1915 reporter Nocoechea — [Morelia FF](https://www.moreliafilmfest.com/en/peliculas/el-automovil-gris). Twist: a photographer-reporter whose plates capture things that aren't there.
10. **The Vasconcelos Missionaries and Muralists.** Basis: SEP 1921, cultural missions 1923, murals, green books — [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/600/60048431005.pdf). Twist: muralists paint protective (or binding) images on walls; teachers sent to "civilise" the barrios find older powers there.
11. **The Pelonas.** Basis: 1924 attacks on bobbed-haired women — [Rubenstein](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-modern-women-and-their-enemies-mexico-city-1924/). Twist: a network of typists, telephone girls and dance-hall regulars who pass information across the city.
12. **The Preparatoria students (reactionary toughs).** Basis: the same 1924 attacks — [Rubenstein](https://profiles.laps.yorku.ca/publications/the-war-on-las-pelonas-gender-and-the-revolutionary-state-in-mexico-city-1924/). Twist: a pseudo-patriotic gang enforcing "tradition" at night.
13. **The Carpa players and the Pelado.** Basis: carpas as government-tolerated popular theatre; Cantinflas c.1930 — [Cantinflas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantinflas); [Pelado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelado). Twist: a comic who talks in fog (cantinflear) and smuggles news in his jokes; travelling tents as moving safehouses.
14. **The Tiple and the Old Regime salons.** Basis: María Conesa, Porfirian society's survivor parties — [María Conesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Conesa). Twist: a revue star loved by both generals and old aristocrats, selling secrets in both directions.
15. **The Pulqueros vs. the Brewers.** Basis: 1000+ pulquerías, decline under beer competition and hygiene smears; murals of Mayahuel — [historicalmx](https://historicalmx.org/items/show/93). Twist: pulque as an old, sacred (and occult) drink; the pulquería guild resists brewery barons and reformers.
16. **The Monte de Piedad clerks.** Basis: 1775 pawnshop off the Zócalo — [Monte de Piedad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacional_Monte_de_Piedad). Twist: the pawn vault keeps what people pledge — including things that are not objects.

### Inferences
- Lecumberri's panopticon, Salón México's three class floors, the silent churches, the Santo Domingo arcade, La Bombilla's garden restaurant and the pulquería are the strongest "level seeds" from this research.
- Two watchman archetypes (sereno vs. gendarme) give stealth variety grounded in history.

### Gaps
- Seeds 1, 2, 5 and 16's "twists" are pure invention for the alternate universe; the bases are cited.
- Photo-archive pass still to do for visual reference: [INAH Mediateca](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/) (Casasola: 400,000+ pieces acquired by the state in 1976; Fototeca Nacional ~900,000 pieces, 1847-present — [Plano Informativo](https://planoinformativo.com/996097/el-archivo-casasola-germen-del-fotoperiodismo-en-mexico)); [University of Alabama 1922 Mexico street market photo](https://archives.lib.ua.edu/repositories/3/archival_objects/291756); [UNM Winfield Scott collection](https://nmarchives.unm.edu/repositories/20/resources/4889). The Library of Congress search did not return a specific 1920s Mexico City street-trades collection.
