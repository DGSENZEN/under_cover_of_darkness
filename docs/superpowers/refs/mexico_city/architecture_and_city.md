## 1. Tenochtitlan and the lake: causeways, chinampas, canals, Templo Mayor, the drainage (Desagüe) and subsidence

### Takeaway
Mexico City began as an island city of 200-400k people, set on a grid aligned to the compass and laced with canals, joined to shore by three causeways with removable bridges. Over four centuries the lake was drained: tunnels and cuts from 1607, then the Porfirian Gran Canal in 1900. Draining the lake left the city on soft clay that is still sinking, by more than 12 m in under a century in places, so its churches and palaces lean, crack and sit below street level. That sinking is the city's most distinctive physical "uncanny" fact.

### Cited Findings
**Tenochtitlan, the lake city**
- Three causeways joined the island to Tepeyac (north), Iztapalapa (south) and Tlacopan (west). Bernal Díaz said they were wide enough for ten horses. The main causeway ran about a league (4.83 km), and bridges that could be pulled away cut through them for canoe passage and defence — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)
- The levee (albarradón) of Nezahualcoyotl, 12-16 km long and finished c.1453, separated fresh spring water from the brackish lake. Two terracotta aqueducts, each over 4 km, brought water from Chapultepec — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)
- The city was "interlaced with a series of canals" so every section could be reached on foot or by canoe. It was split into four zones, each of 20 calpullis (districts). The ceremonial centre was a walled square 500 m a side holding about 45 public buildings. The city covered 8-13.5 km² with 200,000-400,000 inhabitants — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)
- Ordinary houses were of reed plastered with mud under thatch; the wealthy had stone houses with flat roofs — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)
- The streets and canals ran to the cardinal directions in square blocks. The Spanish kept the main N-S and E-W roads; the colonial traza covered 180 ha in 100 blocks; there were eight principal canals, one along the south side of the main plaza (the Zócalo). The canals began to shrink early as streets were widened — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)
- Earliest European images were woodcuts published in Augsburg around 1522, plus the "First European map of Tenochtitlan, 1524" (the Cortés/Nuremberg map) — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan). Images: Commons "Old maps of Tenochtitlan" [16] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Old_maps_of_Tenochtitlan>; "Tenochtitlan" [52] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tenochtitlan>; Codex Mendoza (founding glyph: eagle on cactus on a rock in the water) [223] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Codex_Mendoza>
- The Tlatelolco market, by Cortés's estimate, saw 60,000 people trading daily (Sahagún: 20,000, or 40,000 on feast days) — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan). Images: Tlatelolco [27] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tlatelolco>; Plaza de las Tres Culturas (Aztec platforms + colonial church + modern blocks in one view) <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Plaza_de_las_Tres_Culturas>

**Templo Mayor**
- A twin pyramid (Huēyi Teōcalli) about 100 x 80 m at the base. Two shrines on top, each with its own staircase: Huitzilopochtli (war) and Tlaloc (rain). It was rebuilt six times after 1325, each stage built over the last like nested shells. The Spanish almost totally destroyed it in 1521 and built the cathedral nearby; the ruins lie in the block between Seminario and Justo Sierra streets — [Wikipedia: Templo Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templo_Mayor)
- Offerings found include Tlaloc effigy pots, skeletons of crocodiles, fish and turtles, coral, decorated skulls and obsidian/flint knives — [Wikipedia: Templo Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templo_Mayor)
- The Coyolxauhqui disc (3.25 m diameter) was found in the 1978-82 excavations — [Wikipedia: Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitlan)
- Images: Templo Mayor Archaeological Zone [111] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Templo_Mayor_Archeological_Zone>; Museo del Templo Mayor [22] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Museo_del_Templo_Mayor>

**Chinampas, Xochimilco, trajineras, Canal de la Viga**
- Chinampas were not floating. They were artificial islands made by fencing rectangles with wattle and stakes and filling them with mud, lake sediment and rotting vegetation. Willows (āhuexōtl, *Salix bonplandiana*) and cypresses (āhuēhuētl) were planted at the corners, and canoe-width channels ran between the beds — [Wikipedia: Chinampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinampa). Images [25] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Chinampas>
- Xochimilco keeps about 170 km of canals (a UNESCO World Heritage Site) ridden on trajineras. Aztec emperors used royal barges on this water route to Chalco/Xico. The "Island of the Dolls", a chinampa hung with decaying dolls, is reached by canal — [Wikipedia: Xochimilco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xochimilco). Images: Isla de las Muñecas [133] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Isla_de_las_Muñecas>
- A trajinera is a flat-bottomed wooden boat poled or rowed by one man. Once lake transport, in the 20th century it became a tourist boat with painted canopies carrying names, often women's names — [Wikipedia: Trajinera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajinera). Images [86] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Trajineras>
- The Canal de la Viga (also called the Acequia Real or Canal Nacional) was part of the Mexico-Chalco water route through Culhuacán, Mexicalzingo, Iztacalco and Santa Anita, entering the city at the Garita (toll gate) de la Viga. It was the main supply route, and the Paseo de la Viga beside it was a social promenade. Search-result summaries say it was paved over in sections between 1921 and 1940, with one source giving 1933 for covering. These come from UAM/UNAM papers I did not open in full — [UAM Fuentes Humanísticas](https://fuenteshumanisticas.azc.uam.mx/index.php/rfh/article/download/928/907); Santa Anita arch on the city tourism site <https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/santa-anita-monument-arch-la-viga/?lang=es>. Images: Commons "Canal de la Viga" [20 files, mostly 19th-c. paintings and prints] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Canal_de_la_Viga>
- Steamboats once ran on the Canal de Chalco — [México Desconocido](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/cuando-los-barcos-de-vapor-navegaban-por-canal-de-chalco.html) (headline only; article not opened)

**The Desagüe (draining the lake) and the Gran Canal**
- Called "the greatest engineering project of colonial Spanish America". Moctezuma I and Nezahualcoyotl built a dike in the late 1440s. Floods in 1555-56 brought a second dike, and floods in 1604 and 1607 set off tunnelling by forced indigenous labour, work much like mining. A Dutch engineer, Adrian Boot, was consulted. Huge rains in 1628 overwhelmed the works; "most of the population fled, and buildings collapsed", and the crown even proposed moving the capital. The tunnel was then turned into an open-cut trench — [Wikipedia: Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe)
- In 1629 rain flooded the capital and the water stayed for several years. The first works were an 8-mile combined ditch and tunnel; 3,000 workers continued in 1608 — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)
- British contractor Weetman Pearson (S. Pearson & Son) finished the work under Díaz with imported machinery, including five steam dredges, for 16 million pesos. It was inaugurated with great fanfare in 1900 as a symbol of modernity, and it also carried sewage and waste from tanneries and slaughterhouses — [Wikipedia: Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe). Díaz opened it on 17 March 1900. The excavations turned up the Tequixquiac sacrum, a prehistoric carved bone, 12 m down — [es.Wikipedia: Gran Canal del Desagüe](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Canal_del_Desag%C3%BCe_%28Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico%29)
- Gran Canal + Tequixquiac Tunnel system built 1888-1903 for 13 million pesos (sources disagree: 13 vs 16 million); total length 47.5 km; a second tunnel 1937-46 — search summaries of [agua.org.mx PDF](https://agua.org.mx/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/equilibrio_hidrologico_valle_de_mexico.pdf) and [UANL PDF](https://cdigital.cabu.uanl.mx/fpm/34/1020132682/1020132682_029.pdf)
- Images: Tajo de Nochistongo, the colonial open cut [6] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tajo_de_Nochistongo>; Lake Texcoco <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Lake_Texcoco>

**Subsidence: the sinking city**
- Parts of the city, including the airport and the Angel of Independence, sink about 2 cm a month (~24 cm/yr), and the ground has dropped more than 12 m in under a century. The Metropolitan Cathedral is "visibly tilted" — [AP via 10News, 2026](https://www.10news.com/news/mexico-city-is-sinking-so-quickly-it-can-be-seen-from-space); also [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/nasa-satellite-reveals-just-how-fast-mexico-city-is-sinking)
- Bellas Artes "has sunk some four meters into the soft soil" since 1904 — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Bellas Artes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes)
- The cathedral used light tezontle and chiluca because of the poor soil. It was on the World Monuments Fund's endangered list for "dropping water tables and accelerated sinking" until 1990s works stabilised it (delisted in 2000) — [Wikipedia: Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Metropolitan_Cathedral)
- The Old Basilica of Guadalupe suffered differential subsidence. It had control piles fitted in 1976-82, and the sinking was one reason the new basilica was built (1974-76) — [Wikipedia: Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_of_Guadalupe)
- Templo de Loreto (1806-1819/1816, neoclassical, a huge dome, design involving Tolsá and others) leans markedly. The archdiocese lists it among churches at risk of loss from uneven sinking — [HMdb marker page](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=122640); [CDMX tourism: Plaza de Loreto](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/plaza-de-loreto/?lang=es)
- Most buildings around Plaza de Santo Domingo were rebuilt in the 18th century because of sinking and floods — [es.Wikipedia: Plaza de Santo Domingo](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Santo_Domingo_%28Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico%29)
- Images: Leaning buildings in Mexico [2] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Leaning_buildings_in_Mexico>; Metropolitan Cathedral <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Mexico_City>; La Santísima, a visibly sunken church [53] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:La_Santisima_Church>

### Inferences
- The visual signature of the "lake memory" is canal water edged with ahuejote willows, low flat-bottomed boats, toll-gate garitas at the waterline, dikes and causeways with gaps, and below-street-level church doors reached by steps *down*. The last of these is a common sight in the historic centre today (my observation, not sourced above).
- "Rebuilt six times, each over the last" (Templo Mayor) is a ready-made level-design principle: nested shells, with older layers reachable inside newer ones.
- Desagüe history gives real flavour for an AU where the drainage failed: forced labour in tunnels, the 1629 flood that lasted years, and the crown proposing to move the capital.

### Gaps
- I did not find a reliable source with exact widths or construction of Aztec canals, or the date and survival of specific colonial acequias other than La Viga.
- The exact date the Viga canal was filled is uncertain (1921-40 in sections vs 1933); the sources are search summaries only.
- I found no sourced claim about the extra steps added to the base of the Angel of Independence as the ground fell, though it is commonly reported.

## 2. Colonial (New Spain) architecture: materials, baroque, palaces, convents, aqueducts, fountains, plazas

### Takeaway
The colonial centre is a grid of blood-red, porous tezontle walls framed by pale grey chiluca/cantera stone. Its palaces are fortified, with corner towers, cannon-shaped gargoyles and big zaguán gateways opening onto arcaded patios. The churches carry Churrigueresque retablo façades built on the estípite (an inverted-pyramid pilaster), tiled domes, and indigenous details carved by native masons (atrial crosses, a reused Aztec serpent head).

### Cited Findings
**Materials**
- Tezontle is a porous, highly oxidised volcanic scoria, reddish from iron oxide. It is light, which "helps impede a building from sinking" into the lakebed, and is common on centre façades (National Palace, Cathedral, Nacional Monte de Piedad, Casa del Conde de la Torre de Cossío) — [Wikipedia: Tezontle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezontle). Images [4] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tezontle>
- The standard pairing is a tezontle face with doors and windows framed in grey chiluca. The Palace of the Inquisition is an example — [search summary of mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx Palace of the School of Medicine](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/palacio-de-escuela-de-medicina/)
- Talavera tiles: blue-and-white tin-glazed tiles from Puebla — [Wikipedia: Casa de los Azulejos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos); craft background <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talavera_pottery>

**Metropolitan Cathedral and Sagrario Metropolitano**
- Built 1573-1813, mixing Gothic, Baroque, Churrigueresque and Neoclassical. The Altar of the Kings by Jerónimo de Balbás (finished 1737) is 13.75 m wide, 25 m tall and 7.5 m deep, nicknamed "the golden cave" — [Wikipedia: Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Metropolitan_Cathedral)
- The Sagrario is by Lorenzo Rodríguez, "1749-1760" per the cathedral article and "1749-69" per the Churrigueresque article (a conflict). It is called the masterpiece of "the most visually intoxicating form of the style", Mexican Churrigueresque — [Wikipedia: Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque); [Wikipedia: Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Metropolitan_Cathedral)
- The estípite is a pilaster shaped like an inverted cone or obelisk and was the central ornamental element of Spanish/Mexican baroque from 1720 to 1760 — [Wikipedia: Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque)
- Images: Metropolitan Cathedral <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Mexico_City>; Sagrario sacristy <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Sagrario_Metropolitano_%28Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Mexico_City%29_-_Sacristy>; Plaza de la Constitución / Zócalo [198] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Plaza_de_la_Constituci%C3%B3n_%28Mexico_City%29>; National Palace <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:National_Palace_%28Mexico%29>

**Palaces (casas señoriales)**
- **Palacio de Iturbide** (1779-85, Francisco Antonio Guerrero y Torres; Madero 17). Three floors plus a mezzanine. Tezontle and cantera façade between two "fortified towers", with a central loggia. Carvings include flowers, small double-tailed mermaids and male figures. A big archway leads into a courtyard of 18 arches on Tuscan columns. Built as a dowry palace said to copy the royal palace of Palermo; Iturbide took the imperial crown from its balcony; it was a hotel for over 100 years after 1855 — [Wikipedia: Palace of Iturbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Iturbide). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palace_of_Iturbide>
- **Casa de los Azulejos** (the House of Tiles). Façade covered in blue-and-white Puebla Talavera. Carved stone frames the windows and doors, with French porcelain crowns. A baroque patio fountain sits under a 20th-century stained-glass roof. Sanborns bought it in 1917, and Orozco painted *Omniscience* on the stair in 1925 — [Wikipedia: Casa de los Azulejos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_de_los_Azulejos>
- **Casa de los Condes de Santiago de Calimaya** (now the Museo de la Ciudad de México, Pino Suárez 30, on the old Iztapalapa causeway). A tezontle façade with cantera portal and windows and basalt friezes. The baroque portal rests on "four clawed feet and mascarons". Its "cannon-shaped gargoyles... could be fired, but only with permission of city authorities". An Aztec feathered-serpent head, probably from the Templo Mayor, is set as its cornerstone. Guerrero y Torres remodelled it from 1777 — [Wikipedia: Museum of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Mexico_City). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Museo_de_la_Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico>
- **Palacio de los Condes de San Mateo de Valparaíso** (Guerrero y Torres; known for a spiral staircase, which has its own Commons subcategory) [15] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_de_los_Condes_de_San_Mateo_de_Valparaiso>
- **Casa de los Condes de Heras y Soto** [35] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_de_los_Condes_de_Heras_y_Soto>
- In the conquest era houses were "residences, warehouses or stores, and fortresses all at once", copying Seville. Calimaya began as a "fortress-house" — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City); [Wikipedia: Museum of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Mexico_City)

**Inquisition, Santo Domingo and the scribes' arcade**
- **Palace of the Inquisition** (1732-36, Pedro de Arrieta). It stands on the "esquina chata" (flat, chamfered corner) facing Plaza de Santo Domingo, with its entrance on the cut corner. Tezontle walls with chiluca frames. It was the Holy Office's seat until 1820 and is now the Museum of Mexican Medicine — [Wikipedia: Palace of the Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_the_Inquisition); [CDMX tourism](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/palace-school-medicine/?lang=es). Images: exterior [45] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_de_La_Inquisici%C3%B3n>; interior and museum [81] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palace_of_Inquisition-Museum_of_Mexican_Medicine>
- **Plaza de Santo Domingo**: the Santo Domingo church, the Inquisition palace, the old customs house and the Portales de los Evangelistas. Under these arcades, from the 19th century, public scribes ("evangelistas") wrote letters for the illiterate, first with quill pens, later typewriters — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Santo_Domingo_%28Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico%29). Images: plaza [38] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Plaza_de_Santo_Domingo,_Mexico_City>; church <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Church_of_Santo_Domingo_%28Mexico_City%29>

**Atrial crosses and open chapels**
- Atrial crosses are large stone crosses in church atriums, carved in relief by indigenous artisans. They mix the Arma Christi (instruments of the Passion) with local plants and sometimes an indigenous-featured face of Christ, in the Tequitqui style; their central placement echoed the Mesoamerican axis mundi — [Wikipedia: Atrial cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrial_cross). Images [50] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Atrial_cross>
- Open chapels (capillas abiertas) were outdoor sanctuaries facing large atriums for mass conversions — [Wikipedia: Capilla abierta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_chapel)

**Aqueducts and fountains**
- The Chapultepec aqueduct (1711-16) had 904 arches over 4,663 varas (about 4 km), following the Aztec route; it was also called the Aqueduct of Belén. About 22 arches survive near Metro Sevilla. It ends at the **Salto del Agua** fountain, a baroque fountain commissioned by Viceroy Agustín de Ahumada; the one in the street is a replica by Guillermo Ruiz, and the original is at the Museo Nacional del Virreinato. In the 19th century iron and lead pipes replaced the open troughs — [Wikipedia: Chapultepec aqueduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapultepec_aqueduct). Images: aqueduct [46] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Chapultepec_Aqueduct>; fountain [24] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Salto_del_Agua_Fountain>; Avenida Arcos de Belén <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Avenida_Arcos_de_Bel%C3%A9n_%28Mexico_City%29>; Aqueducts in Mexico City <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Aqueducts_in_Mexico_City>

**Courtyards, zaguanes, vecindad origins**
- Courtyards in Mexico City [24] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Courtyards_in_Mexico_City>
- Vecindades began as old elite mansions in the historic centre, subdivided so families rented rooms around a central patio and shared lavatories and kitchens — [Wikipedia: Vecindad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vecindad)

### Inferences
- Kit-bash palette for the colonial quarter: oxblood tezontle ashlar or rubble walls; grey cantera frames around every opening; chamfered or fortified corners; crenellated parapets with cannon gargoyles; carved portals on clawed feet; a central balcony; a tall zaguán entrance through to an arcaded courtyard; the flat roof (azotea) behind a parapet.
- The church kit needs an estípite retablo façade (it reads as an altarpiece turned inside out onto the street), an octagonal drum dome with Talavera tiles, a walled atrium with a carved cross, and sunken thresholds.
- Calimaya's Aztec serpent cornerstone and the Templo Mayor beside the Cathedral literalise "colonial city built on the Aztec one". This should be a repeated visual motif: idols, stones and glyphs embedded in the bases of colonial walls.

### Gaps
- I could not open a source for the exact date of the Salto del Agua fountain (commonly given as 1779) or a full description of its iconography.
- The popular legend of why the Casa de los Azulejos was tiled was not in the source I read.
- I found no dedicated Commons category for the Casa de Calimaya façade beyond the museum category, nor for zaguanes or tiled domes as a type.

## 3. Porfirian era (c.1876-1911): Beaux-Arts, Art Nouveau, iron and glass, colonias, Lecumberri

### Takeaway
Díaz's city put imported European and American skins (Italian marble, French Art Nouveau, German Jugendstil iron halls, New York steel frames) onto the sinking colonial grid. It opened new western colonias (Roma, Juárez, Santa María la Ribera) of eclectic mansions, and built a panopticon prison and a drainage canal as monuments of "order and progress".

### Cited Findings
- **Palacio de Bellas Artes**: Adamo Boari began it in 1904; the Revolution stopped it by 1913; Federico Mariscal finished it in 1932-34. The façades are white Carrara and Guerrero marble over beige Yautepec marble, Art Nouveau and Neoclassical outside and Art Deco inside. Pre-Hispanic motifs include "serpents' heads on window arches and Maya Chaac masks". The glass-and-iron roof is by Géza Maróti. The Tiffany glass curtain (about a million pieces, 24 t) shows the volcanoes Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl. The building has sunk about 4 m — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes>
- **Palacio Postal** (Boari; first stone 14 Sept 1902; opened 1907) stands on the site of a demolished Franciscan hospital, on a 70 cm steel-reinforced "Chicago" slab foundation made by Milliken Bros. of New York. Mostly Neo-Plateresque, faced in pale, almost translucent chiluca. It has "iron dragon light fixtures", polished Italian brass, different window styles on each floor, an Art Nouveau iron canopy, and slender Solomonic columns in the 4th-floor gallery — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Correos de México](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Correos_de_M%C3%A9xico). Images [38] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_Postal>
- **El Palacio de Hierro**, the first iron-and-steel building in the city (five storeys, Ignacio de la Hidalga; finished 1891). People asked "what iron palace are they building?". It burned on 15 April 1914 and reopened in 1921 in Paul Dubois's Art Nouveau building with stained-glass ceilings by Jacques Grüber of Nancy — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Palacio_de_Hierro). Images [15] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:El_Palacio_de_Hierro>
- **Casa Boker** (1900; De Lemos & Cordes of New York, the Macy's architects; built in 16 months). It is a German hardware emporium on a corner of 16 de Septiembre and Isabel la Católica. Cantera façade, decorated cornice, copper-sheet crest and corner entrance; US steel beams, Belgian glass, German cement and Italian marble. Digging the foundations turned up Aztec sculptures, including a decapitated eagle — [es.Wikipedia: Casa Boker](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_Boker). Images [21] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_Boker>
- **Edificio La Mutua** (1903-05, Theodore de Lemos with engineer Gonzalo Garita) was offices of the Mutual Life Insurance Co. of New York on Av. 5 de Mayo. Neoclassical outside; Carlos Obregón Santacilia remodelled it in 1926 into the Banco de México (Art Deco inside) — [Banco de México museum](https://museobancodemexico.mx/en/architectural-icon); [Crónica](https://www.cronica.com.mx/cultura/describen-transformacion-edificio-mutua-banco-mexico.html). Images [31] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Bank_of_Mexico_building,_Mexico_City>
- **Pabellón / Kiosco Morisco** (José Ramón Ibarrola): Mexico's pavilion at the 1884 New Orleans World's Fair, later shown in St Louis. Neo-Mudéjar, all wrought iron with a glass cupola, built from panels that can be taken apart. First set up on the Alameda, it was moved to Santa María la Ribera to make room for the Juárez monument — [Wikipedia: Morisco Kiosk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiosco_Morisco). Images [131] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Kiosco_Morisco>
- **Museo del Chopo**, a Jugendstil iron-and-glass hall by Bruno Möhring, made in Oberhausen for the 1902 Düsseldorf exhibition. It was shipped over and rebuilt in 1903-05 and nicknamed the "Crystal Palace": 1,500 m² with 47 m towers. From 1913 it was the Natural History Museum, home of a Diplodocus cast from 1926 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Universitario_del_Chopo). Images [120] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Museo_Universitario_del_Chopo>
- **Lecumberri** ("the Black Palace") opened in 1900 as a Bentham-style panopticon with 804 cells, designed for 800 men, 180 women and 400 children, with workshops and a nursery. Pancho Villa escaped in 1912. It became the National Archive in 1980, with cells used as document vaults — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Lecumberri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Lecumberri). Images [33] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_de_Lecumberri>
- **Hemiciclo a Juárez** (white marble, 1910 Centennial) [118] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Hemiciclo_a_Ju%C3%A1rez>; **Paseo de la Reforma** [47] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Paseo_de_la_Reforma>; the Angel of Independence was inaugurated at the 1910 grito — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City); **Chapultepec Castle** <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Chapultepec_Castle>; **Reloj Chino** (Chinese clock, Bucareli) <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Chinese_clock_in_Mexico_City>
- **Colonias**: Roma was planned as an upper-class Porfirian district in the early 1900s, built on the shallows of Lake Texcoco around the islet village of Aztacalco. Its streets are lined with Art Nouveau and Neoclassical houses. The **Edificio Río de Janeiro** ("Casa de las Brujas", 1908) is a red-brick castle-like block whose corner-tower windows seem to form a face, with an Art Deco entrance added in the 1930s — [Wikipedia: Colonia Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonia_Roma). Images: Roma [45] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Colonia_Roma_%28Mexico_City%29>; Casa de las Brujas <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_R%C3%ADo_de_Janeiro>; Juárez [16] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Colonia_Ju%C3%A1rez_%28Mexico_City%29>
- Santa María la Ribera is now a mix of old mansions (more than 1,000 catalogued as of historic value), tenements and abandoned buildings — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonia_Santa_Mar%C3%ADa_la_Ribera)
- By the early 20th century the city had gas and electric lighting and streetcars; during the Revolution there were battles in its streets and it was briefly occupied by Villa and Zapata — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City). Images: Trams in Mexico City [50] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Trams_in_Mexico_City>; Street lights [41] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Street_lights_in_Mexico_City>
- The Porfirian state saw pulquerías as the main source of vice and restricted them around 1900 — [Wikipedia: Pulqueria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulquer%C3%ADa)
- Cemeteries: **Panteón Francés de la Piedad** sold plots and buried from 1865 and was formally opened in 1872 (Porfirian elite mausolea) — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pante%C3%B3n_Franc%C3%A9s_de_la_Piedad); **Panteón de Dolores** opened in 1875 and is the largest cemetery in Mexico, with about 700,000 tombs and the Rotonda de las Personas Ilustres — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pante%C3%B3n_de_Dolores)

### Inferences
- The Porfirian layer works as the "foreign skin": marble and bronze fronts, iron canopies and glass roofs on structures that are visibly sinking (Bellas Artes down 4 m). For an AU, Boari's Chaac masks and serpent heads on a Beaux-Arts palace already show the "occult deco" fusion beginning around 1904.
- Imported, flat-packed buildings (Kiosco Morisco, Chopo) are a real precedent for an AU of buildings bought from catalogues and reassembled. They are also good iron-lattice climbing interiors.
- Lecumberri is a ready-made stealth level: a radial cell-block star with a central watchtower, later a document archive.

### Gaps
- Lecumberri's design is credited to Miguel S. Macedo by English Wikipedia, which I could not reconcile with the frequently cited architect Antonio Torres Torija. Treat attribution as unverified.
- No verified Commons category was found for the Panteón Francés's mausolea beyond a small category (7 files): <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pante%C3%B3n_Franc%C3%A9s>

## 4. The 1920s-30s: Neocolonial revival, neo-prehispanic "Aztec Deco", Estridentismo

### Takeaway
After the Revolution the city produced vertical Art Deco: stepped, setback, templo-mayor-like massing in concrete and granite (La Nacional, 1932), triangular "flatiron" mixed-use blocks (Ermita), streamlined frontones and cinemas, and the Porfirian legislative palace's dome turned into a 67 m deco triumphal arch-mausoleum. Alongside it ran the Estridentistas' imagined "Estridentópolis", an absurd, vertiginous modern city that is a direct literary source for an alt-1920s metropolis.

### Cited Findings
- **Monumento a la Revolución**: begun in 1910 as Émile Bénard's neoclassical / French Renaissance Federal Legislative Palace and halted by the Revolution. Carlos Obregón Santacilia reused the abandoned structure under Cárdenas, finishing in 1938 in "eclectic Art Deco and Mexican socialist realism". It is 67 m tall (described as the world's tallest triumphal arch), with stone sculpture groups by Oliverio Martínez, and is a mausoleum for Madero, Villa, Carranza, Calles and Cárdenas, former enemies buried together — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumento_a_la_Revoluci%C3%B3n). Images [160] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Monumento_a_la_Revoluci%C3%B3n_%28M%C3%A9xico%29>
- **Frontón México** (jai alai hall; cornerstone 1928, opened 10 May 1929; Teodoro Kunhardt and Joaquín Capilla). Art Deco with two pan-coupé (chamfered) façades bearing the carved name, "successive rectilinear planes" and octagonal windows, and its fronton wall built from leftover Bellas Artes marble. A betting palace for the elite where jacket and tie were obligatory (and could be rented at the door) — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front%C3%B3n_M%C3%A9xico). Images [15] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Front%C3%B3n_M%C3%A9xico>
- **Edificio La Nacional** (Manuel Ortiz Monasterio, 1929-32), the city's first skyscraper over 50 m (55 m, 13 floors) and the tallest building in 1932-37. Steel, concrete and granite on about 100 concrete piles driven to 55 m. Its design is said to be "inspired by the Templo Mayor" — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_La_Nacional). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_La_Nacional>
- **Edificio Ermita** (Juan Segura, 1927-33, Tacubaya), a triangular Art Deco block. It was the first mixed-use modern building in Mexico: a cinema (later Teatro Hipódromo), ground-floor shops and 66 flats of 35 m². Diego Rivera designed details such as the roof skylight. It housed Spanish Republican exiles — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_Ermita). Images [4] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_Ermita>
- **Edificio Basurto** (Francisco J. Serrano, 1940-45, *later than scope*): 14-storey post-Deco with a famous spiral stair, Condesa — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basurto_Building). Images [11] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_Basurto>
- **Condesa / Hipódromo**: many buildings date from the 1920s (e.g. a 1928 apartment complex now a hotel), and Art Deco was predominant into the 1940s. Examples include Edificio San Martín and Parque México — [Wikipedia: Condesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condesa). Images: Condesa [36] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Condesa_%28Mexico_City%29>; Parque México [162] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Parque_M%C3%A9xico>; Plaza Popocatépetl fountain <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Fuente_de_Plaza_Popocat%C3%A9petl>; Edificio Roxy <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_Roxy>; Edificio San Martín <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_San_Mart%C3%ADn_%28Condesa%29>; Art Deco architecture in Mexico [25] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Art_Deco_architecture_in_Mexico>
- **Museo de Arte Popular**, an Art Deco former fire/police station in the centre — images [55] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Museo_de_Arte_Popular,_Mexico_City> (building date not verified here)
- **Abelardo L. Rodríguez Market** (1934, built in the old San Gregorio convent precinct, *slightly later than scope*): a prototype modern market with day care and an auditorium, and about 1,450 m² of walls and ceilings covered in murals by Rivera's circle — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelardo_L._Rodr%C3%ADguez_Market). Images [15] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Abelardo_Rodriguez_Market>
- **Cine Ópera** (Deco cinema, 1949, *later*) <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cine_%C3%93pera>; **Teatro de la Ciudad Esperanza Iris** (1918 theatre) [43] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Teatro_de_la_Ciudad_%22Esperanza_Iris%22>; **Edificio Corcuera** (1930s tower that collapsed in the 1957 quake, per the La Nacional article) <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_Corcuera>
- The architectural mood of the time: historicist "California colonial" revival with baroque or neoclassical ornament on modern buildings competed with Art Deco as the expression of modernity — [es.Wikipedia: Edificio La Nacional](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_La_Nacional); background on revival styles <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Revival_architecture>
- **Estridentismo** (founded by Manuel Maples Arce; first manifesto Mexico City, 31 Dec 1921; first expo at the Café de Nadie, 1924; moved to Xalapa in 1925, recast as "Estridentópolis"; dissolved 1927). It drew on Futurism, Dada and Cubism with a revolutionary social edge. Members included Leopoldo Méndez, Ramón Alva de la Canal, Fermín Revueltas, Germán and Lola Cueto, Jean Charlot, Silvestre Revueltas, Edward Weston and Tina Modotti — [Wikipedia: Stridentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estridentismo)
- Estridentópolis is not literally Xalapa but the movement's projection of a modern city. It is "absurd, vertiginous, seat of multitudinous modernity", poised "between utopia and dystopia" — [es.Wikipedia: Estridentópolis](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estrident%C3%B3polis). Images (photographers in the circle): Tina Modotti <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tina_Modotti>; Edward Weston <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edward_Weston>

### Inferences
- "Aztec Deco" in the real city reads as stepped setbacks evoking the pyramid (La Nacional), chamfered corners (Frontón, a modern echo of the Inquisition's esquina chata), granite and marble cladding, octagonal windows and relief panels. Inside Bellas Artes, Deco rendered pre-Hispanic masks and serpents in metal and glass. This is the closest real anchor for Deadlock-style occult deco.
- The Monumento a la Revolución, a dome begun as a parliament and finished as an arch-tomb for enemies, is a strong gothic set piece: a reused skeleton, a crypt of rival dead and a monumental scale.
- Ermita's triangular plot and its mix of cinema, shops and tiny flats is a compact, vertical, stealth-friendly building type.

### Gaps
- I did not verify specific 1920s deco cinemas in the centre (e.g. Cine Olimpia, Teatro Lírico); Commons categories were not found for them.
- The unfinished Legislative Palace's exposed steel dome (1910s-1933) is widely photographed, but I did not open a source describing it. Search the Casasola/INAH Mediateca for "Palacio Legislativo".
- Edificio Basurto (1940-45) and Cine Ópera (1949) are outside the 1930 cutoff and are labelled as later.

## 5. Vernacular and everyday city: vecindades, pulquerías, cantinas, markets, azoteas, barrios, cemeteries, domes, street furniture

### Takeaway
The everyday fabric is courtyard tenements (vecindades) in old palaces, loudly painted pulquerías with absurd names, market quarters (La Merced, Lagunilla, Tepito) on old canal docks, flat azoteas full of water tanks and laundry, Garibaldi's mariachi cantinas, and 19th-century cemeteries with heroes' mausolea. These are the layers that make the city feel lived-in rather than monumental.

### Cited Findings
- **Vecindades**: subdivided elite houses in the historic centre, with rooms around a patio rented to families who shared lavatories and kitchens. Purpose-built ones copying the type appeared in the early 20th century. Outsiders stigmatised the residents; inside they formed a dense communal web — [Wikipedia: Vecindad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vecindad). The vecindad, copied from the centre's 17th-century tenements, was common in Tepito by the 20th century, a "barrio bravo" without services until 1929 — [es.Wikipedia: Tepito](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tepito). Images: Tepito [38] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tepito>; Courtyards <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Courtyards_in_Mexico_City>
- By the 1980s many of the Centro's old mansions were "abandoned or turned into tenements for the poor" — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)
- **Pulquerías** were inside and out "decorated with vibrant, gaudy murals (commonly of the Virgin of Guadalupe)" and had "extravagant names" taken from opera (*Norma*, *La Traviata*), literature (*Don Quixote*, *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame*) and military glory ("The Great Napoleon"). They were tied to music, gambling, fighting and crime; drunk customers slept in the entryway; the state treated them as a threat — [Wikipedia: Pulqueria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulquer%C3%ADa). Images [6] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pulquer%C3%ADas>
- In 1926 Diego Rivera published two essays in *Mexican Folkways* praising pulquería exterior paintings, illustrated with Edward Weston photographs — [Princeton research portal](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-last-word-in-direct-naive-realism-diego-rivera-edward-weston-/)
- **Plaza Garibaldi** (formerly Plaza Santa Cecilia; renamed 1920). The Salón Tenampa became the home of mariachi in the city in the 1920s — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_Garibaldi). Images <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Plaza_Garibaldi>; cantina background <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantina>
- **La Merced** has been commercial since early colonial times, when traders arrived from across New Spain. A permanent market was decided in the 1860s on the grounds of the La Merced monastery. In the early 20th century it was the city's main wholesaler, and it is known for street prostitution — [Wikipedia: La Merced Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Merced_Market). Images [77] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Merced_Market,_Mexico_City>; Markets in Mexico City [70] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Markets_in_Mexico_City>; Lagunilla <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Lagunilla>
- **Azoteas**: flat roofs used for hanging laundry and holding tinacos (water tanks), cisterns and lightning rods — [es.Wikipedia: Azotea](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azotea). Images: Water tanks in Mexico <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Water_tanks_in_Mexico>
- **Cemeteries**: **Panteón de San Fernando** (in use 1832-1872) is one of the city's oldest surviving cemeteries and a key example of 19th-century funerary art, holding Benito Juárez and Ignacio Zaragoza. Before it, burial was inside churches — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pante%C3%B3n_de_San_Fernando). Images [66] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pante%C3%B3n_de_San_Fernando,_Mexico_City>. **Panteón de Dolores** (1875; largest in Mexico, about 700,000 tombs) [20] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pante%C3%B3n_Civil_de_Dolores_Cemetery,_Mexico_City>. **Panteón Jardín** [38] <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pante%C3%B3n_Jard%C3%ADn,_Mexico_City>. Overview: Cemeteries in Mexico City <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cemeteries_in_Mexico_City>
- **Street furniture and transport**: gas and electric lighting and streetcars by the early 1900s — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City). Images: Trams <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Trams_in_Mexico_City>; Street lights <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Street_lights_in_Mexico_City>; Kiosco Morisco (bandstand) <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Kiosco_Morisco>
- **Garitas** (city toll gates at the city edge, e.g. the Garita de la Viga where canal traffic entered) — [UAM paper search summary](https://fuenteshumanisticas.azc.uam.mx/index.php/rfh/article/download/928/907)

### Inferences
- Vecindad = a palace corpse. A noble courtyard house with its rooms rented out one by one gives a natural stealth hub: one zaguán gate, a patio with shared basins and laundry lines, galleries on two levels, and stairs up to an azotea that connects to the neighbours' roofs.
- Pulquería façades are the most "Grim Fandango" element in the real record: hand-painted murals, saints and absurd grandiose names. Use them as readable landmarks and wayfinding.
- Church-floor burials before 1832, followed by the walled panteones, support crypts under every church as a plausible network.

### Gaps
- I found no strong sourced description of 1920s cantina interiors (swinging doors, spittoons, bans on women) beyond general claims; the English Cantina article is thin.
- Lagunilla market history (iron market halls of the 1900s?) was not verified; the Commons "Lagunilla" category may mix other places.
- I found no sourced material on tiled church domes as a building type in Mexico City (beyond the Old Basilica's Talavera dome) or on 1920s lamp-post designs.

## 6. Stealth-friendly level fit and Alternate-universe hooks

### Takeaway
The best real precedents for a dense stealth level are the colonial courtyard block (palace turned vecindad) with linked azoteas, the canal-and-chinampa edge (Viga/Xochimilco), the panopticon (Lecumberri), the iron-and-glass hall (Chopo, Palacio de Hierro), the sinking church with crypts, and the cemetery. The strongest AU premise grounded in fact is that the Desagüe failed: the 1629 flood never receded, and the 1900 Gran Canal was never finished or was sabotaged.

### Cited Findings
- The 1629 flood stayed for years, most people fled, buildings collapsed, and the crown proposed moving the capital — [Wikipedia: Desagüe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desag%C3%BCe); [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)
- The east side of the plaza was built first because it was firmer; buildings there had "the lake's waters up against the walls" — [Wikipedia: History of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico_City)
- Lecumberri's panopticon let one guard watch all prisoners without them knowing when they were watched — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Lecumberri)
- Calimaya's cannon gargoyles could be fired with permission of the city, and an Aztec serpent head is its cornerstone — [Wikipedia: Museum of Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Mexico_City)
- The Casa Boker excavation found Aztec sculptures, and the Gran Canal excavation found the prehistoric Tequixquiac sacrum — [es.Wikipedia: Casa Boker](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_Boker); [es.Wikipedia: Gran Canal](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Canal_del_Desag%C3%BCe_%28Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico%29)
- The scribes ("evangelistas") under the Santo Domingo arcades wrote letters for the illiterate, next to the old Inquisition palace — [es.Wikipedia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Santo_Domingo_%28Ciudad_de_M%C3%A9xico%29)
- Island of the Dolls: a chinampa covered in hanging dolls, reached by a long canal ride — [Wikipedia: Xochimilco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xochimilco)

### Inferences (Alternate-universe hooks)
**Stealth suitability ranking (designer judgement from the facts above):**
1. **Courtyard block / vecindad in a colonial palace**: zaguán chokepoint, two-level arcaded galleries, a patio with basins, interconnected azoteas with tinacos and laundry for cover. High verticality, many routes.
2. **Canal quarter (Viga-style)**: towpath, garita toll gate, trajinera traffic as moving cover, ahuejote willow screens, chinampa islets, and low bridges you can swim under.
3. **Sinking church + crypt**: below-grade nave, tilted floors (a physics and readability hook), church-floor burials, the Sagrario/sacristy as a vault.
4. **Panopticon (Lecumberri)**: a star of cell wings around a tower. A pure "watcher" level; the archive future suggests cells full of documents.
5. **Iron-and-glass halls (Chopo, Palacio de Hierro, Kiosco Morisco)**: lattice climbing, light pooling through glass at night, a natural history museum with a dinosaur skeleton.
6. **Cemeteries (San Fernando, Dolores, Francés)**: mausolea as cover, walled gardens, ironwork.
7. **Deco tower (La Nacional / Ermita)**: setback terraces as stepping ledges, a triangular plan, a cinema to infiltrate.

**Alternate-universe hooks (grounded in the facts above):**
- **The lake was never drained.** Pearson's Gran Canal failed in 1900 (or a 1629-style flood returned and stayed). The 1920s city is a canal city again: Porfirian avenues are half-flooded boulevards, trajineras serve as trams, and the Canal de la Viga is never paved. Ground floors are abandoned, life moves up to the piano nobile and the azoteas (good for rooftop routes), and the garitas are canal tolls with excise guards.
- **The city sinks unevenly, quarter by quarter.** Use the real numbers (Bellas Artes down 4 m; more than 12 m regional drop in under a century) at fantasy speed. Churches lean like Loreto and the Santísima, and doors open onto steps going down. A "drowned floor" layer of streets lies under streets, with old façades visible below new pavement. Each district has a different tilt and floor level, which suits verticality and leaning-geometry puzzles.
- **Deco over the Templo Mayor.** La Nacional is already said to be inspired by the Templo Mayor, and the real Templo Mayor was rebuilt six times, each shell over the last. Build a deco skyscraper as the seventh shell, with the older pyramid stages reachable as inner levels. Foundations hit idols, as at Casa Boker (decapitated eagle) and the Gran Canal (carved sacrum).
- **Palaces with Aztec cornerstones.** Make Calimaya's serpent head a city-wide rule: every great house has a buried idol at its corner. Gargoyles that are real cannons, fired only "with permission of the city", suggest a ward-permit bureaucracy for the occult.
- **The Holy Office never closed (it really closed in 1820).** An AU Inquisition still sits on the esquina chata of Santo Domingo, with the scribes' arcade next door as an information market where letters are written and copied for spies.
- **Lecumberri as the Black Palace**: an occult panopticon whose central eye is literal, with an archive of confiscated documents in the cells.
- **The Monumento as an unfinished cage.** In the 1920s the Legislative Palace stands as a stalled Porfirian skeleton (it was halted in 1910s reality). In the AU it is a ruined parliament dome turned shrine-tomb where enemies are buried together.
- **The pulquería as the guild hall of folk magic**: painted saint-murals as wards, opera-titled names as passwords, Rivera-style folk art as signage.
- **Imported flat-pack buildings**: iron pavilions bought from world's fairs (Kiosco Morisco, Chopo) let the city "import" strange foreign structures, such as a German glass cathedral, holding a Diplodocus, reassembled by a cult.
- **Estridentópolis made real**: the Estridentistas' "absurd, vertiginous" radio-age city exists as an avant-garde district of antennas, manifestos and loudspeakers. It is the clearest period-authentic source for a stylised, "other-world" 1920s Mexican modernity.
- **The Island of the Dolls principle applied to chinampa outskirts**: offerings, ahuejote willows and hanging effigies along the canals as folkloric horror markers.
