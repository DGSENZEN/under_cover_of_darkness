## 1. Stone and wall finishes (tezontle, chiluca/cantera, basalt, adobe, lime plaster and lime-wash colours, brick, sinking/cracked walls, salt-petre, peeling paint)

### Takeaway
The signature street look of viceregal Mexico City has three parts:
- Walls faced in blood-red or black porous tezontle.
- Door and window frames, cornices and corner stones in grey-white chiluca or other cantera.
- Lime-plastered and lime-washed rubble or brick everywhere else.

The neoclassical 19th century plastered much of this over. In the 1920s nationalist "neocolonial" revival, tezontle was put back on show. On top of every surface sits the city's ruin layer: tilted, sinking buildings on the old lake bed, rising damp with white salt bloom at the base of walls, and layered peeling paint.

### Cited Findings
**Tezontle (red/black volcanic scoria)**
- Tezontle is "a porous, highly oxidized, volcanic rock", usually reddish from iron oxide, and described as well-cemented, agglomeritic and scoriaceous. It is light, which helped keep buildings from sinking into the unstable lake bed. Buildings with tezontle façades include the Metropolitan Cathedral, the National Palace, the Nacional Monte de Piedad, the Church of San Bernardo and the House of the Count de la Torre de Cossío — [Wikipedia: Tezontle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezontle)
- Tezontle has been used in construction for at least 20 centuries, back to Teotihuacan, and "can be considered the most representative building stone of Mexico" — [IUGS Geoheritage: Tezontle volcanic scoria](https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=11830)
- In the viceroyalty it served as visible facing and also as fill for vaults, because it is light. Chroniclers called it a very fine reddish stone, unique in the world and easy to work. Door and window frames used the heavier chiluca instead — [El Universal, "El tezontle desde la arquitectura virreinal a la neocolonial"](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/el-tezontle-desde-la-arquitectura-virreinal-la-neocolonial/)
- **Decline and revival (directly relevant to a 1920s setting):**
  - The San Carlos Academy (founded 1781) imposed Neoclassicism and condemned the Baroque. Architects hid tezontle under plaster and cantera veneers, and Porfirian brick and French taste pushed it further aside.
  - After the Revolution, a 1917–1920 tax exemption encouraged buildings in viceregal style.
  - The Palacio Nacional's third storey (1926–28, architect Augusto Petricioli) was added with a tezontle façade. Later examples are the INEGI building on Balderas (1938, **[post-1930]**) and the Hotel Virreyes (1947, **[post-1930]**).
  - Quarries: Peñón de los Baños, Tepetzinco, Cerro de la Estrella, Santa Martha and the Sierra de Santa Catarina. The stone comes in reddish and black tones.
  - Source for all of the above: [El Universal (same article)](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/el-tezontle-desde-la-arquitectura-virreinal-la-neocolonial/)
- The Tlaxcala House in the historic centre has most of its façade in "blood-red" tezontle, with chiluca, "a grayish white stone", framing the windows and doors — [Wikipedia: Tlaxcala House, Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlaxcala_House,_Mexico_City)
- Photo reference: [Commons: Category:Tezontle](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tezontle)

**Cantera and chiluca (quarried tuffs/andesites)**
- Cantera is a quarried volcanic rock from compacted ash. It is porous and light, carves in fine detail, and its colour varies by region with mineral impurities; Oaxaca cantera, for example, is green. It has "stood for centuries" in cathedrals and haciendas. The name means "quarry" — [Wikipedia: Cantera (stone)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantera_(stone)). Photos: [Commons: Category:Cantera](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cantera)
- The pairing of tezontle and cantera is "characteristic" of Mexico City buildings, for example the former San Felipe Neri church façade — [Wikipedia: Mexican Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Baroque)

**Construction, masonry and decay**
- Historic Mexican buildings of the 16th–19th centuries were built of local stone ashlar or clay brick laid in lime mortar. Some historic-centre properties were built of courses of tezontle, adobe and brick where money was short. Humidity drives mineral salts into porous masonry, where they crystallise. Damage concentrates in the lower parts of exterior walls: micro-flaking, scaling and patina. Missing waterproofing and drainage made it worse. Sources (Spanish academic papers on CDMX historic masonry, surfaced via search): [Redalyc PDF](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/4276/427639589004.pdf); [INAH, El Correo del Restaurador](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/elcorreodelrestaurador/article/download/21217/22612/44410)
- General efflorescence reference: [Wikipedia: Efflorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efflorescence); photos: [Commons: Category:Efflorescence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Efflorescence); peeling paint: [Commons: Category:Peeling paint](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Peeling_paint)
- Lime plaster and lime wash as materials: [Wikipedia: Lime plaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_plaster). Adobe: [Wikipedia: Adobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe); [Commons: Category:Adobe in Mexico](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Adobe_in_Mexico)

**Sinking and tilting**
- Parts of the metropolitan area have sunk by as much as 27 ft since the late 19th century. Because the sinking is uneven, buildings in the colonial centre are damaged. The Metropolitan Cathedral (begun 1573) and other monuments are "visibly tilted". The 17th-century Holy Trinity church is "listing to the side and sinking faster than the surrounding streets" — [OPB/AP, 2026-05-01](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/05/01/mexico-city-is-sinking-so-quickly-it-can-be-seen-from-space/)
- The cause: the Spaniards built on Aztec Tenochtitlan's lake island and drained the lake; groundwater pumping has made it worse for more than a century — [OPB/AP (same)](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/05/01/mexico-city-is-sinking-so-quickly-it-can-be-seen-from-space/)

**Pigments for walls (añil, almagre, ochre, cochineal)**
- Façade pigments documented in Spanish/colonial building practice are albayalde (lead white/ivory), açofaira (gold), almagra (red earth) and prieto (shadow/black). Cochineal red and añil (indigo) blue were the two standout Mesoamerican colourants. Cochineal and indigo were among New Spain's largest exports, behind silver. FTIR has found indigo and cochineal on painted wooden ceilings and choirs of five Jesuit mission churches in northern Mexico (1674–1723) — [UPV RiuNet: Pre-hispanic pigments and Italian renaissance designs at Spanish colonial missions](https://riunet.upv.es/entities/publication/4eb20e7d-bff6-479d-9f0f-c583aff3e40f) (summary via search; full paper not read)
- Añil comes from *Indigofera suffruticosa* (Nahuatl *xiquilite*) — [Wikipedia: Indigofera suffruticosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigofera_suffruticosa); cochineal — [Wikipedia: Cochineal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochineal); Maya blue (pre-Hispanic indigo–clay pigment) — [Wikipedia: Maya blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_blue)
- 39 years of excavation in the Centro Histórico have recorded only red, ochre, blue, white and black in the pre-Hispanic palette, almost all inorganic — [INAH Templo Mayor exhibition labels PDF](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/images/templomayor/20170807_cedulas_ok.pdf) (from search summary)

### Inferences
- **Tezontle as a texture.**
  - Look: a vesicular (pitted), very rough, oxblood-to-rust red stone with black specks. A black variety exists.
  - How it is laid in Mexico City: on palace façades as roughly squared or irregular blocks set flush in pale lime mortar, giving a "red field with pale veins". Every opening is framed by smooth, finely carved grey chiluca.
  - Texture plan: one tiling red-scoria wall map plus a separate grey trim set (plain jambs, mouldings, corner quoins).
  - Weathering: pores collect black soot and grime, so the stone darkens to maroon at street level and near drips. Rain darkens it strongly.
- **Chiluca/cantera.** Look: pale grey to pinkish-grey and fine-grained, so it can carry Baroque relief. Weathering: black crusts in sheltered hollows of reliefs, rounded edges, and pale streaks under cornices where rain runs off. For a PS2-style look, bake this dirt into the albedo (sheltered = darker).
- **Lime plaster (aplanado).** Look: a hand-trowelled, slightly wavy surface. The wash colours sit in thin, chalky, patchy layers that fade unevenly. The base 1 m or so of the wall is often a darker band (guardapolvo/rodapié). Weathering: where the plaster falls away (aplanado caído), the substrate shows through: brick, tezontle rubble or adobe. A "plaster over masonry, spalled" family of textures is therefore the workhorse of the level.
- **Salt-petre.** Should read as a white crystalline tide line at roughly 0.3–1.2 m, with blistered and scaling paint above it and darker damp below. Use a decal or vertex-colour layer rather than separate textures.
- **Sinking.** The gothic-horror angle writes itself: tilting churches, door frames out of square, floors sloping toward the cathedral, cracked lintels. In a low-poly game this is a modelling trick (skewed meshes and doors) supported by diagonal crack decals.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found with exact historical lime-wash recipes or named colour charts for 18th-century Mexico City (as opposed to Puebla or general Spanish practice). The Puebla INAH colour catalogue link that turned up returned 404.
- "Rosa mexicano" as a façade colour before 1930 is not supported: the name dates from 1949 (see section 5).
- Basalt use in CDMX (paving, foundations, pre-Hispanic sculpture) was not specifically sourced in this pass.

## 2. Tile (Talavera poblana, azulejo façades, tiled domes, mosaico de pasta, terrazzo, deco mosaics)

### Takeaway
Talavera poblana is the defining ceramic. It is tin-glazed earthenware from Puebla, painted mostly in cobalt blue on white, with a licensed palette of blue, yellow, black, green, orange and mauve. It covers whole façades (Casa de los Azulejos, 1737), church domes, kitchens and the 1920s street-name plaques. Porfirian and 1920s houses add unfired, hydraulic-pressed cement tiles (mosaico de pasta) on the floors. Deco buildings add terrazzo and stained glass.

### Cited Findings
**Talavera poblana**
- Tin-glazed earthenware was made in Mexico City by the mid-16th century. From the early 17th century Puebla became the main centre. The blue-and-white wares were inspired by Chinese porcelain that came on the Manila galleons — [Met Heilbrunn Timeline: Talavera de Puebla](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/talavera-de-puebla) (via search summary; fetch rate-limited)
- Puebla made high-quality ware by 1550 and was the centre of production by 1580. 1650–1750 was the "Golden Age". The 1653 guild ordinances reserved cobalt blue for the finest pieces — [Library of Congress Law blog: Talavera ceramics](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2023/09/talavera-ceramics-at-the-intersection-of-art-history-and-law-in-mexico/)
- Permitted colours are blue, yellow, black, green, orange and mauve, all from natural pigments. The 1653 ordinances set grades: fine, semi-fine and everyday (común). Influences are Chinese (blue), Hispano-Moresque, Italian (18th-century palettes) and indigenous.
  - Decline: after independence only seven of 46 workshops survived, under competition from cheap English wares.
  - Revival: the Catalan Enrique Luis Ventosa arrived in 1897 and in 1922 worked with potter Ysauro Uriarte Martínez on new designs that blended pre-Columbian and Art Nouveau motifs. This is a ready-made 1920s "neo-prehispanic Talavera".
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Talavera pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talavera_pottery)
- The designation of origin covers San Pablo del Monte (Tlaxcala) and the districts of Atlixco, Puebla, Cholula and Tecali. The processes were added to UNESCO's Intangible Heritage list in 2019 — [LoC Law blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2023/09/talavera-ceramics-at-the-intersection-of-art-history-and-law-in-mexico/)
- Process and history from a working workshop: [Google Arts & Culture: Uriarte Talavera, process of making](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/-QWxdJXfSNivTg); [Uriarte Talavera](https://www.uriartetalavera.com.mx)
- Single-tile object reference: [LACMA: Tile with Hunter (Azulejo con cazador)](https://collections.lacma.org/node/2109477); [Met: Barrel-shaped Flower Pot](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/784873)
- Photos: [Commons: Category:Talavera de Puebla](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Talavera_de_Puebla); [Commons: Category:Azulejos in Mexico](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Azulejos_in_Mexico)

**Azulejo façades and petatillo**
- **Casa de los Azulejos (CDMX):**
  - In 1737 the Countess had the palace's stonework and façade covered with Puebla azulejos, blue and white. Diego Durán Berruecos tiled the arches, columns, skirting, doors, window cornices and balustrades.
  - Stone-carved window and door frames.
  - French porcelain crowns on the courtyard columns and a fountain crowned with mosaics.
  - Copper courtyard railings, some made in China.
  - The staircase mural "Omniscience" by Orozco, **1925**.
  - A stained-glass courtyard roof added in the 20th century.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia: Casa de los Azulejos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos); photos: [Commons: Category:Casa de los Azulejos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_de_los_Azulejos)
- Puebla's "petatillo" façades combine Talavera with brick pieces in a woven, mat-like pattern. They are part of the World Heritage site's Outstanding Universal Value — [UNESCO international assistance page](https://whc.unesco.org/en/intassistance/3247%2F) (search summary). Puebla's Casa de los Muñecos has a Talavera mosaic façade — [Wikipedia: Mexican Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Baroque)
- INAH publication on Talavera in historic buildings: ["A tradition for conservation: the use of talavera in antique buildings" (INAH Mediateca)](https://www.mediateca.inah.gob.mx/islandora_74/islandora/object/libro%3A915) (did not respond to the link check; may be slow)

**Tiled domes and cupolas**
- Around Puebla, scores of churches have tile-covered domes and towers. In the 18th century Puebla interiors and exteriors were tiled on a scale unequalled elsewhere — [UW digital collections, "Pottery of Mexico" text](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AHJ4Q2N6XJUX6U8F/text/AW4QWOJDW5QWC38T) (search summary)
- Puebla Cathedral's main cupola is tiled in yellow Talavera — [Architonic: Puebla Cathedral](https://www.architonic.com/en/pr/puebla-cathedral/5101078) (search summary)
- Santo Domingo, Puebla: the Capilla del Rosario was so covered in tile, gilded carving and painting that it was called the eighth wonder of the world — [famous-historic-buildings.org.uk](https://famous-historic-buildings.org.uk/church-of-santo-domingo-puebla.html)

**Cement tile (mosaico de pasta / mosaico hidráulico)**
- Cement tiles appeared in the 1850s in Catalonia and were shown at the 1867 Paris Exposition by Garret & Rivet.
  - Construction: a 3–4 mm colour layer (white Portland cement, marble powder, sand, mineral pigment) pressed into a mould onto a grey cement body by hydraulic press. They are **not fired and not glazed**. 20×20 cm is a typical size.
  - Wear: good tiles polish shinier, poor ones go dull. Hairline cracks come from differential shrinkage, and non-mineral pigments fade.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Cement tile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement_tile)
- In Mexico: "created in Europe in the last third of the 19th century, the mosaicos reached Mexico at the end of that century" for plazas, pavements, churches, civic monuments and houses — [Posta Yucatán: Mosaicos de pasta](https://www.posta.com.mx/yucatan/mosaicos-de-pasta-tesoro-yucateco-de-arte-y-tradicion-bajo-tus-pies/vl2072390). Monterrey had its first mosaic factory, "La Industrial", from the last third of the 19th century — [Nuevo León museum note (docx)](https://www.nl.gob.mx/sites/default/files/repositorio/Dependencias/3%20Museos/Noticias/20241204_04_museo_mosaico_industria.docx)
- Photo reference for Yucatán pasta floors, the best-documented Mexican tradition: [Yucatán Today: Yucatecan pasta tiles](https://yucatantoday.com/yucatecan-pasta-tiles-hand-crafted-beauty-from-yucatan/?lang=en); [Matador: pisos yucatecos](https://matadornetwork.com/es/la-gran-belleza-de-los-pisos-yucatecos/); [Commons: Category:Cement tiles](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cement_tiles); [Commons: Category:Encaustic tiles](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Encaustic_tiles)

**Terrazzo and deco glass**
- Edificio Ermita (1930–32, Juan Segura, **[borderline 1930]**) has a stained-glass roof (vidriera) attributed to Diego Rivera — [Mexico City tourism: Edificio Ermita](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/edificio-ermita); [Commons: Category:Edificio Ermita](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_Ermita)
- Terrazzo photo reference (generic, not Mexico-specific): [Commons: Category:Terrazzo floors](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Terrazzo_floors)

### Inferences
- **Talavera tile texture.**
  - Look: a slightly uneven white tin glaze (warm, faintly bluish or creamy, never pure white) with hand-brushed cobalt that pools darker at stroke ends. Yellow, orange and green come in for "polychrome" grades. Glaze crawls at the edges, and the tiles are slightly irregular in size with wide grout.
  - Weathering: chipped corners expose buff/pink earthenware, crazing fills with dirt, and whole tiles are missing (bare mortar socket).
  - Nearest-neighbour filtering suits this well if the tile is authored at low resolution with deliberate brush shapes.
- **Façade azulejo.** Usually a repeat of 4-tile rosette units, set diagonally or square, with stone or brick framing. Casa de los Azulejos is blue-white with stone trim.
- **Domes.** Read from the street at a distance. A tiled dome needs a radial-gore UV layout and a high-contrast pattern (yellow/blue/green chevrons or a lattice) so it reads at low resolution. A good landmark material.
- **Mosaico de pasta.** Matte, chalky colour with soft pattern edges (the brass divider leaves a slight bleed). Typical are a border tile plus a field tile. Wear concentrates along walking paths into a polished, faded, grey-showing track. Its unfired matte surface contrasts with glazed Talavera's specular, so the shaders should differ.
- **Petatillo** (brick + Talavera woven pattern) is a cheap, recognisable "Puebla-style" wall to borrow for the alt-universe city.

### Gaps
- No documented catalogue of specific Mexico City cement-tile pattern names or manufacturers (as opposed to Yucatán and Monterrey) was found.
- No Mexico City deco-mosaic primary source was found (e.g. specific lobby floors of La Nacional or Frontón México).
- The Met Talavera essay text was not fetched in full (HTTP 429), so its facts come from the search summary.

## 3. Wood and metal (carved doors, zaguán doors and knockers, celosías, herrería, iron-and-glass, bronze, gilded retablos with estofado, vigas, petates)

### Takeaway
Interiors run from bare to lavish:
- Bare end: the poor have vigas and petate mats.
- Middle: the bourgeois have courtyard houses with carved zaguán doors, wrought-iron balconies and grilles.
- Lavish end: the church has blazing gold-leaf retablos with estofado "brocade" polychromy.
- Porfirian end: imported iron-and-glass, marble and Florentine bronze.

### Cited Findings
- **Gilded retablos.**
  - The Altar of the Kings in the Metropolitan Cathedral was built and carved by Jerónimo de Balbás in 1736, a key estípite piece — [Wikipedia: Mexican Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Baroque); [Wikipedia: Altar of the Kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar_of_the_Kings)
  - Santo Domingo (Puebla, built 1572–1608) has a retablo of carved wood covered in gold leaf. The retablo at Yanhuitlán (Oaxaca, late 16th century) is gilded and painted wood with sculptures and panel paintings — [Getty: Retablo of the Church of Santo Domingo](https://www.getty.edu/projects/retablo-church-santo-domingo)
  - General: [Wikipedia: Retablo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retablo)
- **Estofado** imitates gold brocade (from Italian *stoffa*, fabric). It is used on wood sculpture and relief. In 1777 Spain's Royal Academy ordered polychrome wood replaced by marble or stone, which pushed estofado down to "popular" status — [Wikipedia: Estofado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estofado); photos: [Commons: Category:Estofado](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Estofado)
- **Vigas** are wooden beams that carry flat roofs from wall to wall; beam ends often project outside — [Wikipedia: Viga (architecture)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viga_(architecture)). Colonial houses used stone, lime plaster, brick, heavy timber, terracotta tile, wrought iron and carved wood. Ceilings were 4–6 m high and rooms were arranged around a central patio — [Cultural Daily: A guide to Mexican colonial home architecture](https://www.culturaldaily.com/a-guide-to-mexican-colonial-home-architecture/) (secondary/popular source)
- **Petates.**
  - Material: woven palm, tule, maguey or reed mats (Nahuatl *petlatl*).
  - Uses: rolled-up beds and sun-drying surfaces, and burial shrouds for the poor.
  - Sayings: "ya se petateó" means "he has died", and "llamarada de petate" means a brief flare.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Petate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petate); photos: [Commons: Category:Petate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Petate)
- **Herrería.** Iron rejas (window grilles), balcony railings and cancelas (inner grille gates) defined the ornament of 18th–19th-century domestic architecture in the Spanish world — [IAPH repository](https://repositorio.iaph.es/handle/11532/100648?locale=en) (search summary). UNAM thesis on ironwork (San Juan Diego chapel, CDMX): [UNAM PDF](https://ru.dgb.unam.mx/bitstream/20.500.14330/TES01000839323/3/0839323.pdf). Photos: [Commons: Category:Wrought iron balconies](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Wrought_iron_balconies)
- **Museo Franz Mayer** (CDMX decorative arts museum in a 16th-century building) has about 10,000 pieces, mostly Mexican colonial to 20th century. Among them are Talavera tiles (some from the Casa de los Azulejos), furniture and chests, and Villa Alta (Oaxaca) marquetry furniture with lace-like ironwork hardware — [Frommer's: Museo Franz Mayer](https://www.frommers.com/destinations/mexico-city/attractions/museo-franz-mayer); [CDMX Secreta](https://cdmxsecreta.com/museo-franz-mayer-cdmx/); museum: [franzmayer.org.mx](https://franzmayer.org.mx)
- **Porfirian iron, bronze and marble: Palacio Postal** (1902–1907, Adamo Boari). Mexican quarry stone and marble; staircases of Mexican marble. The ironwork of the main staircase, gates, windows and railings, and the ornamental bronze, came from the Fonderia Pignone, Florence. The style mixes Art Nouveau, Plateresque, Venetian Gothic and Moorish — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Correos de México](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Correos_de_M%C3%A9xico); [Commons: Category:Palacio Postal](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Palacio_Postal)
- **Copper railings** at the Casa de los Azulejos courtyard, partly made in China — [Wikipedia: Casa de los Azulejos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos)
- **Other Porfirian commercial reference:** [Commons: Category:Casa Boker](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_Boker); [Wikipedia: Palacio de Hierro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Hierro)

### Inferences
- **Zaguán door.** A double leaf, tall enough for a carriage, often with a small wicket door (postigo) cut into one leaf. It has studded or panelled cedar or mesquite boards, a bronze or iron knocker (aldaba/aldabón), and big strap hinges. Weathering: grey, sun-split wood with dark rot at the bottom rail. Iron nail heads leave rust bleed.
- **Herrería.** Cast shadows matter more than albedo. Model the grilles as geometry or alpha-tested cards with a simple flat black-to-rust texture. Rust streaks run down the plaster below every grille anchor, which is a good decal.
- **Gilded retablo.** In a PS2-style renderer, use a dark-gold albedo plus a specular or matcap trick and candle-flicker lighting. Estofado can be faked with a gold base under a red, blue or green painted pattern with scratched-through lines. Blackened gold in neglected, candle-smoked churches suits the gothic tone.
- **Vigas and petates.** Dark, smoke-stained beams with a lime-washed board or brick ceiling between (the "terrado" deck is my assumption, see Gaps). Petates: a light straw-coloured diagonal weave, good on floors, walls of poor rooms, and as a "body wrapped in a petate" prop.
- **Celosías.** Wooden lattice screens, Mudéjar-derived, seen on convent choirs. See Gaps.

### Gaps
- No specific source was found on celosías in Mexico City (convent grilles/lattices), on knocker typologies, or on the terrado roof construction (beams + boards + earth + lime). These come from general knowledge and are not sourced here.
- No CDMX-specific herrería study was read in full. The UNAM thesis is about later work.

## 4. Ornament and lettering (estípite, Churrigueresque, neo-prehispanic deco, rótulos and pulquería murals, papel picado, street signs, posters, cinema signage, ghost signs)

### Takeaway
There are three ornament layers to stack:
- **Colonial Churrigueresque.** Estípite pilasters (inverted tapered pyramids), heavy relief and gilded retablos.
- **The 1920s nationalist turn.** Neocolonial tezontle, neo-prehispanic/Aztec-Maya deco (Frontón México 1929, La Nacional 1929–32, Amabilis's Seville pavilion 1929), and Ventosa–Uriarte's pre-Columbian Talavera.
- **Popular street graphics.** Hand-painted rótulos, garish pulquería murals and names, Talavera street-name plaques put up in the 1920s, Posada's calavera broadsides and papel picado.

### Cited Findings
**Churrigueresque and estípite**
- Lorenzo Rodríguez originated Mexican Churrigueresque. His masterpiece is the Sagrario Metropolitano (1749–69), whose estípite pilasters were copied all over New Spain — [Britannica: Lorenzo Rodríguez](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/506681); [Wikipedia: Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque)
- The Sagrario's south façade has grapes and pomegranates (the Blood of Christ and the Church), apostles, saints, martyrs, biblical scenes and animal reliefs — [Wikipedia: Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque) (search summary)
- Two phases: Salomónico (mid-17th century, twisted columns), then Estípite (early 18th century) — [Wikipedia: Mexican Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Baroque)
- Photos: [Commons: Category:Estipite](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Estipite); [Commons: Category:Churrigueresque architecture](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Churrigueresque_architecture); [Commons: Sagrario East Facade](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Sagrario_Metropolitano_(Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Mexico_City)_-_East_Facade); [Commons: Metropolitan Cathedral of Mexico City](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Metropolitan_Cathedral_of_Mexico_City); Tepotzotlán (the other estípite masterpiece): [Commons: Category:Tepotzotlán](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tepotzotl%C3%A1n)
- Polychrome stucco relief (argamasa) from Puebla and Tonantzintla, the "indigenous Baroque" with angels, fruit and faces — [Wikipedia: Mexican Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Baroque); [Wikipedia: Santa María Tonantzintla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Mar%C3%ADa_Tonantzintla)

**Neo-prehispanic deco (1920s)**
- **Frontón México** opened 10 May 1929 (architects Teodoro Kunhardt and Joaquín Capilla). It has vertical projections and recesses along a long street wall, and reliefs with pre-Hispanic themes over the two corner entrances — [Mexico City tourism: Frontón México](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/fronton-mexico/?lang=en); [Commons: Category:Frontón México](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Front%C3%B3n_M%C3%A9xico)
- **Edificio La Nacional** (Av. Juárez, 1929–1932) is Art Deco, with ziggurat setbacks that echo stepped pyramids — [Wikipedia: Edificio La Nacional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_La_Nacional); [Commons: Category:Edificio La Nacional](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Edificio_La_Nacional)
- Art Deco absorbed Aztec and Maya themes, which matched the post-revolutionary aim of a modern nation proud of its pre-Columbian past — [Untapped Cities: 10 Art Deco gems of Mexico City](https://untappedcities.com/10-art-deco-architectural-gems-of-mexico-city)
- **Manuel Amabilis** designed the Mexican pavilion for the 1929 Seville Ibero-American Exposition (neo-Maya) and the Rendón Peniche sanatorium in Mérida (1919) with Maya-inspired arches and serpent patterns — [Archiseek: 1929 Mexico Pavilion, Seville](https://www.archiseek.com/1929-ibero-american-exposition-mexico-pavilion-seville-andalusia-spain/); [Mid City Beat: Mérida's Neo-Maya architecture](https://midcitybeat.com/artandculture/exploring-meridas-neo-maya-architecture-a-journey-through-time/)
- Ventosa and Uriarte's 1922 Talavera designs blended pre-Columbian and Art Nouveau elements — [Wikipedia: Talavera pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talavera_pottery)
- Cinema palaces: the Cine Olimpia opened in 1921 with 4,000 seats, ballrooms and a Wurlitzer organ, and showed *The Jazz Singer* (1927) — [Untapped Cities](https://untappedcities.com/10-art-deco-architectural-gems-of-mexico-city) (search summary). Scholarly study: ["Los palacios cinematográficos de la Ciudad de México", Apuntes (Javeriana)](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/revApuntesArq/article/view/26395/22317); [Architectuul: FOMA 18, Mexican cinema spaces](https://architectuul.com/digest/foma-18-building-up-the-screen-the-rise-and-fall-of-the-mexican-cinema-spaces)
- Photos: [Commons: Category:Art Deco architecture in Mexico](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Art_Deco_architecture_in_Mexico)
- **[post-1930]** Edificio Basurto (1940–45, Francisco J. Serrano) — [Wikipedia: Basurto Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basurto_Building). Edificio Ermita (1930–32) — see section 2.

**Rótulos and pulquerías**
- Rótulos are hand-painted signs on buildings that describe a business or advertise. They come out of 19th-century sign painting and predate 1930s Muralism. They are still common in working-class neighbourhoods — [Wikipedia: Rotulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotulo)
- **Pulquerías:**
  - By 1900 there were nearly a thousand in Mexico City. This is when they took on elaborate décor and outlandish names.
  - Murals inside and out, often of the Virgin of Guadalupe. Huge lettered names next to landscapes, dancers, mariachis and agave.
  - After the Revolution they were gathering points for artists.
  - In the early 1940s the city banned pulquería murals and closed bars for "immoral" names and "inappropriate" murals **[post-1930 regulation; the 1920s are the high point]**.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Pulquería](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulqueria) (search summary)
- La Nuclear pulquería murals show Mayahuel (agave goddess) and the Virgin of Guadalupe with drinking monks. That pairing of Catholic and Aztec imagery is excellent for folk-horror — [HistoricalMX: Pulquerías in Mexico City](https://historicalmx.org/items/show/93) (modern establishment; mural date not given)
- The whitewashing of rótulos in modern CDMX (context): [Zócalo Public Square / ASU](https://live-zocalopublicsquare.ws.asu.edu/?p=128655) (returned 404 when fetched; may be moved); [Yucatán Living: the dying art of rótulos](https://yucatanliving.com/posts/rotulos-painting-mexican-art)
- Photos: [Commons: Category:Pulquerías](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pulquer%C3%ADas); [Commons: Category:Pulque](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pulque)

**Street signs**
- The 1920s city government put Talavera tile plaques on street corners giving the **old colonial street names**. Around 1921 (the independence centenary) most street names were changed, many to Latin American countries (Brasil, Chile, Argentina). Colonial names came from trades and legends (e.g. Plateros, the silversmiths). Restoration teams surveyed 19 streets for plaques surviving from **1896** — [Fox News/AP: Reminders of centuries past emerge along Mexico City streets](https://www.foxnews.com/world/reminders-of-centuries-past-emerge-along-mexico-city-streets); [La Jornada column](https://www.jornada.com.mx/2018/10/28/opinion/030a1cap)
- Photos: [Commons: Category:Street signs in Mexico City](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Street_signs_in_Mexico_City)

**Posada, calaveras, broadsides**
- Most of José Guadalupe Posada's calaveras were published by Antonio Vanegas Arroyo's press. It printed cheap literature, thousands of satirical broadsides, chapbooks and games. One collection holds 506 such items — [USC archives subject](https://archives.usc.edu/subjects/4458); [Berkeley Library: Print Art of José Guadalupe Posada](https://exhibits.lib.berkeley.edu/spotlight/art-of-posada); [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/product/the-artistic-purgatory/); [NYPL Digital Collections example](https://rp-digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/e937eec0-2f1d-0138-bc36-4fb19236443c); [Commons: Category:José Guadalupe Posada](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jos%C3%A9_Guadalupe_Posada); [Wikipedia: La Calavera Catrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Calavera_Catrina)

**Papel picado**
- San Salvador Huixcolotla (Puebla) is considered its birthplace; Puebla made it the official "cradle" on 22 Sept 1998. The tradition was established in Puebla by the early 1800s. The tissue paper is "papel de China", which came via the Asia trade. By the 1920s Huixcolotla artisans were making cut-paper banners for markets and festivals — [Mexican Folk Art Guide: Papel picado](https://www.mexican-folk-art-guide.com/papel-picado.html); [Wikipedia: Papel picado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papel_picado); [Commons: Category:Papel picado](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Papel_picado)

**Muralism**
- Muralism starts in the 1920s. Orozco's "Omniscience" (1925) at the Casa de los Azulejos is an in-period mural inside a colonial house — [Wikipedia: Casa de los Azulejos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos); [Wikipedia: Mexican muralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_muralism)

### Inferences
- **Rótulo lettering** for the game should be hand-drawn rather than font-perfect:
  - Fat outlined serif and slab capitals, drop shadows, banners, pointing hands.
  - Two or three flat colours on lime wash, faded in sun and running at the bottoms of strokes.
  - Over time they become **ghost signs**: older rótulos bleed through newer lime wash. This "palimpsest wall" is a cheap, lore-rich texture (overlay a decal at 20–40% opacity).
- **Pulquería** façades are the ideal place for the game's folk-occult voice: absurd names, Mayahuel-meets-Guadalupe murals, agave and maguey motifs, saloon doors and coloured paper. Authoring them as unique hand-painted textures is worth it. They are landmarks.
- **Talavera street plaques.** A small blue-and-white tile panel with the old name ("Calle de ..."), mounted at second-storey corners. Great diegetic wayfinding for an investigation game, and the 1921 renaming gives a lore hook: two names for every street.
- **Neo-prehispanic deco** suits the Deadlock-like occult alt-1920s direction. Use stepped-fret (xicalcoliuhqui) friezes, serpent heads, ziggurat setbacks, sun discs and glyph-panel reliefs in cast stone or concrete, plus metal grilles with Aztec frets. Model these as trim sheets.
- **Estípite** should be a reusable modular mesh plus a relief normal/albedo trim. At PS2 polycounts, a single extruded "inverted tapered pyramid" stack with painted relief reads well.
- **Papel picado.** Bright translucent tissue (alpha-tested, double-sided). At night it backlights against lamps. Good for markets, cantinas and Día de Muertos.

### Gaps
- No primary 1920s sources were found on **enamel street signs** in Mexico City. Whether the 1920s name plates (as opposed to the Talavera historical plaques) were enamel, painted tin or tile is unconfirmed.
- No source was found on **cinema signage** (marquees, lettering, neon arrival dates) or **period posters** (cinema carteles mostly date from the 1930s–50s golden age, **[post-1930]**, unverified).
- Detailed pulquería lettering styles and named painters of the 1910s–20s were not found in this pass. Wikipedia's Rótulo article has little history.
- Mixtec codex glyph ornament used in 1920s deco: no specific CDMX building with Mixtec (rather than Aztec/Maya) motifs was confirmed.

## 5. Colour palettes (colonial towns, Porfirian interiors, 1920s deco, Día de Muertos; night/rain/lamplight)

### Takeaway
The period-true base palette is earthy:
- Tezontle oxblood and black.
- Chiluca grey.
- Lime white, and lime washes in red earth (almagre), ochres and indigo blue (añil).
- Cochineal reds.
- Gold in churches.
- Talavera's six glaze colours on top.

"Rosa mexicano" as a *named* colour is a 1949 invention, though the hot pink is pre-Hispanic. Día de Muertos supplies cempasúchil orange-gold. In the 1920s the city's street lamps had recently gone from gas to electric (1896–1905 transition). Wet volcanic stone and warm lamplight are the defining night read.

### Cited Findings
- **Colonial pigments.**
  - Spanish façade pigments: albayalde (white), açofaira (gold), almagra (red earth), prieto (black/shadow).
  - Mesoamerican stars: cochineal red and añil blue, which were huge exports — [UPV RiuNet paper](https://riunet.upv.es/entities/publication/4eb20e7d-bff6-479d-9f0f-c583aff3e40f)
  - Pre-Hispanic finds in the Centro Histórico are limited to red, ochre, blue, white and black — [INAH Templo Mayor PDF](https://www.templomayor.inah.gob.mx/images/templomayor/20170807_cedulas_ok.pdf)
- **Talavera glaze palette:** blue, yellow, black, green, orange, mauve — [Wikipedia: Talavera pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talavera_pottery)
- **Rosa mexicano.**
  - The colour belongs to the pre-Hispanic "chichiltic" group of fiery colours and appears on serapes and in folk art.
  - The *name* was popularised by Ramón Valdiosera, who presented the shade in New York in 1949. It later became prominent through Luis Barragán **[post-1930]**.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia: Mexican pink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_pink); [La Razón](https://www.razon.com.mx/virales/2024/05/18/conoce-la-curiosa-historia-detras-del-origen-del-color-rosa-mexicano/); [Commons: Category:Mexican pink](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Mexican_pink)
- **Cempasúchil** (*Tagetes erecta*): a yellow-orange marigold. The Florentine Codex (16th century) records its use in two Aztec feasts of the dead. It is used in garlands, ofrendas and petal paths to guide souls — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/10/30/1050726374/why-marigolds-or-cempasuchil-are-the-iconic-flower-of-dia-de-los-muertos); [Wikipedia: Tagetes erecta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagetes_erecta); [Commons: Category:Cempasúchil](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cempas%C3%BAchil); [Wikipedia: Day of the Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead)
- **Porfirian interior palette:** Mexican marble staircases, Florentine bronze, mixed Art Nouveau/Gothic/Moorish ornament (Palacio Postal, 1907) — [Wikipedia: Palacio de Correos de México](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Correos_de_M%C3%A9xico)
- **Street lighting:** 1896–1905 was the transition from gas to electric public lighting in Mexico City (Compañía Mexicana de Gas y Luz Eléctrica and competitors). A doctoral thesis covers lighting provision 1881–1938 — [Letras Históricas / Biblat abstract](https://biblat.unam.mx/en/revista/letras-historicas/articulo/de-empresa-de-alumbrado-a-empresa-de-servicio-de-energia-electrica-la-cmgle-en-la-ciudad-de-mexico-1896-1905); [Instituto Mora thesis PDF](https://mora.repositorioinstitucional.mx/jspui/bitstream/1018/457/1/MOR000104892%20nuevo%20problema.pdf)
- **Modern Puebla regulation:** a joint city–INAH colour catalogue for façades exists (2022). The link found returned 404 — noted only as evidence that such catalogues exist.

How the materials read at night and in rain:
- **Night (1920s).** Incandescent and arc lamps cast warm pools; moonlit sky fill is cool. Warm light makes ochre and almagre glow and turns tezontle near-black. Añil blue and Talavera cobalt sink into darkness except under direct lamps. This suggests leaning the night grade blue-green in shadow and amber in light pools.
- **Rain.** Porous tezontle and cantera darken sharply when wet. Use a wetness term that lowers albedo and adds specular. Glazed Talavera already has specular, so wet tiles read as mirrors of lamps. Lime wash streaks and runs. Rain is a good excuse for a dynamic "darken + spec" shader parameter.
- **Day.** High-altitude sun gives hard shadows and bleached lime wash. Use a slightly desaturated sky for the gothic mood.

### Gaps
- No documented measured palette (e.g. a spectrophotometric survey) of Mexico City historic façades was found. The only official catalogue found (Puebla 2022) was a dead link.
- No source was found on what kind of lamps lit 1920s CDMX streets (arc vs. incandescent proportions, colour temperature).
- Porfirian domestic interior colour schemes (wallpapers, painted ceilings) were not sourced. Only the Palacio Postal's materials were.

## 6. Sourcing: CC0 libraries, scans, museum open access, photo-reference sets, licences

- Poly Haven (CC0, 866 textures as of today) and ambientCG (CC0) cover nearly every generic substrate: plaster in weathered red, blue and yellow, peeling paint, volcanic rock tiles, terracotta, terrazzo, mosaic, shutters, rusty grids, gold, bronze and wicker. They are safe to commit.

- Wikimedia Commons is the best photo-reference source, but its CC BY-SA files carry obligations if pixels are reused.
- Museum open-access images (Met CC0 for public-domain works; LACMA) are the cleanest source for tile and ornament *patterns*.

### Inferences
- **Licence policy for the public repo:**
  - Commit only CC0 (Poly Haven, ambientCG) or self-made textures.
  - Commons photos: use them as *reference* to paint from. Do not bake them in unless they are CC0 or public domain. CC BY / BY-SA require attribution (BY-SA also share-alike on the derived texture), so keep a CREDITS file if any are used.
  - Period photos: Brehme and Casasola files may be public domain in the US but not in Mexico, which has a long copyright term. Treat them as reference only unless the Commons licence tag says otherwise.
- **What to paint ourselves:**
  - Talavera tile sets are best built as small (32–64 px) palette-limited tiles, using the six official glaze colours, from museum pattern references.
  - Tezontle: Poly Haven's volcanic_rock_tiles / volcanic_herringbone_01 or ambientCG Rock029 are good starting substrates. Recolour toward oxblood and re-lay in an irregular ashlar pattern, since no CC0 scan of a Mexico City tezontle wall is known.
  - Chiluca and cantera: tint a fine grey stone (stone_wall_04, castle_brick_07) and carve relief into trim sheets.
- **Pipeline note for Godot 4 retro:** download at 1K, downscale to 128–256 px, palette-quantise, set nearest filtering. CC0 photo-scans become period-appropriate when crushed this way, and the colour grade carries most of the "Mexico".

### Gaps
- The Met and LACMA open-access licence pages were not fetched in this pass (rate limits). Their CC0/public-domain status is from general knowledge and should be confirmed per object before reusing pixels.
- No CC0 scan of a tezontle façade, estípite or Talavera panel was found. The Sketchfab search page loads, but individual model licences were not reviewed.
- No dedicated "Talavera pattern archive" (an open-licence digitised pattern book) was found. The best substitutes are museum objects and Uriarte's Google Arts & Culture stories.
- Texture Haven merged into Poly Haven (general knowledge; not re-sourced here).

## 7. Prioritised texture shopping list for a first level (about 28 surfaces)

### Takeaway
A first street-plus-interior level (a colonial street with a church front, a pulquería, a courtyard house and a deco frontage) needs about 28 surfaces. Plaster and stone carry roughly 60% of the screen, so do them first. Then the signature items that make it read as Mexico City: tezontle, Talavera, a rótulo and a dome. The base CC0 material is listed where one exists; "PAINT" marks surfaces that must be self-made.

### Cited Findings
The source pages for each base texture are the verified Poly Haven and ambientCG links in section 6; the historical justification for each item is in sections 1–5. Priority order:

**Exterior walls**

1. **Lime-washed plaster, ochre/yellow, weathered.** Base: [yellow_plaster_02](https://polyhaven.com/a/yellow_plaster_02). Historical basis: lime and earth pigments ([UPV paper](https://riunet.upv.es/entities/publication/4eb20e7d-bff6-479d-9f0f-c583aff3e40f)).
2. **Almagre red plaster, weathered.** Base: [red_plaster_weathered](https://polyhaven.com/a/red_plaster_weathered).
3. **Añil blue plaster, faded.** Base: [blue_plaster_weathered](https://polyhaven.com/a/blue_plaster_weathered) / [PaintedPlaster008](https://ambientcg.com/view?id=PaintedPlaster008).
4. **Lime white plaster, clean and dirty variants.** Base: [white_plaster_rough_01](https://polyhaven.com/a/white_plaster_rough_01), [white_stucco](https://polyhaven.com/a/white_stucco).
5. **Spalled plaster over brick/rubble ("aplanado caído").** Base: [red_brick_plaster_patch_02](https://polyhaven.com/a/red_brick_plaster_patch_02), [worn_cracked_plaster](https://polyhaven.com/a/worn_cracked_plaster).
6. **Tezontle ashlar wall, red.** PAINT, from [volcanic_rock_tiles](https://polyhaven.com/a/volcanic_rock_tiles) / [Rock029](https://ambientcg.com/view?id=Rock029) recoloured. Reference: [Commons: Tezontle](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Tezontle).

### Inferences
- **Build order:**
  - Items 1–9 give a playable greybox-plus street.
  - Items 6, 7, 13, 15 and 17 are the "this is Mexico City" identity pass.
  - Items 18–28 are interiors and landmarks.
- **Reuse.** Items 1–4 can be one greyscale plaster texture tinted by vertex colour or a material parameter in Godot. That gives one texture and many lime-wash colours, which also matches the historical practice of re-washing over old colours.
- **Decals.** Items 8, 15, 17 and 24 work best as decals or overlays on the base plaster, not as unique wall textures. That keeps texture memory low in PS2 style.
- **Repo safety.** Nothing in this list requires textures.com. Where the user prefers textures.com photos, keep them in the gitignored local textures folder (the project already does this for textures/ps2 per memory) and ship CC0 or painted fallbacks.

### Gaps
- No CC0 base exists for Talavera, tezontle ashlar, estípite, rótulos or pasta tiles. These will need to be painted or sourced by the user, so the user should be asked for those designs and textures, in line with the project's "ask for textures and designs" practice.
- Quantities and resolutions are estimates; they have not been tested in the engine.
