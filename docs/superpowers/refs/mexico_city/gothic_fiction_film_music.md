# Mexican gothic, supernatural and magical-realist literature, cinema and music: tonal and narrative sources for an alt-1920s Mexico City stealth/investigation game

Conventions used in these notes:
- **[POST-1930]** marks a work made after 1930: usable for tone, but not period-accurate for a 1920s setting.
- **[PD?]** public-domain status. Mexican copyright terms are long (I did not verify the exact rule in this session), and modern restorations carry their own rights, so treat everything as "reference only" unless a lawyer says otherwise.
- **[fetched]** means I read or searched the linked page during this session. **[orientation]** means a standard Wikipedia entry linked so readers can get their bearings. Those facts come from well-established general knowledge and I did not re-check them page by page this session (see the Gaps sections).
- No files were downloaded. Links only.

## 1. Literature: which Mexican (and adjacent) gothic, fantastic, decadent and detective works give this world tone, imagery, plot structures and character types?

### Takeaway
The strongest period-accurate material (pre-1930) is the 19th-century urban ghost story (Roa Bárcena's "Lanchitas", 1877-78), the Porfirian *modernista* decadents (Couto Castillo's *Asfódelos*, 1897; Amado Nervo's *El donador de almas*, 1899). These give the game's city its haunted streets, its spiritualist salons and its morbid dandies. The best-known Mexican gothic texts (Rulfo, Fuentes, Garro, Tario, Arreola, Moreno-Garcia) all come after 1930. Even so, they supply the game's core narrative devices: the dead who talk, the house that remembers, the town as narrator, the double.

### Cited Findings

**19th-century Mexican gothic (period-safe, public domain by age)**
- **José María Roa Bárcena, "Lanchitas" (1877; also dated 1878).** An urban ghost story built on a Mexico City legend. A series of supernatural events transforms its protagonist, a priest. It was written in a period "when positivism and spiritualism coexisted". The text is in *Relatos* (UNAM 1941 edition) as a PDF on the Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes. — [Cervantes Virtual: Relatos](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/obra/relatos/) [fetched via search]; scholarly article: [Sincronía (UdeG)](https://revistasincronia.cucsh.udg.mx/index.php/sincronia/article/download/847/988/3011) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* the core plot of a priest who is called at night to give confession in a decrepit house, finds the penitent is a long-dead man, and is left broken. In the story he becomes the simple-minded "Lanchitas". This is a ready-made side-quest skeleton: a professional called to a house, the client turns out to be a corpse, and the investigator is marked afterwards. The plot summary comes from general knowledge of the story. The search snippets confirm only "supernatural events which transform the priest protagonist".

**Porfirian modernismo / decadents (1890s-1900s, period-safe)**
- **Bernardo Couto Castillo (1879-1901), *Asfódelos* (1897).** Twelve stories about death in its many varieties, "from Lady Death wandering the streets of the city to a hypochondriac who goes mad out of fear of death". He was a founder of *Revista Moderna* and part of the decadent current of modernismo. There is an English translation, *Asphodels* (Snuggly Books). — [Snuggly Books: Asphodels](https://snugglybooks.co.uk/asphodels) [fetched via search]; [UNAM thesis on Couto Castillo](https://ru.dgb.unam.mx/bitstream/20.500.14330/TES01000650024/3/0650024_A1.pdf) [fetched via search]; scholarship on madness as transgression in his stories — [Anecdótica (UNAM)](https://revistas-filologicas.unam.mx/anEcdotica/index.php/anec/article/download/143/114/171) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* absinthe-and-opium bohemians, death personified walking the city streets, and madness from fear of death. These fit a 1920s generation of NPCs who are aging Porfirian decadents haunting cafés and salons.
- **Amado Nervo, *El donador de almas* (serialised 1899 in *Cómico*).** The poet Andrés Esteves can summon spirits and bend them to his will. He gifts the soul of a woman, Alda, to his friend, the physician Rafael, and the story turns on the impossible love between Rafael and that soul. Scholars disagree whether it is fantastic, horror, supernatural or "marvelous". Nervo's other fantastic short novels are *Mencía* (also *Un sueño*), *Amnesia* and *El sexto sentido*. — [UNAM Global: El donador de almas](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/el-donador-de-almas-de-amado-nervo/) [fetched via search]; [Revistas UCM article](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/ALHI/article/download/73120/4564456556800/4564456612513) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* a spiritualist-science "bargain" (a soul as a gift or commodity), a doctor and a poet as investigator archetypes, and possession inside a body. It fits a 1920s Mexico City where Porfirian spiritism survives.

**Mid-century fantastic (tone; [POST-1930])**
- **Francisco Tario, *La noche* (1943) [POST-1930].** Stories told from the point of view of objects and animals. The human characters are "alienated beings on the verge of madness or death". The book was a rarity in a literary scene dominated by realism. Scholars note Tario would have fit the Borges/Bioy/Ocampo *Antología de la literatura fantástica* (1940) but sat on the margins of the literary world. — [Wikipedia: Francisco Tario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Tario) [fetched via search]; [Archipiélago (UNAM) article](https://journals.unam.mx/index.php/archipielago/article/download/19761/18752/31467) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* objects that testify. A coffin, a dress or a house narrates. This maps directly onto investigation "readables" and item barks, where a clue's own voice is the dialogue.
- **Juan José Arreola, *Confabulario* (1952) [POST-1930]**: short, ironic fables with bureaucratic absurdism ("El guardagujas", the railway that never arrives). — [Wikipedia: Juan José Arreola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Jos%C3%A9_Arreola) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* a Kafkaesque civil-service register (absurd permits, trains to nowhere). It suits a post-revolutionary bureaucracy run by the living and the dead.

**The canonical Mexican gothic/magical realism (tone; all [POST-1930])**
- **Juan Rulfo, *Pedro Páramo* (1955) [POST-1930].** Juan Preciado travels to Comala to find his father and finds a town of murmuring ghosts. The novel is a mosaic of voices from the dead, told partly from a shared grave. — [Wikipedia: Pedro Páramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_P%C3%A1ramo) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* the single most useful structure for a dialogue-heavy investigation. The dead give fragmentary, contradictory testimony, and the player assembles the chronology of a cacique's crimes. Its setting (Revolution and Cristero era, Jalisco) overlaps the game's decade.
- **Carlos Fuentes, *Aura* (1962) [POST-1930].** A young historian is hired to edit a dead general's memoirs in a sealed old house in Mexico City's centro. The ancient widow Consuelo and her young niece Aura turn out to be one woman. It is written in the second person. — [Wikipedia: Aura (novel)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aura_(novel)) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* the house in the historic centre that time forgot, the hired researcher reading a dead man's papers (an investigator's literal job), the witch who is both old and young, the double, and a second-person voice that suits first person.
- **Elena Garro, *Los recuerdos del porvenir* (1963) [POST-1930].** The town of Ixtepec itself narrates its memories of the Cristero-era occupation by General Rosas and his mistresses. Julia vanishes; Isabel turns to stone. — [Wikipedia: Recollections of Things to Come](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recollections_of_Things_to_Come) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* the town as narrator (a district's collective voice could be the game's chorus or narrator), military occupation of a town, a vanished woman as the central mystery, and the Cristiada (1926-29) as exactly the game's period.
- **Silvia Moreno-Garcia, *Mexican Gothic* (2020) and *Velvet Was the Night* (2021) [POST-1930].** *Mexican Gothic* (1950s) sends a Mexico City socialite to a decaying English-built mining mansion in Hidalgo, where she finds eugenics, fungal hive-memory and a family-run corruption. *Velvet Was the Night* is 1970s Mexico City noir around the 1971 Halconazo, with a secretary and a goon as dual protagonists. — [Wikipedia: Mexican Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Gothic) [orientation]; [Wikipedia: Velvet Was the Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velvet_Was_the_Night) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* foreign capital and colonial extraction as the "curse" (mine owners, foreign concessions), a society-woman investigator, and state violence as noir texture. These are the clearest modern models for a post-colonial gothic tone.
- **Mariana Enríquez (Argentine), *Things We Lost in the Fire* (2016 Eng.) and *Our Share of Night* (2019) [POST-1930].** Occult secret societies (the Order) tied to dictatorship-era violence, and urban folk horror in which the dead are the "disappeared". — [Wikipedia: Mariana Enríquez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Enr%C3%ADquez) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* an elite occult order feeding on political violence, a natural fit for Deadlock-style occult secret societies in an alt-1920s capital.

**Mexican detective fiction**
- **Antonio Helú.** Possibly the first writer to create a recurring Mexican detective, **Máximo Roldán**, who is not a policeman but a minor criminal: "Roldán" is an anagram of *ladrón* ("thief"). In *La obligación de asesinar* the thief Carlos Miranda solves the crime. That collection (book publication 1957 [POST-1930]) is in Ellery Queen's *Queen's Quorum*. Scholarship reads Helú's thief-detective as bringing out "the failures of the Mexican Revolution". — [Wikipedia: Antonio Helú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Hel%C3%BA) [fetched via search]; [Project MUSE article excerpt](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/589280/pdf) [fetched via search]; [La Razón: Breve historia del noir mexicano](https://www.razon.com.mx/el-cultural/breve-historia-del-noir-mexicano/) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* the thief-as-detective. That fits a Thief-style stealth protagonist who solves crimes while lifting the loot, with cynicism about the Revolution as the moral backdrop.
- **Rodolfo Usigli, *Ensayo de un crimen* (1944) [POST-1930]**, filmed by Luis Buñuel as *The Criminal Life of Archibaldo de la Cruz* (1955). A wealthy man believes a music box gives him the power to kill. Each woman he means to murder dies by other means. — [Wikipedia: Rodolfo Usigli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodolfo_Usigli) [orientation]; [Wikipedia: The Criminal Life of Archibaldo de la Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Criminal_Life_of_Archibaldo_de_la_Cruz) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* a cursed object and a "who really killed her?" structure in which the suspect wants to be guilty, a darkly comic tone close to Grim Fandango.

**Essay**
- **Octavio Paz, *El laberinto de la soledad* (1950) [POST-1930]**, especially the chapter "Todos Santos, Día de Muertos": the fiesta as release and the Mexican's intimacy with, and mockery of, death. — [Wikipedia: The Labyrinth of Solitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Labyrinth_of_Solitude) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* a thematic thesis for the whole game (masks, fiesta, death as companion), and a guide to how NPCs might speak about the dead casually.

### Inferences
- Period-accurate "in-world" literature for readables and NPC quotations should come from the pre-1930 layer (Roa Bárcena, Nervo, Couto Castillo, *Revista Moderna*). A 1920s bookish NPC could plausibly own these. Rulfo, Fuentes and Garro should shape structure and tone but should not appear as in-world texts.
- The *Revista Moderna* circle (1898-1911) is a natural in-world occult/aesthete society: decadent poets, spiritualists and morphinomaniacs. By the 1920s they would be aging survivors of the Porfiriato, which suits the "post-colonial old regime haunting the new" theme.
- Helú's thief-detective and Aura's hired researcher are the two most natural protagonist templates: a criminal with deductive skill, or a hired scholar drawn into a house's memory.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the date Helú's first Máximo Roldán stories appeared (often said to be late 1920s). Only the 1957 collection date is sourced.
- The detailed plot of "Lanchitas" (the night confession of a dead man) comes from general knowledge. The fetched snippets confirm only an urban ghost legend and a transformed priest.
- Lead not checked this session: Luis González Obregón's *Las calles de México* (1922) and Vicente Riva Palacio and Juan de Dios Peza's *Tradiciones y leyendas mexicanas* (1880s) collect colonial street legends of Mexico City (Calle de Don Juan Manuel, La Mulata de Córdoba, and others). They are likely the richest period-safe source of district-specific ghost stories and deserve a dedicated follow-up.

## 2. Cinema: which Mexican silent/early sound, golden-age and later horror films give this world tone and imagery, and which are freely viewable?

### Takeaway
The 1933-34 cycle is period-adjacent and the best visual reference: *La Llorona* (first Mexican sound horror), *El fantasma del convento* and *Dos monjes* (German-expressionist melodrama). Two of the three, plus Fernando de Fuentes' Revolution drama *El compadre Mendoza*, are on archive.org. The 1957-62 Abel Salazar/Chano Urueta/Fernando Méndez cycle, *Macario* (1960) and *Alucarda* (1977) supply the gothic and death-as-character imagery. Every film here is [POST-1930] relative to a 1920s setting. Public-domain status is unverified for all of them.

### Cited Findings

**Early sound "first gothic cycle" (1933-34)**
- **La Llorona (1933, dir. Ramón Peón; written by Fernando de Fuentes and Carlos Noriega Hope).** The first Mexican horror film with sound. Restored in 2021 from a single 16 mm projection print by Permanencia Voluntaria with Filmoteca UNAM, the Academy Film Archive and the Cinema Preservation Alliance. It is available on Blu-ray (Indicator) and streaming (Pragda). — [Wikipedia: La Llorona (1933 film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona_(1933_film)) [fetched via search]; [Pragda](https://pragda.com/film/la-llorona/) [fetched via search]; [Mondo Digital review](https://mondo-digital.com/monastery.html) [fetched via search]
  - *What it offers:* La Llorona's colonial-era backstory framed inside a modern (1930s) bourgeois household, where a child is threatened. It is a direct template for "the legend repeats in modern-day Mexico City".
  - *PD:* **not found on archive.org** in my searches. The 2021 restoration is commercially distributed, so treat it as under rights.
- **El fantasma del convento (1934, dir. Fernando de Fuentes).** A married couple and their best friend shelter in a monastery haunted by a monk who sold his soul for his best friend's wife. It is compared to Dreyer's *Vampyr* for its "protracted sense of dread". It was restored by the UCLA Film & Television Archive and The Film Foundation's World Cinema Project from the original nitrate negative held by Permanencia Voluntaria. — [UCLA Film & TV Archive event page](https://cinema.ucla.edu/events/el-fantasma-del-convento-02-15-19) [fetched via search]; [KVIFF archive entry](https://www.kviff.com/en/programme/film/57/34782-the-phantom-of-the-convent) [fetched via search]
  - **On archive.org:** [fantasma-convento-fhd](https://archive.org/details/fantasma-convento-fhd) and [el-fantasma-del-convento-pantalla-cuadrada-original](https://archive.org/details/el-fantasma-del-convento-pantalla-cuadrada-original) [fetched: archive.org search API]. Uploads are user-contributed, and their PD status is not established.
  - *What it offers:* the love-triangle bargain with the devil, monastic catacombs and mummified monks, and slow dread in place of shocks, which is right for stealth pacing.
- **Dos monjes (1934, dir. Juan Bustillo Oro; cinematography Agustín Jiménez).** An expressionist melodrama with an "audacious flashback structure". An ailing monk recognises a new brother, attacks him, and the shared past is told twice, from each man's point of view, with contrasts heightened through German-expressionist visuals. André Breton praised it as "an audacious and unusual experiment". It was restored by Scorsese's World Cinema Foundation and is on the Criterion Channel. — [Wikipedia: Two Monks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Monks) [fetched via search]; [Criterion essay "Expressionism a la mexicana"](https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/7119-dos-monjes-expressionism-a-la-mexicana) [fetched via search]; [Morelia FF: pionera del cine gótico mexicano](https://moreliafilmfest.com/en/dos-monjes-de-juan-bustillo-oro-pionera-del-cine-gotico-mexicano) [fetched via search]
  - **On archive.org:** [dos-monjes-1934-mexican](https://archive.org/details/dos-monjes-1934-mexican) [fetched: archive.org search API]. PD status unverified.
  - *What it offers:* **the Rashomon-style dual testimony**, the same events told by two unreliable witnesses, which is the ideal structural model for interrogation dialogue. Its expressionist angles and shadows also match the Deadlock-like art direction.
- **El compadre Mendoza (1934, dir. Fernando de Fuentes).** A Revolution-era drama about a landowner who plays both sides and betrays his compadre, a Zapatista general. — **On archive.org:** [ElCompadreMendoza](https://archive.org/details/ElCompadreMendoza) [fetched: archive.org search API]; [Wikipedia: El compadre Mendoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_compadre_Mendoza) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* not horror, but the best period source for the Revolution's moral rot: hacienda interiors, a dinner-party betrayal and a hanged man. It is backstory material for the generation that runs the 1920s city.

**Golden-age and later horror ([POST-1930], tone and imagery)**
- **El Vampiro (1957, dir. Fernando Méndez; Germán Robles as Count Lavud)** and **El ataúd del vampiro (1958)**: a Mexican gothic hacienda vampire with fog, cobwebs and a decaying estate. The sequel moves the coffin to the city (a wax museum, a theatre). — [Wikipedia: El Vampiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Vampiro) [orientation]; [Wikipedia: The Vampire's Coffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vampire%27s_Coffin) [orientation]
- **El espejo de la bruja (1962, dir. Chano Urueta).** A witch's mirror, a murdered wife's vengeance, and a surgeon grafting stolen hands. — [Wikipedia: The Witch's Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Witch%27s_Mirror) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* a haunted object as witness (the mirror shows the past), a gothic mansion in the city, and science-occult hybrid horror.
- **Macario (1960, dir. Roberto Gavaldón; from B. Traven's story).** A poor woodcutter shares his turkey with Death, who gives him a healing water. The film is set in the colonial/Viceregal era around Día de Muertos and ends in a candle-filled cave of lives. It was the first Mexican film nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. — [Wikipedia: Macario (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macario_(film)) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* **death as a character you can bargain with**, the candle-of-life cave (a ready-made set piece or hub), and the Inquisition as the earthly antagonist.
- **Alucarda (1977, dir. Juan López Moctezuma).** A convent possession and witchcraft film; excess, Catholic gothic. — [Wikipedia: Alucarda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alucarda) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* convent interiors and clerical cruelty. Treat as a strong flavour reference only.
- **Santo films (from 1958) [POST-1930; label later].** Masked luchador vs vampires, mummies, zombies. — [Wikipedia: El Santo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Santo) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* pulp folk-hero energy, Grim Fandango-adjacent camp, and masked vigilantes. Lucha libre itself started in Mexico in the 1930s, so treat it as an anachronism to label.
- **Cronos (1993, dir. Guillermo del Toro).** A 16th-century alchemist's clockwork scarab grants eternal life and vampirism. An antiques dealer finds it inside an archangel statue. — [Wikipedia: Cronos (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cronos_(film)) [orientation]
  - *What it offers:* the colonial-era occult device rediscovered in a modern city shop, a direct fit for a clockwork-occult art direction and a "MacGuffin of the Viceroyalty" plot.

### Inferences
- The 1933-34 films are the closest thing to period-accurate cinema for the art team. *Dos monjes* gives expressionist lighting and architecture; *El fantasma del convento* gives slow-dread pacing and colonial monastery spaces.
- *Dos monjes*' dual-flashback testimony and *Pedro Páramo*'s chorus of the dead together suggest a dialogue system in which witnesses (living or dead) give conflicting, partial accounts that the player reconciles.

### Gaps
- **Public domain:** I could not establish PD status for any of these films. Archive.org uploads exist for *Dos monjes*, *El fantasma del convento* and *El compadre Mendoza*, but archive.org hosting does not prove PD, and the Film Foundation/UCLA restorations are separately protected. *La Llorona* (1933) was not found on archive.org.
- I did not search Filmoteca UNAM's own streaming catalogue this session.
- Details of the golden-age films are [orientation] (well-known but not re-fetched).
- Silent-era Mexican horror (pre-1930) appears to be almost lost. I found no silent Mexican gothic titles that are viewable online.

## 3. Music and soundscape: what did 1920s Mexico City sound like, and where are period recordings available as reference?

### Takeaway
The game's 1920s city should sound like: Porfirian **barrel organs** (German Wagner & Levien instruments, in use since 1884) on street corners, **orquestas típicas** and military bands, **danzón** in the dance halls, **corridos** of the Revolution and the Cristiada, church bells and sung **alabados**, foxtrot and tango in cabarets, and, right at the decade's end, **Agustín Lara**'s cabaret bolero and the first radio (Lara on radio from Sept 1930). For an orchestral horror palette, **Silvestre Revueltas** (dissonant, street-band ostinatos, 1931-40) is the key reference. Period recordings are best found in the **Strachwitz Frontera Collection (UCLA)** and the **Library of Congress National Jukebox** (1901-1925, also mirrored on archive.org).

### Cited Findings

**Street sound**
- **Organilleros (barrel-organ players):** the first barrel organs reached Mexico in **1884** from the German factory **Wagner & Levien**. Porfirio Díaz embraced the idea of "music in the city". There were about **500 organilleros in the Porfiriato** against about **50 today**. They played in the Zócalo, Coyoacán and the parks, with repertoire such as "Las Mañanitas" and "Cielito Lindo". The tradition survives today only in Mexico City and Santiago de Chile. — [Xataka: organilleros](https://www.xataka.com/magnet/organilleros-parte-tejido-cultural-ciudad-mexico-problema-que-no-hay-quien-fabrique-instrumentos) [fetched via search]; [Local.mx: de dónde viene la tradición](https://local.mx/en/ciudad-de-mexico/de-donde-viene-la-tradicion-de-los-organilleros-en-la-ciudad/?amp=1) [fetched via search]; [Chilango: Confesiones de un organillero](https://www.chilango.com/musica/confesiones-de-un-organillero/) [fetched via search]
  - *Sound-design use:* a slightly out-of-tune, wheezing barrel organ is the signature "Mexico City street" sound. In the 1920s it was common (hundreds of players), so it can be a district motif, or a diegetic cover for stealth noise.
- **Modern field recording (reference only, [POST-1930]):** "Ciudad de México (Zona Sur)", a 2020 sound postcard on archive.org. — [archive.org: postal-sonora-cdmx](https://archive.org/details/postal-sonora-cdmx) [fetched: archive.org search API]

**Period recordings: archives**
- **Strachwitz Frontera Collection (UCLA / Arhoolie Foundation):** the largest repository of commercially produced Mexican and Mexican-American vernacular recordings, nearly 160,000 recordings on 78s, 45s, LPs and tapes, from 1908 to the 1990s. It holds first recordings of corridos, canciones, boleros, rancheras and sones. Recordings can be streamed on the UCLA site. Examples include Cristero-rebellion corridos such as "Corrido del Padre Pedroza" and "Corrido de la Unión Popular" (from an album "Corridos de la Rebelión Cristera"). — [UCLA Frontera collection page](https://frontera.library.ucla.edu/content/collection) [fetched via search]; Cristero corrido records: [node 344144](https://frontera.library.ucla.edu/node/344144), [node 344139](https://frontera.library.ucla.edu/node/344139) [fetched via search]; [Arhoolie Foundation](https://arhoolie.org/?p=1405) [fetched via search]; [Ethnomusicology Review overview (PDF)](https://www.chicano.ucla.edu/files/news/Ethnomusicology%20Review_Highlights%20from%20UCLA%27s%20collections_%20The%20Strachwitz%20Frontera%20Collection%20_62813%20.pdf) [fetched via search]
- **Library of Congress National Jukebox:** more than 10,000 free historical recordings from **1901-1925**. Típica orchestras from Hispanic nations made hundreds of Victor records in the 1910s-30s. — [National Jukebox](https://www.loc.gov/jukebox) [fetched via search]; [Jukebox dataset README](https://data.labs.loc.gov/jukebox/README.html) [fetched via search]
  - Jukebox items are mirrored on archive.org with the identifier pattern `loc-jukebox-NNNN-title` (e.g. [loc-jukebox-9647-olvido, 1925](https://archive.org/details/loc-jukebox-9647-olvido)). I did not verify that this particular item is Mexican. [fetched: archive.org search API]
- **Orquesta Típica Mexicana "Anáhuac", Victor 79174 (1926)**: a Mexico City típica orchestra on record in the game's exact decade. — [Old Time Blues: tag Mexico City](https://oldtimeblues.net/tag/mexico-city/) [fetched via search]
- **Orquesta Típica Nacional, "Jesusita en Chihuahua" (RCA Victor Mexicana 75516-A)**, a Revolution-era polka standard, is on archive.org. — [archive.org item](https://archive.org/details/orquesta-tipica-nacional-jesusita-en-chichuahua-rca-victor-mexicana-75516-a) [fetched: archive.org search API]. The recording date is not shown in the listing.
- Argentine tango 78s of the period (e.g. Orquesta Típica Victor, 1926; Francisco Canaro, 1931) are on archive.org for the cabaret layer. — e.g. [OrquestaTipicaVictorSecreto1926](https://archive.org/details/OrquestaTipicaVictorSecreto1926) [fetched: archive.org search API]

**Composers and genres**
- **Agustín Lara (1897-1970):** born in Tlacotalpan, Veracruz. He began performing in cabarets around 1927, was scarred in the face by a showgirl, moved to Puebla, and returned to Mexico City in 1928 as composer-pianist for the tenor Juan Arvizu. In **September 1930** he began a successful radio career. He is famous for the bolero (*Solamente una vez*, *Veracruz*, *Pecadora*). — [Wikipedia: Agustín Lara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agust%C3%ADn_Lara) [fetched]
  - *Use:* a cabaret pianist with a scarred face is a ready-made 1927-29 NPC archetype. The bolero is right at the edge of the period, so use it in late-night cabarets.
- **Silvestre Revueltas (1899-1940):** *Esquinas* and *Ventanas* (1931), *Cuauhnáhuac* (1931), *Janitzio* (1933, rev. 1936), *Redes* film score (1935), *Homenaje a Federico García Lorca* (1936), *Sensemayá* (1938), *La noche de los Mayas* film score (1939), and scores for *¡Vámonos con Pancho Villa!* (1936). His style drew on folk traditions and street music, with dissonant harmonies and rhythmic ostinatos. He was assistant conductor of the National Symphony Orchestra under Carlos Chávez (1929-1935). — [Wikipedia: Silvestre Revueltas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvestre_Revueltas) [fetched]
  - *Use:* the reference palette for an orchestral horror score. Street-band brass gone sour, obsessive ostinatos (*Sensemayá*) for chase and alert states, and *La noche de los Mayas*' percussion for occult climaxes. All [POST-1930] but by a composer active in the period.
- **Carlos Chávez, *Sinfonía india* (1935-36) [POST-1930]**: uses Indigenous melodies and percussion; Chávez founded and led the Orquesta Sinfónica de México from 1928. — [Wikipedia: Sinfonía india](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinfon%C3%ADa_india) [orientation]; [Wikipedia: Carlos Chávez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Ch%C3%A1vez) [orientation]
- **Danzón**: the Cuban-derived couple dance that dominated Mexico City dance halls. The Salón México dance hall opened in the 1920s, and Copland's *El Salón México* (1936) was inspired by it. — [Wikipedia: Danzón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danz%C3%B3n) [orientation]; [Wikipedia: El Salón México](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Sal%C3%B3n_M%C3%A9xico) [orientation]
- **Son jarocho** (Veracruz harp, jarana, zapateado). — [Wikipedia: Son jarocho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_jarocho) [orientation]
- **Corrido** (narrative ballad of the Revolution). — [Wikipedia: Corrido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrido) [orientation]
- **Alabado** (sung hymn of praise, often unaccompanied, associated with dawn prayers and wakes). — [Wikipedia: Alabado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabado) [orientation]
- **Juventino Rosas, "Sobre las olas" (1888)**: the Porfirian waltz on every barrel organ. It is public domain as a composition, though not necessarily as a recording. — [Wikipedia: Sobre las Olas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobre_las_Olas) [orientation]
- **XEW radio (founded 1930, label "post-period")**. — [Wikipedia: XEW-AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XEW-AM) [orientation]
- **Día de Muertos** (music, bells, vigils). — [Wikipedia: Day of the Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_the_Dead) [orientation]

### Inferences
- A layered district soundscape for a 1920s city:
  - Daytime: barrel organ, street vendors' *pregones* and church bells.
  - Evening: a military band in the Zócalo bandstand, danzón from dance halls.
  - Late night: cabaret foxtrot, tango and early bolero (Lara, after 1927).
  - Churches and wakes: alabados and bells.
  - Cristero corridos sung in pulquerías as political danger.
- For the horror score, Revueltas (street-band dissonance) gives a distinctly Mexican alternative to generic gothic strings. His instrumentation (small brass, tuba, percussion, sour clarinets) can be approximated from licensed packs.
- The Frontera Collection and National Jukebox are the best places to *listen* for period timbre. Licensed packs remain the actual sound source, as the brief requires.

## 4. Narrative structures: which structures from these works suit a dialogue-heavy stealth investigation game?

### Takeaway
Five recurring structures in this corpus map cleanly onto investigation mechanics: (1) the chorus of unreliable dead narrators (*Pedro Páramo*, *Dos monjes*), (2) the house that remembers (*Aura*, *El espejo de la bruja*, "Lanchitas"), (3) the town under a curse that narrates itself (*Los recuerdos del porvenir*, *Mexican Gothic*), (4) the double / the old-young witch (*Aura*, *Dos monjes*), and (5) the bargain with death or the devil (*Macario*, *El fantasma del convento*, *El donador de almas*, *Cronos*). Two further templates come from Mexican detective fiction: the thief-detective (Helú) and the man who wants to be the murderer (Usigli).

### Cited Findings
- Unreliable, split testimony: *Dos monjes* tells the shared past twice, from each man's viewpoint — [Wikipedia: Two Monks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Monks); [Criterion](https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/7119-dos-monjes-expressionism-a-la-mexicana). The chorus of the dead is *Pedro Páramo*'s structure — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_P%C3%A1ramo) [orientation].
- Objects and animals as narrators: Tario's *La noche* (1943) — [Wikipedia: Francisco Tario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Tario).
- The bargain: a monk sold his soul for his friend's wife (*El fantasma del convento*) — [UCLA](https://cinema.ucla.edu/events/el-fantasma-del-convento-02-15-19). A soul given as a gift by a spirit-summoning poet (*El donador de almas*) — [UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/el-donador-de-almas-de-amado-nervo/).
- Death personified in the city's streets: Couto Castillo's *Asfódelos* — [Snuggly Books](https://snugglybooks.co.uk/asphodels).
- Thief as detective, critique of the Revolution: Helú — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Hel%C3%BA); [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/589280/pdf).
- Legend reframed in the modern household: *La Llorona* (1933) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona_(1933_film)).

### Inferences
These are design proposals derived from the works above, not sourced facts.

**Mechanic translations**
- **Unreliable dead narrators → testimony system.**
  - Ghosts, and the living, give partial and contradictory accounts.
  - The player stealthily gathers physical evidence to "contradict" a witness, which unlocks the next layer of testimony (*Dos monjes* plus Grim Fandango dialogue trees).
  - Comala's murmurs suggest ambient "overheard" ghost barks while sneaking. This would build on the existing overheard-notes and talk systems.
- **House that remembers → environmental storytelling plus time-layered rooms.**
  - A Centro Histórico mansion where frobbing an object (a mirror, a memoir) replays its memory (*Aura*, *El espejo de la bruja*).
  - "Lanchitas" gives a quest beat: the investigator is summoned to a house whose occupant is long dead and leaves "marked", a persistent consequence.
- **Town/district under a curse → district-level mystery.**
  - Each district has a collective voice (Garro's Ixtepec) and an inherited colonial sin: extraction (*Mexican Gothic*), Inquisition (*Macario*), Revolution betrayal (*El compadre Mendoza*).
  - Lifting or exposing the curse is the district's main arc.
- **The double → identity mystery.** The old widow who is also the young girl (*Aura*), and the two monks who are each other's shadow. These suit a twist where the client, the victim and the culprit are the same person across time.
- **Bargain with death → a Death/Catrina-like broker NPC.**
  - Death bargains (Macario's shared meal; a candle cave of lives as a hub showing each NPC's remaining life).
  - Paz's "intimacy with death" sets the voice: wry, festive, unafraid. It aligns with Grim Fandango's tone.

**Protagonist templates**
- Helú's gentleman thief who solves crimes while robbing (closest to the Thief/Garrett fantasy).
- Aura's hired scholar or archivist.
- Mexican Gothic's society-woman sleuth.

**Antagonist templates**
- A cacique (Pedro Páramo).
- A revolutionary general occupying a town (General Rosas in Garro).
- A Porfirian occult society of decadents (*Revista Moderna* circle).
- A foreign mining concession family (*Mexican Gothic*).
- A colonial alchemist's device (*Cronos*).

### Gaps
- I found no academic source that maps Mexican gothic structures to game design specifically. Everything in this section beyond the cited plot facts is inference.
- A follow-up on Mexico City colonial street legends (Calle de Don Juan Manuel, La Mulata de Córdoba, the Callejón del Beso is Guanajuato, so check), via González Obregón and Riva Palacio and Peza, would give district-specific plot seeds. It was not researched in this pass.
