# Mexican visual art, prints, photography, folk art and figures (colonial era to ~1930) for an alternate-1920s gothic Mexico City

Notes on sourcing. Links marked **(opened)** were returned or opened by search/fetch during this research session. Links marked **†** are standard reference or collection pages (Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons categories, museum search pages) built from known, stable URL patterns but NOT opened in this session; the report writer should spot-check them before publishing. Descriptive facts sourced only to a † link come from established art-history knowledge and are lower confidence than facts from opened sources. Items after ~1930 are labelled **[LATER]**.

Copyright shorthand used throughout:
- **PD-MX**: public domain in Mexico. Under the Commons analysis of Mexican law, works by authors who died **before 1952** are public domain in Mexico, because the 1982 extension to life+50 was not retroactive; the current term is life+100 (2003), and photographs are also life+100 (Art. 92 bis) — [Commons: Copyright rules/Mexico (opened)](https://blogs.ashmolean.org/commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons_Copyright_rules_by_territory/Mexico.html).
- **PD-US**: works published before 1931 are public domain in the US as of 1 Jan 2026 (1930 publications entered the US public domain on that date; general US rule, not re-verified this session).
- **REF-ONLY**: in copyright somewhere that matters; study it, do not reproduce it.

## 1. Prints and graphic art (Posada, Manilla, Vanegas Arroyo, TGP, Estridentismo, Lotería)

### Takeaway
The Vanegas Arroyo broadside world (Manilla and Posada, c. 1880–1913) is the richest PD source the game has. It is cheap printing, skeletons, crimes, miracles, monsters and the end of the world, and it is directly reusable as in-game posters, broadsides and chapbooks. The 1920s Estridentista woodcut is the bridge to an "occult modern" 1920s look. Fermín Revueltas (d. 1935) is PD in Mexico; Méndez and Alva de la Canal are not. Use the Lotería as a structural idea (54 emblematic figures) but draw a new deck.

### Cited Findings
**José Guadalupe Posada (1852/1853–1913)** — PD-MX, PD-US. Directly usable.
- The Library of Congress holds "over a hundred prints by Posada" in the Prints & Photographs Division's popular graphic art holdings. LOC's "Revisiting Rights-Free" series presents them as free of copyright restrictions — [LOC Picture This blog (opened)](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2020/10/revisiting-rights-free-popular-graphic-art-prints/). Example: *Altar de la Virgen de Guadalupe* (1910), [hdl.loc.gov/loc.pnp/ppmsc.04604 (opened via blog)](http://hdl.loc.gov/loc.pnp/ppmsc.04604). Search all items: [LOC pictures search "Posada" †](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/search/?q=Posada).
- LOC's Posada holdings came from the gift and bequest of the collectors Caroline and Erwin Swann. LOC also holds zinc blocks Posada used for some of his most famous images — [LOC blog 2023, "Posada's Lively Calaveras" (opened)](https://blogs.loc.gov/loc/2023/10/jos-guadalupe-posadas-lively-calaveras-and-enduring-legacy/).
- Birth year conflict: LOC/OAC finding aid gives "1853-1913" — [OAC finding aid (opened)](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/ft1r29n6tm). Most references give 1852.
- The calavera as a theme was "probably invented" by Manuel Manilla, and Posada made it a national icon — [Met, Manilla/Posada broadside entry (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649).
- Met Open Access lets you copy, modify and distribute public-domain images even for commercial use. Example: a 1905 Vanegas Arroyo sheet combining *The pantheon of all the skeletons who are eating and drinking in a cemetery* (Posada) with skeletons holding scythes (Manilla), printed on green paper — [Met 729587 / 729649 (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649). Search: [Met Open Access "Posada" †](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search?q=Posada&showOnly=openAccess).
- Art Institute of Chicago holds *Calavera Tapatía* — [AIC (opened)](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/52264/calavera-tapatia). UC Berkeley has an online Posada exhibit, "Illustrating Mexico one page at a time" — [Berkeley Spotlight (opened)](https://exhibits.lib.berkeley.edu/spotlight/art-of-posada/catalog/28-1418).
- **La Calavera Garbancera / "La Catrina"** (Posada zinc etching, c. 1910–13): a skeletal lady in a European plumed hat, mocking Indigenous-born women who aped French fashion. Diego Rivera's 1947 Alameda mural fixed the name "Catrina" **[LATER]**. — [Wikipedia: La Calavera Catrina †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Calavera_Catrina); [Commons: José Guadalupe Posada †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jos%C3%A9_Guadalupe_Posada).
- Other Posada and Vanegas Arroyo sheets to study (titles from standard catalogues; verify on LOC/Met): *Gran calavera eléctrica*, *Calavera revolucionaria*, *Calavera oaxaqueña*, *Calavera de Don Quijote*, *Remate de calaveras alegres y sandungueras*, *El fin del mundo*, crime sheets such as *Guadalupe Bejarano*, parricide "ejemplos", monster births, miracle and apparition sheets, executions — [Commons category †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jos%C3%A9_Guadalupe_Posada).

**Manuel Manilla (c. 1830–late 1890s)** — PD-MX, PD-US.
- Little is known of his life. He was born c. 1830, died of typhus in the late 1890s, and was the first illustrator-engraver at Vanegas Arroyo's studio from 1882. He popularised calaveras for Día de Muertos sheets — [Met entry (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649).
- *Calavera Tapatía* (a Guadalajara skeleton drinking tequila, a Catrina behind him), Manilla with printer Vanegas Arroyo, is Met Open Access — [Met 714945 (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/714945).

**Antonio Vanegas Arroyo press (1880s–1917, continued by heirs)** — the publisher, PD.
- Broadsides (*hojas volantes*), corridos, chapbooks, games and *calaveras* on cheap coloured paper (green, magenta, yellow). The Met sheet above is on green paper — [Met (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649); [Wikipedia: Antonio Vanegas Arroyo †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Vanegas_Arroyo).

**Taller de Gráfica Popular (founded 1937)** — **[LATER]**, REF-ONLY (Méndez d. 1969 and others in copyright) — [Wikipedia: Taller de Gráfica Popular †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taller_de_Gr%C3%A1fica_Popular).

**Estridentismo (1921–c. 1927)**
- An avant-garde movement founded in Puebla by Manuel Maples Arce at the end of 1921. Ramón Alva de la Canal, Leopoldo Méndez and Fermín Revueltas illustrated its publications in 1921–26. Its first exhibition was at the "Café de Nadie", Mexico City, in 1924. Edward Weston, Jean Charlot and Germán Cueto were associated. In 1928 Alva de la Canal and Revueltas co-founded the Grupo 30-30 — [Wikipedia: Ramón Alva de la Canal (opened via search)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram%C3%B3n_Alva_de_la_Canal); [Wikipedia: Stridentism (opened via search)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stridentism); [Monoskop: Estridentismo (opened via search)](https://monoskop.org/Estridentismo).
- Status: **Fermín Revueltas (d. 1935) is PD-MX** under the pre-1952 rule. Alva de la Canal (d. 1985), Méndez (d. 1969), Charlot (d. 1979) and Cueto (d. 1975) are REF-ONLY; their 1920s published prints may be PD-US only. Death years are standard biographical data, not re-verified here.

**Lotería**
- Commonly told origin: lotería came to New Spain in 1769. Clemente Jacques, a French businessman, began publishing a deck in 1887 from the press that printed his canned-food labels, with 54 images of daily life (El Catrín, La Chalupa, El Cantarito). Copies were said to be packed with army rations — [Azteca Jalisco (opened via search)](https://www.aztecajalisco.com/arte-y-cultura/quien-creo-la-loteria-mexicana-el-origen-de-clemente-jacques-y-las-54-cartas/). These are popular-press sources, so the 1887 date and the army story should be treated as tradition.
- Historical Clemente Jacques card brands (El León, Victoria, Gallo) are catalogued by The World of Playing Cards — [WOPC: Jacques (opened via search)](https://www.wopc.co.uk/mexico/jacques/victoria).

### Inferences
- The Vanegas Arroyo format (a woodcut or zinc image on top, rhyming text below, a sensational headline, cheap coloured paper) is a ready-made template for in-game wanted posters, crime broadsides, miracle sheets and Day-of-the-Dead satire. Real Posada and Manilla sheets can hang on walls as-is.
- The Clemente Jacques name and the modern classic card art are an active commercial brand. Treat lotería as a mechanic and design idea, not a copy source.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm whether the surviving "classic" Clemente Jacques card artwork is truly the 1887 design or a later redraw, or who owns rights in the current art.
- Did not locate an online catalogue of Estridentista woodcuts with clear rights statements (the Museo Nacional de Arte and Museo de Arte Moderno hold these).

## 2. Painting (Symbolists, early modernists, muralists, colonial, devotional)

### Takeaway
The game's gothic heart is **Julio Ruelas** (d. 1907, PD): satyrs, drowned women, insects boring into skulls, a self-portrait as the damned. Around him sit **Saturnino Herrán** (d. 1918, PD; Indigenous-Catholic syncretism), **José María Velasco** (d. 1912, PD; the Valley of Mexico) and the whole colonial canon (PD). Muralists are reference-only except possibly Orozco. The devotional genres (crowned nuns, ánimas, ex-votos, Hell murals, dead-child portraits) give the game its "religious horror" vocabulary.

### Cited Findings
**Julio Ruelas (1870–1907)** — PD-MX, PD-US. KEY.
- Principal illustrator of *Revista Moderna* and the central figure of Mexican Symbolism. His themes are macabre, sinister and melancholy, drawn from German Romanticism and French Symbolism after study in Germany (1892–95) and France (1904–07). His etched self-portraits are made of tormented, twisted black lines. Works include *La domadora* (1897), *El rapto* (1901), *Autorretrato con musa* (1903), *Confiteor* (1903), *Fauno tocando la flauta* (1903), *En el país de los gigantes* (1904), *Romance en el país de los enanos* (1904) and *Auto de fé* (1906). Works are held at the Museo de la Ciudad de México and in Zacatecas — [Wikipedia: Julio Ruelas (opened via search)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_Ruelas).
- The Met holds a Ruelas item, page 716935; the fetch was rate-limited (HTTP 429), so title and rights are unconfirmed — [Met 716935 (search result)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/716935). Ruelas images are also on Artvee: *The Critic* (*La crítica*, the etching of a bug-creature drilling into the artist's head) and *The Magnolia* — [Artvee: the critic 2 (search result)](https://artvee.com/dl/the-critic-2); [Artvee: the magnolia (search result)](https://artvee.com/dl/the-magnolia). The Coppel Collection has *Pierrot doctor* — [Coppel (search result)](https://modulaciones.coppelcollection.com/pierrot-doctor).
- Commons category: [Julio Ruelas †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Julio_Ruelas).

**Saturnino Herrán (1887–1918)** — PD-MX, PD-US.
- He made many studies (1914–18) for *Nuestros dioses*, a never-realised mural for the Teatro Nacional (now Bellas Artes) on Mexican syncretism. Its Coatlicue-and-Christ panel is the key image. He studied under Ruelas at San Carlos. MUNAL mounted "Saturnino Herrán y otros modernistas" — [Wikipedia: Saturnino Herrán (opened via search)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturnino_Herr%C3%A1n); [UIC "Ancient Americas Appropriated": *Coatlicue y Cristo* (search result)](https://aaa.digital.uic.edu/s/ancientamericasappropriated/item/6308).
- Other works (standard catalogue): *La ofrenda* (1913; a trajinera of marigolds and the ages of man), *El rebozo*, *La leyenda de los volcanes*, *El cofrade de San Miguel*, criollas and tehuanas — [Commons: Saturnino Herrán †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Saturnino_Herr%C3%A1n).

**José María Velasco (1840–1912)** — PD. Panoramas of the Valley of Mexico (for example *El Valle de México desde el cerro de Santa Isabel*, 1875, MUNAL), with the lakes, the volcanoes and the city as a pale smear — [Commons: José María Velasco †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jos%C3%A9_Mar%C3%ADa_Velasco); [MUNAL †](https://www.munal.mx).

**Dr. Atl (Gerardo Murillo, 1875–1964)** — REF-ONLY (copyright in Mexico to 2064). Volcano and curvilinear-horizon landscapes; Atl-colour technique; co-author with Guillermo Kahlo of *Iglesias de México* (1924–27) — [Wikipedia: Gerardo Murillo †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerardo_Murillo).

**Roberto Montenegro (1885–1968)** — REF-ONLY. Beardsley-style Art Nouveau illustration, later muralist — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Montenegro).

**Ángel Zárraga (1886–1946)** — **PD-MX under the pre-1952 rule**; pre-1931 published works also PD-US. Symbolist and religious work (*La dádiva*, 1910; *Ex-voto (San Sebastián)*, 1912) — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81ngel_Z%C3%A1rraga).

**Early muralism** — REF-ONLY by default: Rivera (d. 1957), Siqueiros (d. 1974). **Orozco died 1949**, which would make him PD-MX under the pre-1952 rule, but Mexican public-domain status of famous artists is contested; see a 2026 post on "Frida Kahlo, Diego Rivera and the Public Domain in Mexico: Who's Right?" — [Hugh Stephens blog (search result)](https://hughstephensblog.net/2026/07/). Treat all muralists as REF-ONLY without legal review.

**Colonial painters** — all PD.
- **Cristóbal de Villalpando** (c. 1649–1714): the Mexico City Cathedral sacristy canvases; *Vista de la Plaza Mayor de México* (1695), the best period view of the Zócalo — [Commons †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Crist%C3%B3bal_de_Villalpando).
- **Juan Correa** (c. 1646–1716): Cathedral sacristy; Afro-Mexican master of apocalyptic and Marian canvases — [Commons †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Juan_Correa).
- **Miguel Cabrera** (1695–1768): Sor Juana portrait, casta series (1763), crowned nuns. His 1759 portrait of Sor María Josepha Augustina Dolores is cited as one of the finest crowned-nun portraits — [Smarthistory, "Crowned nun portraits" (search result)](https://smarthistory.org/?p=54313); [Commons †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Miguel_Cabrera).
- **Baltasar de Echave Orio** (c. 1548–1623): Mannerist martyrdoms at MUNAL — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltasar_de_Echave_Orio).

**Casta paintings** — PD.
- Sets showing mixed-race families; the inscriptions encode a hierarchy of "purity" and "degeneration". LACMA holds sets by Juan Patricio Morlete Ruiz (c. 1760) and Miguel Cabrera, and offers ~20,000 public-domain image downloads — [LACMA: Morisca y albina (search result)](https://collections.lacma.org/node/220040); [LACMA: *De español y torna atrás, tente en el aire* (search result)](https://collections.lacma.org/node/222687).

**Monjas coronadas (crowned nuns)** — PD.
- The Museo Nacional del Virreinato (Tepotzotlán) has the largest collection in Latin America. Nuns wear crowns of paper flowers and wax figurines and carry the Christ Child or a crucifix. There are profession portraits and death-bed portraits of deceased nuns — [Museo Nacional del Virreinato guide (search result)](https://audiala.com/es/mexico/municipio-de-cuautitlan-izcalli/museo-nacional-del-virreinato); [Smarthistory (search result)](https://smarthistory.org/?p=54313); [Escudo de monja image, Linkgua (search result)](https://visual.linkgua-ediciones.com/en/imagen/escudo-pintura-de-monjas-coronadas-anonimo-novohispano).

**Hell and Last Things (postrimerías)** — PD.
- 16th-century Hell murals in the Augustinian open chapels at **Actopan** and **Santa María Xoxoteco** (Hidalgo) were found in the 1970s under whitewash. In the 18th century, infernal scenes left churches and were replaced by **ánimas (souls in purgatory) paintings**, for example at Yauhquemecan, Tlaxcala — [UAEH paper on Actopan and Xoxoteco Hell murals (search result)](https://www.uaeh.edu.mx/investigacion/productos/5979/el_infierno_en_la_pintura_mural_agustina_del_siglo_xvi__actopan_y_xoxoteco_en_el_estado_de_hidalgo.pdf); [UNAM, *Cielo, infierno y purgatorio* chapter (search result)](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cielo/ciyp_04cap.pdf). Santa Cruz Tlaxcala is cited as a leading 17th–18th-century ensemble — [UPAEP (search result)](https://upaep.mx/images/vinculacion_sociedad/arpa/docs/tripticos/prrqstaCruz.pdf).

**Ex-votos and retablos (devotional tin paintings)** — 19th–early 20th century, mostly anonymous. Small oil on tin; a holy image above, the disaster below, a handwritten thanks — [Wikipedia: Retablo †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retablo).

**Biombos (folding screens)** — PD. Colonial screens with city views and the Conquest; enconchado (mother-of-pearl inlay) — [Wikipedia: Biombo †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biombo).

**Death portraits** — PD. Painted portraits of dead children ("angelitos") and of dead nuns are a documented genre (see the crowned nuns above). There is also a photographic tradition of post-mortem portraits — [Smarthistory (search result)](https://smarthistory.org/?p=54313) for the nuns.

### Inferences
- Ruelas plus Herrán give the game two "fine-art" registers that are fully reusable: decadent European-trained gothic (Ruelas) and syncretic nationalist mysticism (Herrán). Hung in a 1920s salon, a Ruelas etching or a *Nuestros dioses* study is period-correct and copyright-safe.
- Villalpando's 1695 *Plaza Mayor* is an ideal source for the layout and street life of an alternate Zócalo.

- Could not open the Met Ruelas record (rate-limited) or confirm which Ruelas images MUNAL serves online.
- No verified list of specific 18th-century rural Hell paintings beyond the Actopan and Xoxoteco murals and the Yauhquemecan ánimas.
- Did not verify the open-access policy of MUNAL or the Museo Nacional del Virreinato. INAH and INBAL images likely carry reproduction fees (see section 5).

## 3. Photography (1880s–1930)

### Takeaway
For streets, faces and architecture of 1900–1930, use **C.B. Waite** (d. 1927), **W.H. Jackson** (d. 1942), **Guillermo Kahlo** (d. 1941) and the **Casasola** archive (Agustín Víctor Casasola d. 1938). These photographers are PD-MX under the pre-1952 rule, but INAH-held scans carry INAH reproduction terms. **Hugo Brehme** (d. 1954) is the best pictorialist mood source but is in copyright in Mexico until 2055 (his pre-1931 postcards are PD-US). Modotti is borderline; Weston and Álvarez Bravo are reference-only.

### Cited Findings
- **Casasola Archive / Fototeca Nacional (INAH, Pachuca)**: the Casasola archive is the founding collection of the Fototeca Nacional and covers the Porfiriato and the Revolution — [Wikipedia: Casasola Archive (search result)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casasola_Archive). It holds 325,290 glass negatives and photographs, moved to the Ex-Convento de San Francisco, Pachuca, on 15 Nov 1976. The Fototeca has about 900,000 items by 2,000+ authors from 1847 to the present, including named collections for Tina Modotti, Guillermo Kahlo and Hugo Brehme. It is searchable via the [Mediateca INAH †](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/) — [search summary of INAH sources (search result)](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/alquimia/article/download/3192/3082/5081).
- **Hugo Brehme (1882–1954)**: German-born founder of Mexican pictorialism. He came to Mexico in 1905, photographed the Revolution, then landscapes and people, made tourist postcards and published widely known collections in the 1920s–30s. He opened "Fotografía Artística Hugo Brehme" by 1912. The largest Brehme holding (1,846 negatives, 278 positives) has been at the Fototeca Nacional since 1977 — [Wikipedia: Hugo Brehme (search result)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Brehme); [UC Riverside finding aid (search result)](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/kt0x0nf24d); [UNM collection (search result)](https://nmarchives.unm.edu/repositories/22/resources/2839); [Google Arts & Culture: Brehme (search result)](https://artsandculture.google.com/entity/g121p79p_). The UC Riverside and UNM finding aids note copyright status as unknown.
- **C.B. (Charles Betts) Waite (1861–1927)**: American, moved to Mexico City in 1896 (studio on Calle de Rosales). His views were sold to travellers: Mexico City street scenes, Guadalupe, bullfights, vendors, the Sacromonte at Amecameca and the Guanajuato catacombs (mummies) — [UC Riverside finding aid (search result)](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8zg6qnh/); [University of Alabama, 1904 album (search result)](https://archives.lib.ua.edu/repositories/3/resources/4970); [UNM album (search result)](https://nmarchives.unm.edu/repositories/22/resources/2831). PD-MX and PD-US (d. 1927).
- **William Henry Jackson (1843–1942)**: Mexico views from the 1880s for the railways; many are in LOC's Detroit Publishing Co. collection — [LOC Detroit Publishing collection †](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/collection/det/); [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Jackson). PD-US.
- **Guillermo Kahlo (1871–1941)**: architectural survey of colonial churches for the Porfirian state, and *Iglesias de México* (1924–27). PD-MX — [Commons †](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Guillermo_Kahlo).
- **Tina Modotti (1896–1942)**: in Mexico 1923–30. PD-MX under the pre-1952 rule; check US publication — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Modotti).
- **Edward Weston** (in Mexico 1923–26; d. 1958) — REF-ONLY. **Manuel Álvarez Bravo** (1902–2002; early work late 1920s) — REF-ONLY — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_%C3%81lvarez_Bravo).

### Inferences
- What these archives show for set dressing: tranvías and mule trams; adobe and tezontle facades with iron balconies; pulquerías with painted fronts; street vendors, cargadores and soldaderas; rurales and federal troops; the catacomb mummies of Guanajuato; church interiors packed with gilded retablos.

### Gaps
- Did not confirm the exact Mediateca INAH reproduction licence or fees for Fototeca scans.
- Did not verify which Modotti or Weston works were published before 1931.

## 4. Figures and folk art (3D and prop reference)

### Takeaway
For props and characters, the strongest period-correct sources are cartonería Judas figures, sugar skulls, santos and bultos, cristos de caña, masks, milagros and Aztec sculpture. **Ocumicho devils (1960s), Metepec trees of life (mid-20th c.), clay Catrinas and alebrijes (1936)** are **[LATER]** and should be treated as inspiration only.

### Cited Findings
- **Judas figures (cartonería)**: papier-mâché effigies, often packed with fireworks, burned on Holy Saturday (Sábado de Gloria) in plazas. The custom descends from colonial missionary teaching and became political satire. The Linares family of La Merced shaped the modern Judas in the early 20th century. In the 1950s Pedro Linares's workshop employed 300+ helpers to meet demand; he later invented alebrijes **[LATER]** — [Mexican Folk Art Guide: Judas burning (search result)](https://www.mexican-folk-art-guide.com/judas-burning.html); [Excélsior (search result)](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/quema-del-judas-arte-efimero-y-tradicion-que-perdura/1308566).
- **Ocumicho devils** **[LATER]**: Marcelino Vicente (1933–1968) began making devil tableaux in the 1960s, likely from Christian iconography with Purépecha or Spanish influence; clay had been women's work until then. He was murdered on 22 Oct 1968 — [Wikipedia: Marcelino Vicente (search result)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcelino_Vicente); [Collectors Weekly (search result)](https://www.collectorsweekly.com/articles/the-curious-tale-of-mexicos-most-peculiar-pottery/).
- **Aztec sculpture** (all held by INAH; commercial reproduction is regulated, see section 5) — [Wikipedia: Coatlicue †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coatlicue); [Aztec calendar stone †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_calendar_stone); [Tlaltecuhtli †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlaltecuhtli); [Xipe Totec †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xipe_Totec); [Mictlantecuhtli †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mictlantecuhtli); [Xolotl †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xolotl).
  - *Coatlicue* and the *Piedra del Sol*: both unearthed in the Zócalo in 1790.
  - *Tlaltecuhtli* monolith: found at the Templo Mayor in 2006 **[LATER find]**.
  - *Xipe Tótec*: the flayed god.
  - *Mictlantecuhtli*: the clay statues from the House of the Eagles.
  - *Xolotl*: the dog-headed god.
- **Codices** (all PD as 16th-century and earlier works):
  - *Florentine Codex*: Sahagún, 1545–90, with Nahua artists, 12 books; on the World Digital Library since 2012; Getty's *Digital Florentine Codex* in 2023 — [Wikipedia (search result)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_Codex); [Infodocket 2023 (search result)](https://www.infodocket.com/2023/10/27/the-digital-florentine-codex-a-rare-500-year-old-manuscript-gets-a-second-life-online/); [Getty Digital Florentine Codex †](https://florentinecodex.getty.edu/).
  - *Codex Borgia* (Vatican Library) — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borgia); [DigiVatLib †](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Borg.mess.1).
  - *Codex Borbonicus* (Assemblée nationale, Paris) — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Borbonicus).
- **Other figures, all †** (standard references, not opened):
  - Calaveras de azúcar (alfeñique, sugar skulls) — [Wikipedia †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_skull).

## 5. Public domain status and open-access collections

### Takeaway
The safe-to-reproduce core is: Posada and Manilla sheets (LOC, Met, AIC), Ruelas, Herrán, Velasco, Zárraga, Revueltas, colonial painting (LACMA Open Access), codices, and photographs by Waite, Jackson, Kahlo and Casasola from non-INAH or open scans. Mexican archaeological monuments (Aztec sculpture) are a legal risk for commercial reproduction.

### Cited Findings
- **Mexico copyright term history**: 1928 (30 years for literary works), 1948 (life+20), 1963 (life+30), 1982 (life+50, not retroactive), 1993–94 (life+75), 2003 (life+100). Authors who died before 1952 are PD. Anonymous works stay protected "until the author or the owner of the rights are identified" (Art. 153) — [Commons: Mexico (opened)](https://blogs.ashmolean.org/commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons_Copyright_rules_by_territory/Mexico.html).
- **INAH and archaeological monuments**: commercial reproduction of archaeological, historical or artistic monuments requires authorisation from the competent institute (Art. 17 of the federal monuments law), via form INAH-01-004. The criteria are that the heritage is not harmed and its image is not distorted. Fees are set by the Ley Federal de Derechos, Art. 288-D — [INAH trámite form (search result)](https://tramites.inah.gob.mx/Descargables/INAH_01_004_A_y_B_REPRODUCCION_MONUMENTOS.pdf). INAH and the Secretaría de Cultura have acted against commercial users, such as MrBeast in 2025 — [Expansión (search result)](https://expansion.mx/tendencias/2025/05/15/inah-demanda-mrbeast-que-paso) — and Max over the Castillo de Chapultepec in 2024 — [El Imparcial (search result)](https://www.elimparcial.com/espectaculos/2024/06/12/inah-podria-demandar-a-max-por-uso-indebido-del-castillo-de-chapultepec/).
- **Open-access collections**:
  - Met Open Access: unrestricted commercial use — [Met (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/714945).
  - LACMA: ~20,000 PD downloads — [LACMA (search result)](https://collections.lacma.org/node/220040).
  - Library of Congress: Posada, popular graphic art and Detroit Publishing — [LOC blog (opened)](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2020/10/revisiting-rights-free-popular-graphic-art-prints/).
  - Art Institute of Chicago — [AIC (search result)](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/52264/calavera-tapatia).
  - World Digital Library (now at LOC) — [LOC WDL †](https://www.loc.gov/collections/world-digital-library/).
  - Biblioteca Digital Mexicana — [bdmx †](http://bdmx.mx).
  - Mediateca INAH — [mediateca †](https://mediateca.inah.gob.mx/).
  - MUNAL — [munal.mx †](https://www.munal.mx).

### Inferences
- In-game Aztec gods should be **invented**, inspired by Coatlicue and the others rather than replicas of specific INAH pieces. This avoids the INAH permit question and suits an alternate universe.

### Gaps
- No legal opinion on whether a fictional videogame depiction counts as "commercial reproduction" under the monuments law. Get legal review.

## 6. Iconography ideas for an alternate-universe city

### Takeaway
Build the city's visual language from the Vanegas Arroyo broadside (public voice), Ruelas Symbolism (high-culture decadence), colonial devotional painting (the church's power) and the excavated Aztec stone (the buried past pushing up).

### Cited Findings
- The calavera was a mass-printed satirical form sold for Día de Muertos — [Met (opened)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/729649).
- Hell murals were whitewashed and later rediscovered, and 18th-century churches swapped Hell for ánimas paintings — [UNAM (search result)](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cielo/ciyp_04cap.pdf).
- Crowned nuns were also portrayed dead, crowned with paper flowers and wax figurines — [Smarthistory (search result)](https://smarthistory.org/?p=54313).
- Coatlicue and the Sun Stone were dug out of the Zócalo in 1790 — [Wikipedia: Coatlicue †](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coatlicue).

### Inferences
- **The broadside economy**: the city talks through cheap coloured sheets. Wanted posters, monster births, miracles and executions are pasted on walls; the player reads them for clues. Use real Posada and Manilla sheets plus new ones in their style.
- **Calaveras as social masks**: the dead mock the living each November. A secret society signs its work with a Garbancera-style skull lady.
- **Whitewashed Hells**: churches hide Hell murals under lime. Scraping the plaster reveals clues or a map of the underworld (Mictlan meets Dante).
- **Crowned dead nuns**: convent portraits of dead sisters in flower crowns, the sisters' eyes following the player — a ghost motif with a real source.
- **Ánimas in the flames**: purgatory paintings with donors' faces among the burning souls; a guild pays to have rivals painted in.
- **The stones beneath the plaza**: excavated gods (invented, Coatlicue-like) in a 1920s museum basement; the city is built over a buried, half-awake pantheon.
- **Judas night**: Holy Saturday effigies of hated officials explode in the streets — a set-piece for chaos and stealth cover.
- **A lotería of fate**: a new 54-card deck (El Catrín, La Muerte, El Diablito-style figures) used by fortune tellers and as the game's item and quest icon system.
- **Ruelas salons**: decadent Revista Moderna poets with insect, satyr and drowned-woman etchings; an aesthete cult.
- **Ex-voto confessions**: tin paintings in a shrine each tell a crime or miracle in a few words, and serve as investigation leads.
- **Photographic ghosts**: Waite-style views of the catacomb mummies and street life as in-world postcards; a pictorialist photographer whose plates show what is not there.

### Gaps
- These ideas are design proposals, not research findings.
