# Mexican Folklore, Legend, Myth, Religious Horror and Occult Traditions (Mexico City focus) — catalogue for an alt-1920s Mexico City stealth/investigation game

Conventions used below:
- **Origin** tags: MESO (Mesoamerican / Nahua), COL (colonial Spanish / Catholic), AFRO (African-diaspora), SYN (syncretic), MOD (19th-20th c. modern).
- **Status**: HIST = documented history; LEG = legend / folklore; MIX = legend attached to a real person, place or event.
- **[Hook]** lines are my own design inferences, not sourced claims.
- **Post-1930** is flagged wherever a thing did not exist (or was not public) in the 1920s.
- Key primary collections: Luis González Obregón, *México viejo* (1891/1900) and *Las calles de México* (1922; 1927 two-volume edition, vol. 1 "Leyendas y sucedidos"); Vicente Riva Palacio & Juan de Dios Peza, *Tradiciones y leyendas mexicanas* (verse, serialised 1882); Artemio de Valle-Arizpe (*Leyendas mexicanas*, 1943, and *Historia, tradiciones y leyendas de calles de México*, later compilation). Full texts are on archive.org: [Las calles de México (archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015), [México viejo 1521-1821 (archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/mexico-viejo-luis-obregon), [México viejo y anecdótico, 1909 (archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/mxicoviejoyane00gonz), [The Streets of Mexico, 1937 English translation (archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-EGN-299).

## 1. Colonial legends of Mexico City's streets (González Obregón, Peza/Riva Palacio, Valle-Arizpe)

### Takeaway
The Centro Histórico has a dense, named-street corpus of colonial ghost and crime legends, mostly fixed in print between 1882 and 1922 — exactly the generation before the game's 1920s — so a 1920s character could plausibly have read *Las calles de México* (1922) as a new bestseller. Many legends attach to real people (Don Juan Manuel, Treviño de Sobremonte, Lamport) and real buildings (Casa de los Azulejos, the Inquisition palace), which makes them ideal investigation "cases" where history and legend disagree.

### Cited Findings

**Source corpus**
- González Obregón published *Las calles de México* in 1922; the 1927 edition was in two volumes: vol. 1 "Leyendas y sucedidos", vol. 2 "Vida y costumbres de otros tiempos"; it explains how figures, events and customs gave names to the city's streets — [TAMU library record / search summary](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000680341); [Centro Histórico "Km Cero" magazine, Apr 2023](https://centrohistorico.cdmx.gob.mx/storage/app/media/Km%20cero/KmCero171_Abril2023.pdf)
- The opening chapters of *Las calles de México* (archive.org OCR) include: "La calle del Puente de Alvarado", "La Llorona", "La Virgen del Perdón", "Un aparecido", "La casa de los Azulejos", "La calle de Don Juan Manuel", "La casa del judío" (the book also contains "La Mulata de Córdoba") — [archive.org full text](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015); [Milenio on La Mulata in *Las calles de México*](https://www.milenio.com/estados/leyendas-mexicanas-la-mulata-de-cordoba)
- Riva Palacio and Peza's *Tradiciones y leyendas mexicanas*: verse narratives serialised from January 1882 in the liberal paper *La República*, later collected as 14 narratives of "supernatural events, tragic happenings and popular beliefs"; includes "Don Juan Manuel", "La mujer herrada" and "La Llorona" — [UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/tradiciones-leyendas-mexicanas-peza-riva-palacio/)
- Valle-Arizpe's books are nostalgic chronicles tied to "names and nicknames of streets, houses, convents"; *Leyendas mexicanas* first published 1943 (post-1930) — [Lecturalia, Leyendas mexicanas](https://www.lecturalia.com/libro/50370/leyendas-mexicanas); [Lecturalia, Historia, tradiciones y leyendas de las calles de México](https://www.lecturalia.com/libro/50274/historia-tradiciones-y-leyendas-de-las-calles-de-mexico)

**Catalogue of street legends**

1. **Don Juan Manuel — "¿Qué hora es?"** (COL; MIX). Calle de Don Juan Manuel = today's República de Uruguay; his house is at Uruguay 94 (now a hotel). Jealous nobleman Juan Manuel de Solórzano makes a pact with the Devil, who tells him to kill whoever passes his door at 11 pm; he steps out, asks the passer-by "¿Qué hora es?", and kills him — night after night (the wife's lover, Antonio de los Reyes, was the intended victim). (The popular reply "Dichoso usted que sabe la hora de su muerte" and the twist that a victim was his own nephew are widely retold but were not confirmed in the sources fetched.) González Obregón dates his end to 1641, executed "by angels" (found hanged from the gallows). Note date drift: one version has him arriving c. 1612 — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/casa-maldita-leyenda-don-juan-manuel-republica-de-uruguay/amp/); [Cultura Colectiva](https://culturacolectiva.com/historia/casa-juan-manuel-de-solorzano-leyenda/); [archive.org, Las calles de México](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015); [UNAM Global (Peza verse version)](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/tradiciones-leyendas-mexicanas-peza-riva-palacio/)
   - [Hook] A recurring 11 pm street-encounter enemy: an NPC who asks the player the time; answering truthfully triggers an attack. Mechanic: a pocket watch item lets you lie.

2. **La Mulata de Córdoba** (AFRO/COL; LEG). A mixed-race woman of Córdoba, Veracruz, who never ages, accused of witchcraft and of being in several places at once, is brought to "the dark prisons of the Holy Office" in Mexico City. She draws a ship in charcoal on her cell wall, asks the jailer what it lacks ("only that it move"), steps aboard and sails away; some versions send her to Manila. González Obregón's 1922 version is the canonical one — [Milenio](https://www.milenio.com/estados/leyendas-mexicanas-la-mulata-de-cordoba); [SEP Nueva Escuela, Leyendas novohispanas](https://nuevaescuelamexicana.sep.gob.mx/contenido/coleccion/leyendas-novohispanas/)
   - [Hook] Magic: "drawn doors". The player sketches an exit in chalk or charcoal on a wall that becomes real for a few seconds. Faction: her descendants as a smuggling and escape guild.

3. **La Llorona** (MESO+COL; MIX). See section 2 for the 1509 omen. González Obregón devotes a chapter to her as a woman in white crossing colonial streets nightly and links her to Cihuacóatl; the Peza/Riva Palacio verse names her Luisa, who kills her children after being abandoned, is executed, and wanders forever; Manuel Carpio's 1849 poem gives her the human name Rosalía; a Spanish story, "El Pozo de la Llorona" (1866), shows the name and the weeping-ghost idea also existed in Spain — [Library of Congress Folklife blog](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2021/10/la-llorona-roots-branches-and-the-missing-link-from-spain/); [UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/tradiciones-leyendas-mexicanas-peza-riva-palacio/); [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015)
   - [Hook] A roaming audio-led threat in canal or flood districts: her cry is a sound cue that makes guards and civilians flee, so the player can use it as a diversion.

4. **El Puente del Clérigo** (COL; LEG). 1649, Santiago Tlatelolco, a bridge over the Texontlali aqueduct. The priest Juan de Nava forbids his niece Margarita Jáuregui to see the rake Duarte de Zárraga; Duarte stabs the priest on the bridge and dumps the body in the aqueduct. Later (a year later in some versions) Duarte is found dead, strangled by a skeleton in a cassock, with his own crested dagger in its skull. The street became "Calle del Puente del Clérigo", given as today's 7a-8a de Allende (north of the Centro toward Tlatelolco) — [es-academic mirror of Spanish Wikipedia "Leyendas de la Ciudad de México"](https://es-academic.com/dic.nsf/eswiki/722014); [ClubEnsayos retelling](https://www.clubensayos.com/Historia/Mitos-Y-Leyendas/1132251.html)
   - [Hook] A revenant-justice case: a murderer the police cannot touch, and a skeletal priest stalking him. Do you protect the target or let the dead finish it?

5. **El Callejón del Muerto** (COL; LEG). Today's República Dominicana (between Tepito and El Carmen). Version A (c. 1600): the merchant Tristán de Alzúcer vows a pilgrimage to Guadalupe for his son's recovery, forgets, and is warned by a corpse-like figure in a white shroud with a yellow candle; the apparition walks at midnight. Version B: a man found stabbed in the alley in 1825 — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/noticias/centro-historico-leyendas-cdmx/); [Chilango, callejones](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/leyendas-de-callejones-en-cdmx/)
   - [Hook] The "unpaid vow" mechanic: NPCs haunted by broken promises to saints. Fulfilling the vow by proxy ends the haunting and opens their district to you.

6. **Calle de la Machincuepa** (COL; LEG). 1714; today a stretch near La Soledad, by the Zócalo. Paz de Quiroga inherits her uncle Mendo de Quiroga's fortune only if she turns a public somersault (*machincuepa*) in the Plaza Mayor at noon. She does it — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/noticias/centro-historico-leyendas-cdmx/)
   - [Hook] Absurd-will puzzle quest (Grim Fandango tone): a humiliating public act as the price of a fortune or a key.

7. **Calle de la Cruz Verde** (COL; LEG). Corner of Regina and Correo Mayor, dated 17 September 1556. Álvaro Villadiego asks María de Aldarafuente to paint a green cross on her house if she returns his love; the cross is said to still be visible — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/noticias/centro-historico-leyendas-cdmx/)
   - [Hook] A diegetic sign language: painted crosses and chalk signs as secret signals between lovers, smugglers and a cult.

8. **Calle de la Quemada** (COL; LEG). c. 1550, around Jesús María street. Beatriz de Espinosa burns her own face so that the Italian Martín de Scúpoli will love her for her character; he marries her anyway — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/noticias/centro-historico-leyendas-cdmx/)
   - [Hook] A veiled, scarred noblewoman as an information broker.

9. **Calle del Indio Triste** (COL/MESO; LEG). Between Correo Mayor/Guatemala and El Carmen. An Indigenous noble mourns the fall of Tenochtitlan over the Templo Mayor ruins; in another version he is punished for failing, while drunk, to warn of a revolt, and begs outside his former home — [Sopitas](https://www.sopitas.com/noticias/centro-historico-leyendas-cdmx/)
   - [Hook] A stone statue that weeps; the source of a "sorrow" spirit.

10. **Un aparecido (the teleported soldier)** (COL; MIX). On 25 October 1593 a Philippine-garrison soldier suddenly appears in the Plaza Mayor of Mexico City, reporting the death of Governor Gómez Pérez Dasmariñas in Manila on the same day; it is in González Obregón — [archive.org, Las calles de México](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015)
    - [Hook] Witness case: a man who "arrives" from across the Pacific overnight. Teleportation magic or a smuggling hoax?

11. **La Casa de los Azulejos** (COL; MIX). Real mansion (Madero / 5 de Mayo). González Obregón traces it from the 16th-century Vivero family to a murder in 1828 during the Acordada uprising — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015)
    - [Hook] In the 1920s it was the Sanborns café, a perfect public meeting place for informants (real building, real period use; verify the date the café opened before using it).

12. **La casa del judío (Tomás Treviño de Sobremonte)** (COL; HIST). A crypto-Jewish merchant burned alive at the great auto de fe of 11 April 1649 for refusing to renounce his faith — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015); [Wikipedia, Mexican Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Inquisition)
    - [Hook] A hidden family archive or reliquary in a walled-up room; descendants still guard it.

13. **La Virgen del Perdón** (COL; MIX). The legend says the painting on the Cathedral's Altar del Perdón was made by a prisoner (a Jew, in versions of the story) to win a pardon. González Obregón shows it was a retablo by the Flemish painter Simón Pereyns (1568) — [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015)
    - [Hook] A forgery or authorship puzzle inside the Cathedral.

14. **La mujer herrada** (COL; LEG). Peza/Riva Palacio's "most unsettling" piece: a priest breaks his vows; a blacksmith is summoned at midnight to shoe a mule; the priest's lover is found dead, "herrada como bestia", shod like a beast (my gloss: horseshoes nailed to her hands and feet) — [UNAM Global](https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/tradiciones-leyendas-mexicanas-peza-riva-palacio/)
    - [Hook] Body-horror case for a forge district; a demonic coachman enemy.

15. **Calle del Puente de Alvarado** (COL; debunked legend). Pedro de Alvarado's pole-vault over the canal on the Noche Triste (30 June 1520). González Obregón and Marroquí found no evidence and say he crossed on a fallen beam; the avenue was renamed México-Tenochtitlan in May 2021 (post-1930 change) — [El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/metropoli/por-esto-la-calle-puente-de-alvarado-ahora-se-llama-mexico-tenochtitlan/); [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015)
    - [Hook] The "Leap" as a parkour route named in-world; a conquistador ghost who keeps re-enacting the jump.

16. **Callejón de la Danza** (COL; LEG). Between República de Uruguay and República de El Salvador; under the viceroyalty said to be a meeting point for *brujos* and *curanderos* — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/leyendas-de-callejones-en-cdmx/)
    - [Hook] A night-market hub for the curandero faction.

17. **La Posada del Sol** (COL/MESO; LEG). Doña Felipa pays a nahual to bewitch a pillow she gives her daughter-in-law, who dies months later — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/leyendas-de-callejones-en-cdmx/)
    - [Hook] A cursed-object forensics case: cut open the pillow (in folklore, feather "crowns" or worms are found inside).

18. **Basilica ghosts** (MOD; LEG). A woman with an unquenchable candle walks from the old basilica to the new one through walls; and the old basilica's bells keep ringing on schedule after the death of a devoted chaplain — [es-academic](https://es-academic.com/dic.nsf/eswiki/722014)
    - [Hook] Bells on schedule as a navigation and timing puzzle.

19. **La Planchada** (MOD; LEG; probably post-1930). The ghost of Eulalia, a nurse at the Hospital Juárez (formerly Hospital de San Pablo), jilted by a Dr. Joaquín, who appears in a perfectly ironed uniform to care for gravely ill patients. Most versions date the story to the 1930s-1950s — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/cultura/leyenda-de-la-planchada/)
    - [Hook] A benevolent ghost who heals the player at night in a hospital level. Back-date her to a 1910s Revolution-era death.

20. **Calle de la Olla, Casa de la Bola, Casa de los Perros**: see Gaps. Note that the famous **Casa de los Perros** with dog and lion statues is in **Guadalajara**, not Mexico City (Escandón family; now the Museo del Periodismo y las Artes Gráficas) — [Azteca Jalisco](https://www.aztecajalisco.com/turismo/leyendas-jalisco-donde-es-la-casa-los-perros/)

21. **The Señor del Veneno** (COL; LEG). A crucifix, originally pale, that turned black after absorbing poison smeared on its feet to kill a priest who kissed them. Originally in the church of Porta Coeli (República de El Salvador); moved to the Cathedral's Altar del Perdón in 1935 (that move is post-1930; in the 1920s it would still be at Porta Coeli) — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/expresiones/leyendas-populares-mexicanas-senor-del-veneno/1726048); [ACI Prensa](https://www.aciprensa.com/noticias/71000/el-senor-del-veneno-la-imagen-mas-venerada-de-la-catedral-de-mexico-es-restaurada)
    - [Hook] An antidote relic: poison mechanics, and a holy object that drains toxins (or curses) into itself and darkens with each use.

22. **La Monja Alférez — Catalina / Antonio de Erauso** (COL; HIST with legend). Escaped a Basque convent (1600), fought as a man in Chile, revealed female sex in 1623 to avoid execution, got papal leave to dress as a man, and by 1630 lived in New Spain as a muleteer between Veracruz and Mexico City, dying c. 1650 near Orizaba — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalina_de_Erauso)
    - [Hook] A duelling ghost or a legendary swordmaster-carrier, the patron of a muleteer and courier guild; the melee tutorial ghost.

23. **William Lamport / Guillén de Lampart** (COL; HIST). The Irishman who came to New Spain in 1640 and plotted (c. 1641-42) an independent kingdom with equal representation for Indigenous, Black and creole people. Held by the Inquisition for 17 years, he escaped on Christmas Eve 1650 and pasted denunciations of the Inquisition around the city centre instead of fleeing. Burned in 1659 (accounts say he strangled himself on his iron collar first). Often called an inspiration for Zorro via Riva Palacio's novel — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lamport)
    - [Hook] A masked agitator legacy; the player finds Lamport's prison graffiti and Latin psalms in the Inquisition palace as a cipher trail.

### Inferences
- Writers can build a "**González Obregón's notebook**" diegetic codex: the 1922 book is new in the game's year, and an in-world chronicler NPC modelled on him (he was a living Centro chronicler) could hand out cases.
- Several legends share a *revenant justice* engine (Puente del Clérigo, Don Juan Manuel's angels, Callejón del Muerto), which suggests a game rule: the dead punish oath-breakers and murderers, and the player's stealth or non-lethal choices could be judged by the dead.
- Calle names changed under the 1920s republic (colonial names to "República de ..."), a natural mechanic: old maps vs new maps, with ghosts only answering to colonial street names.

### Gaps
- I could not get the full table of contents of *Las calles de México* (the OCR view truncated after about 7 chapters); the writers should browse the archive.org full text directly.
- No reliable source found for a **"Calle de la Olla"** legend or a **Casa de la Bola** (Tacubaya) ghost legend; treat as unverified.
- No sourced version found of a Mexico City **cursed mirror** legend or "duelling ghosts" legend specifically; not included.
- The exact current street for Calle de la Quemada and Machincuepa varies by source.

## 2. Mesoamerican myth and beings

### Takeaway
Nahua cosmology gives a ready-made underworld (nine-level Mictlán with a dog guide), a god of mirrors and sorcery (Tezcatlipoca, whose obsidian mirrors physically reached Elizabethan England), an apocalypse calendar (the 52-year cycle, Tzitzimimeh, New Fire), and crossroads ghosts (Cihuateteo). All are documented in the Florentine Codex and colonial codices; much survives physically under the Centro (Templo Mayor, Huey Tzompantli).

### Cited Findings

1. **Mictlán** (MESO). The underworld of nine levels; the passage takes four years; Xolotl guides souls; obstacles include mountains that crash together, a wind of flesh-cutting knives (obsidian), and a river guarded by beasts. It is ruled by **Mictlantecuhtli** and **Mictecacíhuatl**. Not everyone goes there: warriors and the sacrificed go east with the sun; women dead in childbirth go west; those killed by water or lightning go to Tlalocan — [Wikipedia, Mictlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mictlan)
   - Level names (they vary by source): Itzcuintlán / Apanohuaya (dogs and the river), Tepeme Monamictlán (the clashing mountains), Itztépetl (the obsidian mountain), Cehueloyan (the snow, where the dead remember their saddest moments), Itzehecayan (the knife wind), Teocoyolehualoyan, Apanhuiayo, Chiconahuapan, and Mictlán proper — [El Universal English](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/english/mythological-journey-mictlan-aztec-underworld/); [TheCollector](https://www.thecollector.com/descent-nine-levels-aztec-underworld/)
   - [Hook] A dream or near-death level structure: nine layered sub-levels under the city (sewers, crypts, the drained lake bed), each with one of these trials.

2. **Xólotl and the xoloitzcuintle** (MESO). A dog-headed psychopomp god whose animal forms are the xoloitzcuintle dog and the axolotl; a dog helps the dead cross the river (Apanohuaya), and dogs were sacrificed to accompany their masters — [Wikipedia, Xolotl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xolotl)
   - [Hook] A hairless-dog companion who can see ghosts and nahuales; sniffs out tonalli trails.

3. **Tezcatlipoca, "Smoking Mirror"** (MESO). God of the night sky, sorcery, discord, rulership, jaguars and divination, shown with an obsidian mirror in his headdress or in place of a foot. Diviners gazed into polished obsidian mirrors (*tezcatl*) to travel "to the world of gods and ancestors"; after the conquest such mirrors were set into crucifixes or remounted as emblems of vanity — [Getty, Through the Obsidian Mirror](https://getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/obsidian_mirror/through_the_mirror.html); [Wikipedia, Tezcatlipoca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tezcatlipoca)
4. **John Dee's Aztec mirror** (MESO → Elizabethan occult; HIST). X-ray fluorescence (published in *Antiquity*, 2021) showed that the obsidian of the "spirit mirror" used by Elizabeth I's astrologer John Dee came from the **Pachuca** source in central Mexico, i.e., it is Aztec-made; the British Museum acquired related obsidian objects from Mexico in the 1800s — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/john-dee-spirit-mirror-aztec); [The Past](https://the-past.com/news/john-dees-magic-mirror-has-aztec-origins-study-confirms/); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/magic-mirror-elizabethan-court-mystical-aztec-origin)
   - [Hook] Central artefact or MacGuffin: a "sister mirror" to Dee's. Mechanic: scry through obsidian to see a room's past, or to spot guards through reflections; a Tezcatlipoca cult that collects mirrors (and an English occultist rival faction).

5. **Tzitzimimeh (star demons)** (MESO). Female skeletal celestial deities in skull-decorated skirts, labelled "demons" by colonial writers. They were feared to descend and devour people during solar eclipses, at the end of the 52-year cycle, and during the five unlucky Nemontemi days; led by Itzpapálotl from Tamoanchan; also protectors of midwives and pregnant women — [Wikipedia, Tzitzimitl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzitzimitl)
6. **New Fire ceremony (Xiuhmolpilli, "binding of the years")** (MESO; HIST). Every 52 years all fires were put out; priests climbed Huixachtécatl (today's **Cerro de la Estrella, Iztapalapa, Mexico City**) to watch the Pleiades and drill a new fire, seen across the valley. Pregnant women wore maguey masks lest the Tzitzimimeh turn them into monsters, and children wore masks and were kept awake lest they turn into rats — [World History Encyclopedia](https://worldhistory.org/article/866); [INAH, burial of the years](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/5370); [Wikipedia, Cerro de la Estrella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_de_la_Estrella_(archaeological_site)); there is a Museo del Fuego Nuevo on site — [CDMX tourism](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/fuego-nuevo-museum/)
   - [Hook] Main-plot clock. A secret cult counts its own 52-year cycle (e.g., ending in the game year); all lamps in the city must go out on the night (a perfect stealth night) and the cult must kindle a new fire on Cerro de la Estrella. The player can douse fires to call down or deny the Tzitzimimeh.

7. **Cihuateteo** (MESO). Spirits of women who died in childbirth (counted as fallen warriors); they live in Cihuatlampa in the west and escort the sun from noon to sunset. On five dates (1 Deer, 1 Rain, 1 Monkey, 1 House, 1 Eagle) they descend, haunt crossroads, steal children, cause seizures and madness, and seduce men; roadside shrines appeased them. Warriors stole the left middle finger and hair of such women as battle charms — [Wikipedia, Cihuateteo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cihuateteo)
   - [Hook] Crossroads enemies on specific in-game calendar nights; finger relics as illegal amulets traded by soldiers or gangsters.

8. **Cihuacóatl** (MESO). The "serpent woman" goddess, identified by Sahagún's informants with the woman heard weeping at night in Tenochtitlan in 1509 ("My children, where shall I take you?"), the sixth omen before the conquest; tied to infanticide and carrying an empty cradle — [Library of Congress Folklife](https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2021/10/la-llorona-roots-branches-and-the-missing-link-from-spain/); [México Desconocido](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/el-origen-mexicano-de-la-llorona.html)

9. **Nahualli / Nagual and tonalli** (MESO → SYN). The *tonalli* is a day-sign soul fixed by birth date; a *nahualli* is a person who can shapeshift into their animal counterpart (jaguars and pumas for powerful practitioners; in later folklore dogs, owls, bats, turkeys). In colonial and modern rural Mexico "nagual" became a synonym for a blood-drinking *brujo*, while in Indigenous communities naguales can be respected healers and protectors — [Wikipedia, Nagual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagual)
   - [Hook] Player power: brief animal-form scouting (owl for rooftops, dog for scent). Enemies: nahual assassins detectable only by a xolo dog or a mirror.

10. **Martín Ocelotl** (MESO; HIST). A Nahua priest-healer ("Ocelotl" = jaguar; c. 1496-1537?) tried for idolatry under Bishop Juan de Zumárraga's apostolic inquisition (Zumárraga named inquisitor in June 1535, active 1536-43); Mexica royal treasures he guarded were confiscated, and he was banished to Spain (his reputation as a shapeshifting nahualli is part of the brief and of popular retellings; not confirmed in the summary fetched) — [Wikipedia, Martín Ocelotl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mart%C3%ADn_Ocelotl); [LACMA, "Jewels of Martín Océlotl" (2021 copy)](https://www.lacma.org/node/40793)
    - [Hook] His confiscated jewels as a heist target; invent that his exile ship was "lost at sea" and he returned as a jaguar.

11. **Ahuízotl** (MESO). A dog-sized water creature with black, rubbery, slick fur and a human hand at the end of its tail (Florentine Codex). It lurks in deep pools, lures victims by crying like a baby or by stirring up fish, drowns them, and eats only the eyes, nails and teeth. Only priests could handle its victims' bodies, which went to Tlalocan — [Wikipedia, Ahuizotl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahuizotl_(creature))
    - [Hook] A canal and flooded-crypt enemy that baits players with an infant's cry (pairs with La Llorona audio mis-direction). The coroner's "eyes, nails and teeth missing" clue begins a case.

12. **Chaneques** (MESO/Olmec-Nahua; Veracruz especially). Small sprite-like guardians of dangerous places (rivers, caves, forests) who can frighten a soul out of the body (*susto* / soul loss, needing ritual recovery); recorded in Inquisition documents; related to Spanish *duendes* and Yucatec *aluxes* — [Wikipedia, Chaneque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaneque)
    - [Hook] Mischief "pickpocket" spirits in parks and canals who steal items. Curanderos sell soul-recovery ("limpia") services that restore a lost stat.

13. **Huey Tzompantli** (MESO; HIST). The great skull rack of Tenochtitlan. Its remains (over 650 skulls, part of a skull tower) were announced in 2015 in the Templo Mayor zone behind the Cathedral (Calle República de Guatemala). Women and children were among the victims, though about 75% were men. Andrés de Tapia's count of 136,000 skulls is considered inflated. Discovery is post-1930 — [Wikipedia, Huey Tzompantli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huey_Tzompantli)
    - [Hook] In the alt-1920s, a building excavation behind the Cathedral breaks into a skull wall, the inciting incident of an "underground Tenochtitlan" district.

14. **Xipe Tótec and Tlacaxipehualiztli** (MESO). "Our Lord the Flayed", a god of agriculture, renewal, goldsmiths and disease; in the festival Tlacaxipehualiztli captives were killed (some in gladiatorial combat), flayed, and priests wore their skins for days. A temple to Xipe was found at Ndachjian-Tehuacán, Puebla, in 2019 — [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/01/temple-pre-hispanic-flayed-god-unearthed-mexico-190104165959125.html); [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/archaeologists-uncover-temple-of-the-flayed-lord/)
    - [Hook] A skin-wearing cult of "renewal" (disguise magic: wear another's skin or face; the Dishonored-style "possession" verb). Use with care, through implication.

### Inferences
- The 52-year New Fire plus the Tzitzimimeh plus Cerro de la Estrella (a real location inside the city) give a complete main-plot frame grounded in the city's own geography.
- Obsidian links Tezcatlipoca, Dee's mirror and Mictlán's obsidian mountain, so a single "obsidian" magic school (seeing, reflecting, cutting) can unify the game's light magic.

### Gaps
- I did not verify a Mexicolore page for the level-by-level trials of Mictlán; level names vary across popular sources (they ultimately come from the Florentine Codex Book 3 and the Codex Vaticanus A), so writers should treat them as flexible.
- Direct links to the Digital Florentine Codex (Getty, 2023) were not fetched in this session.

## 3. Later and regional folklore usable in a city setting

### Takeaway
Most post-conquest bogeymen are rural or regional (the north, Central America, Veracruz, Yucatán), so in a city game they work best as migrants' beliefs or as urbanised versions. Several famous items are post-1930 (Isla de las Muñecas, public Santa Muerte, the Catemaco Black Mass, La Planchada, *La Mano Peluda* as a radio show) and must be back-dated deliberately or avoided.

### Cited Findings
1. **La Lechuza / witch-owls** (MESO+COL; LEG). A witch who becomes a large owl (in Tejano tellings, a woman with an owl face); owls were omens of death ("Cuando el tecolote canta, el indio muere") and Aztec owls were linked to Mictlantecuhtli — [USC Folklore Archives](https://folklore.usc.edu/?p=80931); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/video/la-lechuza-the-shape-shifting-witch-owl-o3iten/)
   - [Hook] Rooftop sentries that cry out to warn guards; the owl's call as an alarm mechanic.
2. **El Charro Negro** (SYN; LEG). A black-clad horseman on night roads who offers a bag of gold; to accept is to sell your soul. One origin has a poor youth who sold his soul for wealth. Origins are northern, with the legend consolidating around the 1920s per México Desconocido — [México Desconocido](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/la-leyenda-del-charro-negro.html); [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/noticias/leyenda-del-charro-negro-historia-de-terror-y-origen-de-una-bebida-mexicana/)
   - [Hook] The devil's moneylender: a fence or loan shark who buys loot with cursed coin; the player's purse can be "tainted".
3. **El Cadejo** (Maya/Nahua-rooted; Central America and southern Mexico). A white dog that protects night travellers and a black one with ember-red eyes and dragging chains that kills them; speaking to it can cause madness — [Wikipedia, Cadejo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadejo)
   - [Hook] Paired guardian and hunter dogs for drunks and night walkers; a karma-tied escort.
4. **La Mano Peluda** (folklore; urban-legend form mostly modern). A disembodied hairy hand, in one version that of a greedy man and in another of a man wrongly mutilated for theft, returning for revenge. It is famous in Mexico City through a 1990s radio programme of the same name (post-1930) — [Chilango, Casa Negra de la Roma](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/leyenda-de-la-casa-negra-en-la-roma/); [Colombia.com](https://www.colombia.com/colombia-info/folclor-y-tradiciones/leyendas/la-mano-peluda/)
5. **Chaneques, duendes, aluxes**: see section 2, item 12 — [Wikipedia, Chaneque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaneque)
6. **La Pascualita** (MOD; LEG; Chihuahua, 1930). A mannequin in the window of the bridal shop La Popular in Chihuahua said to be the embalmed corpse of the owner's daughter, who died of a spider or scorpion bite on her wedding eve; witnesses report it moving. The 1930 start date is widely cited but not confirmed by the source fetched — [Wikipedia, La Pascualita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Pascualita)
   - [Hook] A corpse-bride mannequin in a department-store window on Avenida Madero; a taxidermy and embalming cult.
7. **Isla de las Muñecas** (MOD; **post-1930**). Xochimilco canals. Julián Santana Barrera began hanging dolls in the mid-20th century, reportedly for a drowned girl (no evidence the girl existed); he died in 2001. The area appears in the 1943 film *María Candelaria* — [Wikipedia, Island of the Dolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_of_the_Dolls)
   - [Hook] Only as an inspired, back-dated invention, e.g., a chinampa hermit's doll island as a ward against the ahuízotl.
8. **Santa Muerte** (SYN; HIST). 18th-century Inquisition records describe people addressing a skeletal figure as "Santa Muerte" and threatening it if miracles failed. American anthropologists first reported private devotion in working-class Mexico City (notably **Tepito**) in the **1940s**. The cult went public only with Enriqueta Romero's street shrine on Calle Alfarería, Tepito, in **2001**, after 1998 press linking it to crime; colour-coded candles (white, red, gold, black); condemned by the Church — [Wikipedia, Santa Muerte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Muerte)
   - [Hook] In the 1920s keep it a clandestine household devotion in Tepito: a secret, criminal-protecting death saint, with the candle colours as a petition system.
9. **San La Muerte** (Guaraní SYN; **not Mexican**). A male skeletal folk saint of Paraguay, NE Argentina and S Brazil after the 1767 Jesuit expulsion; amulets are sometimes inserted under the skin — [Wikipedia, San La Muerte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_La_Muerte)
   - [Hook] A foreign cult brought by an Argentine émigré, a rival to the local death devotion.
10. **Día de Muertos and calaveras** (SYN). José Guadalupe Posada (d. 1913) created the **Calavera Garbancera** (1912) for publisher Antonio Vanegas Arroyo's satirical calavera broadsheets, which "killed" living people in mock-epitaph verse; the name "La Catrina" came later — [El Universal, who invented the Catrina](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/cultura/artes-visuales/quien-invento-la-catrina/); [Clark Art Institute, Posada's Calaveras](https://www.clarkart.edu/microsites/posada/about-the-exhibition/posadas-calaveras); [Revista Caracol (USP), calaveras from the Porfiriato to the Revolution](https://revistas.usp.br/caracol/article/view/227525)
    - [Hook] Calavera broadsheets as in-world newspapers and quest boards: satirical death verses that predict (or cause) who dies next; a printer faction.
11. **Quema de Judas** (COL→SYN). On Holy Saturday (Sábado de Gloria) papier-mâché Judas effigies stuffed with fireworks were exploded in plazas; a festive and satirical tradition (effigies of hated figures). A 1957 fireworks-warehouse explosion at La Merced led to a near-total ban in the capital (post-1930), and the Linares *cartonero* family of Merced Balbuena kept the tradition alive — [Roads & Kingdoms](https://roadsandkingdoms.com/2018/la-quema-dejudas-mexico-city/); scholarly context in María del Carmen Vázquez Mantecón, *Cohetes de regocijo* (UNAM-IIH, 2017) — [UNAM IIH chapter](https://historicas.unam.mx/publicaciones/publicadigital/libros/cohetes/682_04_14_Judas.pdf)
    - [Hook] A Holy Saturday mission: swap a firework Judas for one that carries a bomb meant for a politician (and in 1920s, effigies of Calles or of priests would be explosive politics).
12. **El Sombrerón**, **Aluxes**: covered only through related entries (Chaneque page notes aluxes as the Yucatec equivalent) — [Wikipedia, Chaneque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaneque)

### Inferences
- A 1920s Mexico City had heavy internal migration after the Revolution, so regional beings (Charro Negro from the north, Cadejo from the south, chaneques from Veracruz) can plausibly appear as beliefs carried into tenement *vecindades*, each district with its own imported monsters.
- Posada's calaveras give an authentic period visual language for UI, loading screens and in-world printed matter.

### Gaps
- **Ánimas Solas** (the lone soul in purgatory, a woman in chains among flames, used in folk magic): the Wikipedia page URL I tried returned 404; not verified in this session.
- **El Sombrerón**: no source fetched (mainly a Guatemalan/Chiapas legend of a tiny man in a huge hat who braids horses' manes and serenades girls; unverified here).
- Velorio customs (wakes) and 1920s-specific ofrenda practice: not researched in depth.

## 4. Religion, Inquisition and church horror

### Takeaway
The Holy Office sat in a still-standing palace on Plaza de Santo Domingo, burned people beside the Alameda, and is a documented, citable horror engine (about 50 executions over 1571-1820). The 1926-29 Cristero conflict put Mexico City itself under a regime of closed churches, clandestine masses, a dynamited basilica (1921), a photographed martyr (1927) and a presidential assassination by a sketch artist (1928): a ready-made gothic political backdrop exactly in the game's decade.

### Cited Findings

**The Holy Office in New Spain**
- An episcopal inquisition operated from 1536 under Zumárraga; the formal Tribunal of the Holy Office ran 1571-1820 (abolished 1812, restored 1813-1820). An estimated ~50 people were executed (29 "Judaizers" 1571-1700). Its main targets were crypto-Jews, witchcraft and sorcery, scholars with new scientific ideas, and homosexuality, rather than Indigenous people. Major autos de fe: the Carvajal family (1596, nine executions); 11 April 1649 (twelve crypto-Jews executed, Treviño de Sobremonte burned alive); 1659 (William Lamport) — [Wikipedia, Mexican Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Inquisition)
- **Palacio de la Inquisición** (now the Palacio de la Escuela de Medicina, UNAM), Plaza de Santo Domingo: the tribunal was there from 1571; the present building dates from 1732-36 (architect Pedro de Arrieta); it had hearing rooms, "El Secreto" offices, secret chambers, perpetual prisons and inquisitors' quarters. Autos de fe processed from Santo Domingo to the Plaza Mayor — [CDMX tourism, Palacio de la Escuela de Medicina](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/palace-school-medicine/?lang=en)
- **The Quemadero**: burning place by the church of San Hipólito, beside the Alameda; burnings 1596-1771; executions in the atrium of the convent of San Diego (now Laboratorio Arte Alameda, Doctor Mora 1) — [HMdb marker](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=121057); [Chilango, the dark side of the Inquisition](https://www.chilango.com/general/el-lado-mas-oscuro-de-la-inquisicion/)
  - [Hook] The 1920s medical school occupies the old Inquisition palace: anatomy theatre above, sealed inquisitorial cells below. A body-snatching case that leads into the Secreto archive.
  - [Hook] The Alameda as a haunted park where fire spirits (the burned) rise on auto-de-fe anniversaries.

**Church crypts, mummies, relics**
- **Momias del Carmen (San Ángel, Mexico City)**: c. 1916-17 Zapatista troops occupying the former Carmelite college dug in the crypt for treasure and exposed naturally mummified bodies; locals stopped the friars from reburying them; 12 are displayed at the Museo de El Carmen; their identities are unknown — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/trending/museo-del-carmen-visitar-momias-reales-en-cdmx/1681398); [INAH, Museo de El Carmen](https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4241); [Milenio](https://www.milenio.com/cultura/momias-san-angel-misterio-museo-carmen)
  - [Hook] Period-perfect: a crypt freshly broken open by revolutionaries less than a decade before the game; nameless mummies as witnesses a medium can question.
- **Mummies of Guanajuato** (not Mexico City). Exhumed from 1865 when families did not pay the burial tax; naturally mummified; museum founded 1969; inspired Bradbury, a Santo lucha film (1970s) and Herzog's *Nosferatu* — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummies_of_Guanajuato)
  - [Hook] A travelling "momia" sideshow touring a Mexico City carpa (tent theatre).
- **Señor del Veneno** (see section 1, item 21): relic that absorbed poison; Porta Coeli, moved to the Cathedral in 1935 — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/expresiones/leyendas-populares-mexicanas-senor-del-veneno/1726048)

**The Cristero War (1926-29) in and around Mexico City**
- The Calles Law was signed June 1926; the war began with fighting in Guadalajara on 3 August 1926; a rebellion manifesto was issued on 1 January 1927; the *arreglos* brokered by US ambassador Dwight Morrow ended it on 21 June 1929; about 90,000 died — [Wikipedia, Cristero War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cristero_War)
- **Basilica bombing, 14 November 1921**: Luciano Pérez Carpio, an employee of the presidential private secretariat, hid dynamite in flowers at the altar of the Virgin of Guadalupe. The blast twisted a 34-kg bronze crucifix but left the tilma and its glass untouched; the "Santo Cristo del Atentado" is still displayed — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/el-cristo-que-protegio-a-la-virgen-de-guadalupe-se-cumplen-hoy-100-anos-del-atentado)
- **Miguel Pro**: Jesuit who ran an underground ministry in Mexico City from 1926 in disguise, celebrating secret masses (signing letters "Cocol"); after a November 1927 bomb attempt on Obregón he was arrested and shot without trial on 23 November 1927, crying "¡Viva Cristo Rey!"; Calles had the execution photographed as a warning, which backfired; about 60,000 attended the funeral; beatified 1988 — [Wikipedia, Miguel Pro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Pro)
- **Obregón's assassination**: 17 July 1928 at La Bombilla restaurant, San Ángel, by José de León Toral, who posed as a caricaturist sketching guests before shooting him. The nun Concepción Acevedo de la Llata ("Madre Conchita") was accused of inciting it (20-year sentence). Toral was shot on 9 February 1929; there are questions over multiple weapons — [Wikipedia, José de León Toral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_de_Le%C3%B3n_Toral)
- Calles was a Scottish Rite Mason, honoured by Mexican masonry; Catholic peasants in the 1920s-30s denounced a perceived link between government, teachers, anticlericalism and the Masons — [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/freemasonry-mexican-history-secret-plain-sight/); [Wikipedia, Freemasonry in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry_in_Mexico)
  - [Hook] Factions: Catholic underground (secret masses in safehouses, priests in disguise as stealth NPCs to escort), anticlerical state police, and Masonic lodges. A sketch artist with a gun is a ready assassination-mission template.

### Inferences
- An alternate 1920s with churches closed but the Inquisition's architecture intact supports a "sealed sacred spaces" mechanic: churches are locked state property at night, entered by stealth, with relics guarded by both police and the devout.
- The Cristero conflict lets writers avoid a simple good/evil church: martyrs, bomb-throwers, persecuted nuns and Masonic officials can all be sympathetic or sinister.

### Gaps
- Incorruptible saints on display in Mexico City, cilice and penitent practice in 1920s Mexico City, and specific ossuaries (beyond El Carmen) were not researched in this session.
- The sanbenito display practice in Mexico City's cathedral (hung sanbenitos naming the condemned) was not verified with a source here.

## 5. Occultism and spiritism in early 20th-century Mexico

### Takeaway
Spiritism was mainstream among Mexico City's liberal elite from the 1870s, with a national congress in 1906. The president who launched the Revolution, Francisco I. Madero, was a practising writing medium who wrote a spiritist manual under a pseudonym, and Calles reportedly attended séances. Theosophy grew out of the capital's spiritist circles; Freemasonry was the language of politics. Folk healing (Niño Fidencio, Catemaco brujos) supplies the popular counterpart. Even Juan de Dios Peza, co-author of the legends, was a spiritist.

### Cited Findings
- Spiritism was known in Mexico from 1858. *La Ilustración Espírita* was published in Mexico City by General Refugio I. González (1872-79) and later his son Moisés (1888-92). Named adherents include José María Vigil, Rafael Reyes Spíndola, **Juan de Dios Peza**, Santiago Sierra, Pedro Castera, José Martí and Madero. A public debate with positivists was held at the Liceo Hidalgo on 5 April 1875. Practices included talking tables and spirit photography — [Campos Hernández, *Analéctica* 2015](http://portal.amelica.org/ameli/journal/251/2511186004/html/)
- The **Sociedad Espírita Central de la República Mexicana** promoted Kardec's doctrine; a **Congreso Nacional Espírita** was held in Mexico City from 24 March to mid-April 1906, with Madero taking part; many spiritists were also Masons, and spiritism overlapped with Theosophy — [search summary of UNAM/Redalyc sources; see e.g. Revista Argos](https://revistaargos.cucsh.udg.mx/index.php/argos/article/view/5); [Milenio, Madero the spiritist](https://amp.milenio.com/politica/madero-y-el-espiritismo-batalla-que-libro-antes-de-la-revolucion)
- **Madero**: discovered Kardec in a magazine among his father's books, attended séances in Europe, and believed he had "mechanical" (automatic) writing. Spirit guides included his dead brother Raúl (who made him vegetarian and teetotal), his uncle José Ramiro, and Benito Juárez (signing "BJ"), who by his account urged the 1910 revolution. He wrote the *Manual espírita* under the pseudonym **Bhîma**; it was translated by C. M. Mayo. Presidents Calles and Miguel Alemán allegedly attended séances — [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/president-talked-to-ghosts-madero-spiritism/); [Revista Argos article](https://revistaargos.cucsh.udg.mx/index.php/argos/article/view/5); [SPR Psi Encyclopedia, Madero](https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/francisco-i-madero/)
- **Theosophy**: the first Mexican circles grew from the capital's spiritist groups; the first lodge, "Aura", was founded in 1906 under the Cuban Section; the Mexican Section was recognised on 12 November 1919 — [theosophy.world](https://theosophy.world/es/node/2191)
- **Freemasonry**: first lodge c. 1806; Scottish Rite (escoceses = conservatives) vs York Rite (yorkinos = liberals) in the early republic; many presidents were Masons — [Wikipedia, Freemasonry in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry_in_Mexico)
- **Niño Fidencio** (1898-1938): healer at Espinazo, Nuevo León, famous for painless operations without anaesthesia, for throwing objects at crowds from a pepper tree (those hit were healed) and for a sulphurous mud pool. President Calles visited on 8 February 1928. Followers (fidencistas) channel him through mediums called *cajitas* or *materias*; he is a folk saint, not recognised by the Church — [Wikipedia, Niño Fidencio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%B1o_Fidencio)
- **Catemaco brujería** (Veracruz): known for its community of brujos. Gonzalo Aguirre Pech became famous in the 1950s and claimed a devil's pact on Cerro Mono Blanco. The first-Friday-of-March "Misa Negra" gathering was founded in the 1970s (post-1930) — [Wikipedia, Catemaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catemaco); [México Desconocido](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/el-dia-de-los-brujos-catemaco.html)
- **Stage magic**: David Bamberg ("Fu Manchu") settled in Mexico City in 1934-35 (post-1930) and became one of the most popular magicians in Latin America; the Teatro Lírico of 1920-40 specialised in vernacular revue — [Wikipedia, David Bamberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bamberg); [INAH *Historias* article on 1920-40 entertainment](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/historias/article/download/14447/15529/29321)

**Hooks (inferences)**
- [Hook] A "Madero's séance table" artefact: the dead president's automatic-writing notebooks are a quest chain; whoever holds them can consult the spirits of Juárez and Madero (dialogue-heavy ghost NPCs).
- [Hook] Factions: the Kardecist Society (bourgeois, scientific mediums with spirit cameras), the Theosophical Lodge "Aura" (orientalist occultists), rival Masonic rites (escoceses vs yorkinos as rival secret societies running the police and the ministries), fidencista healer-mediums, and curanderos from the Tuxtlas.
- [Hook] Mechanic: spirit photography as a detective tool (photograph a room to reveal ghosts or past events).

### Inferences
- Spiritism and liberal anticlericalism were allied in Mexico, so in the 1920s the state's séance-going Masons persecuting the Church is historically grounded and gives morally grey occult villains or allies.

### Gaps
- The *Manual espírita*'s publication year (commonly given as 1911) was not confirmed in the sources fetched.
- I found no sourced detail on Rosicrucian orders in 1920s Mexico City, on mesmerists or hypnotists performing in 1920s Mexico City theatres, or on named 1920s Mexico City mediums.

## 6. Ancient stones reburied, relics and cursed objects

### Takeaway
The best-documented "disturbing stone" story is real: the Coatlicue statue, dug up in the Zócalo in 1790, was secretly venerated by Indigenous people at night, so university authorities reburied it, dug it up for Humboldt (c. 1803-04) and buried it again. It is a perfect template for an "idol that will not stay buried" plot.

### Cited Findings
- **Coatlicue**: discovered 13 August 1790 during paving of the Plaza Mayor (Zócalo). Viceroy Revillagigedo sent it to the Royal and Pontifical University as an antiquity; Indigenous people came furtively at odd hours with offerings, candles, flowers and incense; the university reburied it in the same place, fearing it would rekindle old beliefs (and finding it an affront to beauty). It was dug up again for Humboldt and then reburied — [UNAM Big Bang Mex, Coatlicue](https://bigbangmex.unam.mx/en/coatlicue/antecedentes/); [Wikipedia, Coatlicue statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coatlicue_statue); León y Gama documented both stones in 1792 — [Wikipedia, Coatlicue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coatlicue)
  - [Hook] A goddess stone that the city keeps reburying under the Zócalo; each excavation (tram lines, sewers) brings it up and a night cult forms. The player can rebury it, reveal it or steal it.
- **The Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol)**: found in the same paving works (1790) and described by León y Gama in *Descripción histórica y cronológica de las dos piedras* (1792) — [Wikipedia, Coatlicue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coatlicue)
- **John Dee's mirror** (see section 2, item 4): an Aztec obsidian mirror that became an English occult relic — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/john-dee-spirit-mirror-aztec)
- **Martín Ocelotl's confiscated Mexica treasures** (see section 2, item 10) — [LACMA](https://www.lacma.org/node/40793)
- **Santo Cristo del Atentado** (twisted bronze crucifix from the 1921 Basilica bombing) — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/el-cristo-que-protegio-a-la-virgen-de-guadalupe-se-cumplen-hoy-100-anos-del-atentado)
- **Señor del Veneno** (a crucifix blackened by poison) — [Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/expresiones/leyendas-populares-mexicanas-senor-del-veneno/1726048)
- **Cihuateteo finger relics** (left middle finger and hair taken from women dead in childbirth, as warriors' charms) — [Wikipedia, Cihuateteo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cihuateteo)
- **The Posada del Sol pillow** (bewitched gift that kills) — [Chilango](https://www.chilango.com/ocio/leyendas-de-callejones-en-cdmx/)

### Inferences
