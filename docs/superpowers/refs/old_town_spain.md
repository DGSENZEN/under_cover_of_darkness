# The Old Town: what Spanish old towns add (Spain references)

Research pack for the Old Town district of "the city on the rock": a dense, vertical maze of houses, stair-lanes, small squares and rooftops, climbing from the harbour's city wall to the cathedral square. This file covers the Spanish half of the Iberian mix; Porto and Lisbon are covered elsewhere. It extends `iberian.md` sections 2.2-2.5 (Albaicín, Toledo, Cáceres, Arcos) and does not repeat them except where it goes deeper. Compiled 2026-10-02. Research was read-only: nothing was downloaded on purpose, nothing was signed into or submitted.

## How to read this

## 1. Seville: the Barrio de Santa Cruz (the lane maze)

**What and why:** Seville's former *judería*, a tight knot of lanes and little squares pressed against the Alcázar's walls. It is the best Spanish model for the Old Town's ground plan: a walled quarter, gates, blind turns, lanes about a metre wide, and squares that are only widened junctions.

**Features**
- Jews settled in the Santa Cruz and San Bartolomé areas after Ferdinand III's conquest of 1248; after the community was dispersed in 1391, the old *judería* became three parishes ([es.wiki Santa Cruz](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrio_de_Santa_Cruz_(Sevilla))). The quarter had the second-largest Jewish population in Iberia, after Toledo's ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Cruz,_Seville)).
- The walled *judería* covered about 16 ha. Its wall ran from the Alcázar along the present Mateos Gago, Fabiola and Madre de Dios streets to the Puerta de la Carne, also called *Bab Chauar* or Puerta de Minjoar. The assault of 6 June 1391 killed about 4,000 people. Four former mosques had become synagogues; they became the churches of Santa María la Blanca, San Bartolomé, the old Santa Cruz and the Madre de Dios convent ([es.wiki Judería de Sevilla](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judería_de_Sevilla)).
- **The picturesque layer is 20th-century.** Between 1912 and 1920 the Marqués de la Vega-Inclán's reforms created squares in an "Andalusian style". Juan Talavera remodelled the Plaza de Santa Cruz in 1918 and the Plaza de Doña Elvira in 1924 ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrio_de_Santa_Cruz_(Sevilla))). Use the plan; treat the tiled benches and the gardens as optional.
- **Callejón del Agua** runs from the Plaza de Alfaro to Calle Vida, meeting Calle Pimienta and Justino de Neve. It runs right against the old city wall that bounds the Alcázar. The Caños de Carmona aqueduct water that fed the Alcázar ran nearby; a writer in 1839 called it the street next to the wall ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callejón_del_Agua)). Width and length: (not found).
- **Callejón de la Judería**, once the "postigo del Alcázar": from the Patio de Banderas it runs as a covered corridor with a timber ceiling, then an open stretch, then a low, double-angled vaulted space under an orthogonal dome on squinches. It is floored in brick laid on edge, passes a postern in a wall tower, and six small two-storey houses are wedged between the towers and the wall. It was renamed in 1943, and a fountain was added in 1942 ([ExplicArte Sevilla](http://explicartesevilla.blogspot.com/2021/09/un-paseo-por-la-calle-juderia.html), secondary).
- **Narrowest lanes:** Calle Mariscal 101 cm, Lope de Rueda 102 cm, Reinoso 106 cm, all in Santa Cruz ([Sevilla Secreta](https://sevillasecreta.co/sabes-cual-es-la-calle-mas-estrecha-de-sevilla/); [Visita Sevilla](https://visitasevilla.es/sabias-que-algunas-de-las-calles-de-sevilla-estan-entre-las-mas-estrechas-de-europa/), both secondary; the second says there is no official measurement). Reinoso is the "street of kisses": its facing balconies are said to be close enough to kiss across. It was named in 1840 (Sevilla Secreta).
- **Calle Susona**, formerly Calle de la Muerte, runs from the Plaza de Doña Elvira to Callejón del Agua. The legend of 1481: Susona betrayed her father's conspiracy, and by her own will her skull hung behind a grille over her door until 1845, when a tile replaced it ([search summary of several Seville sites](https://sevillaviajera.com/leyenda-de-la-calle-susona/), unverified).
- **Plaza de Doña Elvira:** the site of a 17th-century *corral de comedias* (a courtyard theatre); the square has a central fountain, tiled benches and orange trees (search summary of [andalucia.org](https://www.andalucia.org/listing/plaza-do%C3%B1a-elvira/18244102/), unverified).
- **Plaza de Santa Cruz:** the church that stood here (a synagogue until 1391; Murillo was buried in it in 1682) was demolished by the French in 1810. Today it is a rectangular square with a raised central garden reached by steps on each side, and orange trees. Its wrought-iron cross (1692, by Sebastián Conde; first set up at the corner of Sierpes and Rioja, moved here in 1921) has four lanterns on its base ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Santa_Cruz_(Sevilla))).
- **The Alcázar side:** the Jardines de Murillo (8,500 m², laid out 1911-1923) lie between the Alcázar wall and the outer ring road ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jardines_de_Murillo)). The wall itself runs as long blank stretches with towers and posterns: the Postigo de la Torre del Agua is a 12th-century Almohad gate ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Postigo_de_la_torre_del_Agua_de_Sevilla,_s._XII,_puerta_de_la_muralla_de_la_ciudad_almohade.jpg)).

**Model it**
- Kit: lane-wall modules at 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 and 3.0 m widths (design values bracketing the sourced 1.01-1.06 m minimum), set as house fronts of 2-3 storeys. Corners are rounded or chamfered, and every 3-5 modules the lane bends 15-40 degrees. Never let a sightline run more than 20-25 m (design).
- The city wall with its towers is one long kit along a lane: plain rendered faces 6-10 m high (design) with a postern module (a dark arched door in a tower base).
- The covered passage is three modules: a timber-ceilinged corridor (beams as geometry, 4-6 per module), an open stretch, and a vaulted double elbow (an 8-sided dome on 4 squinches, low-poly). Brick-on-edge floor as a single 256 px texture.
- The squares are widened junctions of 8-15 m (design), each with one hero prop: a fountain, a wrought-iron cross with lanterns, or orange trees as low-poly lollipops.

**Play it**
- Lanes of about 1 m mean a guard and the player cannot pass each other: contact is forced unless the player goes up. That makes balconies and rooftops the escape, and it is the core Santa Cruz loop.
- The wall-side lane (Callejón del Agua) is a long, quiet, dark run with a single exit at each end: a gauntlet when a patrol enters it.
- The covered double elbow is pure shadow and a perfect ambush spot, but it is also a sound trap: footsteps on brick under a dome carry.
- The Plaza de Santa Cruz cross's four lanterns make a lit island in a dark square: an obvious meeting point for the watch.

## 2. The patio house (casa-patio): Seville and Córdoba

**What and why:** the most important Spanish contribution to the district. The house turns a blank face to the lane, and life happens around an inner court seen through an iron gate. It is the natural "enterable house" for the Old Town: one door, a passage, a court, rooms off galleries, a stair, and a roof you can walk on.

**Features: origin and sequence**
- Mostly an Arab inheritance: in the Islamic house the court was reached indirectly, by a bend (*en recodo*), so it could not be seen from outside. Christian Seville added the **zaguán**, a passage linking street and court, and the streets changed from blind walls to a series of glimpses into lit courts (Guerra de Hoyos, [UAM](https://repositorio.uam.es/server/api/core/bitstreams/414e5246-9b2d-45c8-a872-ebceb38b0062/content)).
- *Zaguán* comes from Arabic *istawán* and is documented in Spanish from the 16th century (1551). In al-Andalus it was an angled passage from the street door to the patio; from it one reaches the columned patio holding the stair and the main rooms ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaguán)).
- **Cancela:** a wrought-iron gate that replaced the door between zaguán and patio, a see-through screen for ventilation and display. The first are dated to the Fernandine restoration (Ferdinand VII, after 1814), and they spread afterwards. They were made of forged iron joined with rivets and nails through the 19th century, then of cast iron from the 1880s ([Ollero 2022, p. 313](https://archivohispalense.dipusevilla.es/2022/2022_318-320_10.pdf)). Its job is "to let you see" while stopping passage. The zaguán and the patio are both tiled (*alicatado*) because of rising damp, and **only at night is the outer zaguán door, solid and opaque, closed** (Guerra de Hoyos).
- From the late 16th century, and above all in the 18th, owners opened a straight visual axis: portal, patio, garden. An attempt in 1805 to cut such an axis into the Alcázar was walled up again after public protest (Ollero 2022, p. 312).

**Features: the court**
- Columns carry round (*medio punto*) or segmental (*escarzano*) arches, sometimes both in one court. Upstairs are column galleries again, or balconies opening off corridors. Even good houses have **column-less patios** whose upper corridors rest on beams or metal brackets (*jabalcones*) (Ollero 2022, p. 314).
- Marble for columns and fountains; after the 1755 earthquake, brick replaced rammed earth (*tapial*) (Ollero 2022, p. 310). In the 19th century courts were roofed with glass *monteras*, sometimes coloured, and galleries were glazed (Ollero 2022, p. 319; Guerra de Hoyos). Awnings (*toldos, velas*) shaded the court at midday (Guerra de Hoyos).
- Dimensions of great houses, for scale: **Casa de Pilatos** covers about 10,000 m²; its main patio is 324 m² without the galleries, with 24 Genoese marble columns in two storeys; the *apeadero* (carriage court) is about 270 m², and the Genoese fountain of 1529 is 4.03 m tall ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_Pilatos_(Sevilla))). The **Casa de las Conchas** patio in Salamanca is about 18.80 x 16.80 m ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_las_Conchas)). The **Casa de los Pinelo** (early 16th c.) has arcades on three of its four sides on Carrara columns carved in Genoa around 1540, with an arcaded mirador upstairs ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Pinelo)).
- Ordinary-house patio sizes in Seville: (not found). Córdoba planning requires a main patio of at least **25% of the lot** for new houses on typologically protected plots (López Merino 2022, p. 117, citing the PEPCH, [dialnet](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/8746098.pdf)).
- **Bay depth:** a traditional Andalusian building bay (*crujía*) is limited to 4-5 m by the tiled roof that covers it (J. L. Trillo, [conarquitectura](https://conarquitectura.es/noticia/arquitectura-de-tejados-andaluces/)).

**Features: rooms and uses (Seville, 18th-19th c.)**
- **Summer below, winter above:** rising damp made the ground floor unhealthy in winter, so it was kept for summer. The plan repeats on both floors, "two houses" stacked (Guerra de Hoyos), and travellers saw families move even their furniture between floors with the seasons (Ollero 2022, p. 315).
- On the main floor the *salón de estrado* (reception room) sits over the portal; bedrooms face the street or the garden. A secondary "false stair" links the ground-floor kitchens with those above, around a light-well. Servants lived near the back entrances, the coach houses and stables, or on a third floor (Ollero 2022, p. 315).
- Service areas and kitchens stand near the wells, off the house's axis (Ollero 2022, p. 314). In the 19th century the house split into a reception zone (the street range: zaguán, salons, gaming rooms, studies), a family zone (bedrooms, dressing rooms) and a service zone (kitchen around small service patios, with wash-houses and larders) (Ollero 2022, pp. 318-319).
- **Top floor:** the *soberado* or miradores, used to store straw or grain, as a dovecote or as lumber rooms, open onto **azoteas that connect to other miradores or wash-houses** (Ollero 2022, p. 315). By about 1800 salons gained fireplaces, more doors and more glazing (same page).
- **Córdoba variant:** usually two storeys with three bays in depth, the middle one being the patio, crossed by two transverse ranges. Upper galleries often run only along the street side. The roof is double-pitched on that front and single-pitched elsewhere, draining into the patio "like an impluvium". When one family lived there, the ground floor held workshop, store and stables and the upper floor the living rooms (López Merino 2022, p. 117 and n. 7, citing Ruiz and Martín 2010).

**Features: the front (Seville, second half of the 18th c.)**
- Two storeys plus a *soberado* attic, with openings in a regular rhythm. Street-floor windows have grilles set in the reveal (*rejas en mocheta*); the upper floor has balconies or grilles that project beyond the façade line. The main portal sits on the axis, projects slightly, and has a balcony above it. A soberado often has round-arched openings between pilasters (Ollero 2022, p. 310).
- Render, sometimes painted with fake brick courses (*avitolada*). The fronts are two-coloured, **white or ochre with *almagra* (red earth)**, recalling stone against brick (Ollero 2022, p. 311). Around 1800 the projecting eaves gave way to **azoteas with railings and parapets carrying urns** (Ollero 2022, p. 312).
- Rules of 1850: no projecting grilles on the lower floors; balcony projection regulated and decreasing floor by floor; downpipes buried in the walls. In 1828 the council preferred three storeys for the street range; after an 1854 royal order, streets at least 6 m wide could take a ground floor plus three floors (Ollero 2022, pp. 319-320).

**Model it**
- The house is a blank box with a fat wall (design: 0.5-0.6 m), a portal module and a zaguán tube 1.5-2.5 m wide and 3-5 m long (design). The cancela is one alpha-tested quad (a 256 px wrought-iron pattern) with a darker zaguán behind it.
- The patio court is 1-4 arch bays per side, each bay 2.5-3.5 m (design). Columns are 6-8-sided cylinders; arches are 5-7 segment curves. The upper gallery is either repeated columns or a timber beam on brackets with a railing quad.
- Tile skirting (zócalo) is a 256 x 128 px strip texture to about 1.2 m (design) in the zaguán and on the patio walls. The patio floor is brick with small olambrilla insets (see `iberian.md`).
- Vertex paint: damp darkening at the foot of every patio wall (the sourced damp is the reason for the tiles), bright bounce light in the court, near-black zaguán.
- Glass montera (19th c.) as an option: a translucent lid over the patio that turns it into a lantern at night if lit inside.

**Play it**
- At night the solid street door is shut; by day only the cancela. That gives two states: by day the player can watch the court from the lane through the cancela and the patrol can see in; by night the house must be entered by picking the street door, from the azotea down the stair, or over the garden wall at the back (design).
- Summer means the family sleeps downstairs around the patio, winter upstairs (sourced practice): the same house plays differently by season. A summer mission puts sleepers right next to the court.
- The "false" service stair beside the kitchen light-well is the thief's route: it links the floors away from the patio's sightlines.
- Glazed upper galleries and coloured monteras throw coloured light patches; the patio is the brightest room in the house at moonrise.

## 3. The corral de vecinos and the Córdoba patios

**What and why:** the poor relation of the patio house: one court, many families, shared water, washing and latrines. For the game it is the dense, noisy, many-witness building, the opposite of the quiet patio house.

**Features: Seville**
- Corrales were the typical workers' housing of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Some were purpose-built; others were former mansions, patio houses or convents. All share a large inner patio with a fountain or a well in the middle that supplies the whole building. Galleries carry the room doors; each family has "one or two rooms"; there can be two storeys, and a small secondary court (*patinillo*) for washing. About 190 corrales were still counted in 1990, most empty or in ruins ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrales_de_vecinos_de_Sevilla)).
- Records go back to the 14th century and become precise in the 16th. A guide of 1862 counted about 200; a 1974 study examined 81 purpose-built ones. The typical layout has four corridors framing the square patio, with ground-floor rooms (*salas*) mirrored above ([corraldelconde.com](https://corraldelconde.com/historia-corral-del-conde/origen-de-los-corrales/)).
- Minimal dwellings: one or two rooms, sometimes with the cooking done in a small niche outside the rooms. Shared services (fountains, well, washing troughs, latrines) were in the patio or in small rooms by the stairs, so the patio and galleries became the shared living room (Guerra de Hoyos).
- **Corral del Conde**, Calle Santiago 27: called the most famous and one of the largest, with 113 dwellings in the 20th century (corraldelconde.com). It has Muslim origins with a Mudéjar restructuring; its present form is 18th-century, and it appears on Olavide's map of 1771. It was declared ruinous in 1969, a monument in 1979-1980, and rehabilitated in 1982-1983 ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corral_del_Conde)). The site describes a large paved patio ringed by wooden galleries over several floors, with a wash-house, a bread oven and a chapel ([corraldelconde.com](https://corraldelconde.com/)).
- **Galleries:** wooden posts (*pies derechos*) on square bases or straight on the ground, with timber brackets (*zapatas*) carrying the gallery and roof beams, and parapets of whitewashed brick leaving the post and the rail cap bare. In the 19th century cast-iron columns (often without brackets) and wrought-iron rails took over (López Merino 2022, p. 115).
- **Height:** Seville corrales rarely exceed three floors; Madrid's *corralas* reach five plus an attic; Córdoba's corrales seldom go above the first floor (López Merino 2022, p. 115, n. 3).
- Rules of 1850: tenement houses (*casas de vecindad*) had to be whitewashed **twice a year** (Ollero 2022, p. 321).

**Features: Córdoba**
- The 2001 protection plan lists 125 monuments plus 513 buildings, mostly patio houses of three types: the casa-patio (one family around a patio); the *casa de vecinos* (several families, several bays, one or more patios); and the *corral* (several families in one range against a party wall, reached by a gallery, with shared services). *Casas de vecinos* were already common in the late Middle Ages; purpose-built corrales multiplied from the 16th century. Homes where the shared parts outweighed the private ones were the majority (58.2%), concentrated in the eastern parishes (San Pedro, San Andrés, Santa Marina, Santa María) (López Merino 2022, p. 116).
- **Posada del Potro** (14th-15th c.): a rectangular patio paved with Córdoba pebbles, a stair up to a cantilevered timber gallery, rooms above and stables below; a plain white front, more wall than opening, under a curved-tile roof. The city saved it in 1972 (López Merino 2022, p. 120).
- **Patio San Basilio 44** (15th-16th c.): three porticoed sides on whitewashed stone pillars, a central stair, a medieval well and the original pebble paving (*enchinado*); historically home to up to a dozen families ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patio_San_Basilio,_44)).
- The patio festival: the first contest was in 1921, with three patios and prizes of 100, 75 and 50 pesetas; it was revived in 1933 with 16 patios and resumed in 1944; UNESCO listed it on 6 December 2012 ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtyards_Festival_of_Córdoba)). A secondary source says eight or ten households sometimes shared one patio, and that patios run "10 to 15 degrees cooler" than the street ([Explore Córdoba](https://www.explorecordoba.com/articles/cordoba-patios), secondary).
- **Calleja de las Flores:** a narrow dead-end lane opening into a tiny square framing the Mezquita's tower; the first flowerpots were placed in September 1939, and the octagonal fountain dates from 1960 ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calleja_de_las_Flores)). **Calleja del Pañuelo:** a blind lane ending in a tiny square with a fountain and an orange tree ([Turisigno](https://www.turisigno.fundacioncnse.org/cordoba/barrio-calleja-panuelo.php), secondary); width 70-250 cm (unverified).

**Model it**
- Corral = one courtyard module with 3-4 sides of gallery bays (design: 2.5-3 m per bay), 2 floors (3 at most, matching Seville), a single stair in a corner, and room cells of 3 x 4 m each with one door and one small window onto the gallery (design).
- Gallery kit: post, bracket and beam (8-12 tris each), a parapet of whitewashed brick (box) and a rail cap. For the 19th-century version, swap in cast-iron columns plus a rail quad.
- Shared props cluster in the patio: well with pulley, stone washing trough (*pila*), a latrine closet by the stair, laundry lines (alpha cards), and pots on every rail. Vertex-paint a dirty band at hand height along the galleries.

**Play it**
- Many families means many sleepers, some insomniacs and nobody owning the court: a witness-rich space where an alarm spreads door to door (design).
- The gallery is a ring route above the patio with doors every 3 m: good for lockpicking under time pressure, bad for hiding.
- Laundry lines and pots give soft cover on the gallery; the well and the trough are noise makers (a bucket dropped, a splash).
- The *patinillo* (laundry court) at the back is the quiet way in: low walls, few windows.

## 4. Moorish street devices (adarve, cobertizo, celosía and ajimez, azotea, aljibe)

**What and why:** the street furniture of a city built Islamic-style and kept by its Christian successors. Each device is a ready-made stealth mechanic: gated dead ends, overhead bridges, see-without-being-seen screens, walkable roofs and underground water.

**Features**
- **Adarve:** (1) a wall-walk behind a battlement; (2) in medieval Islamic cities, a **blind alley leading only to private houses, with a door at its mouth** ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adarve)). Guerra de Hoyos lists adarves among the outdoor spaces the Seville patio house turns its back on. In Cáceres "Adarve" survives in names of lanes along the wall (Adarve de Santa Ana, Adarve del Padre Rosalío; see images). Cáceres' old Jewish quarter (San Antonio) has narrow, steep streets with dead ends ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciudad_monumental_de_Cáceres)). Who held the keys of an adarve door: (not found).
- **Cobertizo (Toledo):** several dozen survive, among them Santo Domingo el Real, Santa Clara, San Pedro Mártir, Doncellas, Arco de Palacio, Calle del Ángel and Callejón de Orates. A 1615 reform of the Santa Clara one specified 13 *pies* of clearance at its highest and over 18 at its lowest point (about 3.6 m and 5.0 m, my conversion), and the rules required room for "a mounted knight with raised lance". A royal provision of 1513 under Queen Juana tried to stop new ones, noting the city's existing ordinance was not obeyed ([Tulaytula](https://tulaytula.com/faketoledo-los-cobertizos-de-toledo-el-caballero-y-la-reina-juana-de-castilla/), secondary). A pragmatic of 1509 against overhangs and cobertizos, and Toledo's Title 128 of 1513 forbidding doors, projecting chimneys and overhangs, are reported in search summaries of [Real Academia de Toledo papers](https://www.realacademiatoledo.es/downloads/publicaciones_cont1/1743/los-alarifes-en-las-ordenanzas-para-el-buen-regimen-y-gobierno-de-la-muy-noble-muy-leal-e-imperial-ciudad-de-toledo-de-22-de-diciembre-de-1590.pdf) (unverified). Seville's covered passage is the Callejón de la Judería (section 1).
- **Celosía:** a perforated screen that closes an opening and lets you see without being seen while passing light and air; made of fine wooden slats, stone, plaster or metal. In the *corrales de comedias* (courtyard theatres) celosías screened the boxes of royalty, nobles and clergy ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celosía_(arquitectura))). The **mashrabiya** is a projecting oriel enclosed in turned-wood lattice on upper floors; it was used for privacy and cooling (water jars placed in it), and its earliest evidence is 12th-century Baghdad ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashrabiya)).
- **Ajimez:** (1) a twin window divided by a slim column (*parteluz*) under two arches; (2) a projecting wooden balcony closed with lattices ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajimez)). Ajimeces were common in Toledo, Córdoba, Seville and Málaga, and a royal letter given at Granada on 7 July 1501 ordered them pulled down (search summary of *Laboratorio de Arte* 24 (2012), unverified). Seville's ordinances, compiled under the Catholic Monarchs and printed in 1527, forbade eaves, benches, overhangs and soberados that narrowed the streets (search summary of [Seville's 1527 ordinances](https://idus.us.es/server/api/core/bitstreams/2b5126ca-fda6-421c-902a-ef62db72e6bf/content), unverified).
- **Azotea:** an Arabic loanword for a flat, walkable roof, used to dry laundry and to hold cisterns, typical of dry Mediterranean climates ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azotea)). In Seville, soberados opened onto azoteas linked to other miradores and wash-houses (Ollero 2022, p. 315), and late-18th-century fronts swapped eaves for parapeted azoteas (p. 312). **Cádiz** shows the extreme: watchtowers became common on merchants' roofs in the 17th and 18th centuries; a 1777 model of the city shows 160, and about 126 survive. They come in *terraza*, *sillón*, *garita* and *sillón-garita* types, plus neoclassical ones built after a ban of 1792 (search summary of [grijalvo.com](https://grijalvo.com/wordpress/2017/11/08/torre-tavira-historia-de-las-torres-miradores-de-cadiz/) and [Diario Bahía de Cádiz](https://www.diariobahiadecadiz.com/opinion/oscar-cantero-puyana/las-torres-miradores-de-cadiz/), unverified). **Torre Tavira** is about 33 m tall and stands 45 m above sea level; it was named the port's official watchtower in 1778 and has five floors ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_Tavira)).
- **Aljibe:** an Arab cistern, partly or wholly underground, with a brick barrel vault or dome. Its lining mixed lime, sand, iron oxide, red clay and mastic resin; rain was led in from roofs and channels and drawn up by pulley ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aljibe)). For dimensions, use the Albaicín (at least 20 surviving cisterns; the Aljibe del Rey holds 300 m³) and the Cáceres cistern (13.50 x 9.90 m) in `iberian.md`.

**Model it**
- Adarve: a lane module that ends in a gate module (a timber door in a masonry arch, 1.2-1.8 m wide, design) and a cul-de-sac of 3-6 house doors. The gate is a separate door actor with lock state.
- Cobertizo: a box spanning the lane, its underside a dark barrel vault, clearance 3.6-5.0 m (from the 1615 figures), length 3-15 m (design). Interior: one room with windows on both sides.
- Celosía and ajimez: alpha-tested lattice quads on a shallow projecting box (design: 0.5-0.8 m deep); the twin window is a two-arch opening with a 6-sided mullion.
- Azotea: a flat roof at a slight fall with a 0.9-1.1 m parapet (design), a stair hut (*castillete*), a mirador or lookout box, and a wash-house corner with a trough and lines.
- Aljibe: a brick barrel-vault room with a waterline, entered through a well-head (*brocal*) in a patio or square.

**Play it**
- **Adarve:** a gated dead end. Locked at night, it is a safe pocket for anyone holding the key; a trap for anyone chased into it; a lockpicking puzzle under the watch's beat.
- **Cobertizo:** a bridge over the patrol and a room you cross between two houses: overhead route plus dark tunnel below.
- **Celosía:** NPCs inside see out but you cannot see in. A latticed balcony is a perfect perch for the player (seen-proof) and a perfect hiding place for a watcher (the player cannot tell whether one is there).
- **Azotea:** the thief's highway. Parapets give crouch cover; wash-houses and miradores are rooms on the roofs; Cádiz-style towers are lookouts that the watch can man.
- **Aljibe:** underground links between patios and squares; water noise covers footsteps; climbing up a well-head into a patio is the classic silent entry.

## 5. Granite Spain: Santiago de Compostela

**What and why:** the rainy, granite counterpart to whitewashed Andalusia: arcaded streets, a cathedral square on two levels joined by a stair, and glazed galleries. It is the best model for the Old Town's upper streets near the cathedral.

**Features**
- **Soportales:** the arcades of Rúa do Vilar and Rúa Nova are the city's best-known sequences ([El Correo Gallego](https://www.elcorreogallego.es/santiago/2024/10/30/curiosidades-compostela-sabias-los-soportales-de-santiago-110697515.html), secondary). Their arches are round, pointed or lintelled, the lintelled ones belonging to the oldest buildings ([Compostela Histórica](https://compostelahistorica.wordpress.com/compostela-a-pie/ruta-2-as-ruas/rua-do-vilar-y-rua-nova/), secondary). Upper floors were built out over the walkway on stone columns or pillars, gaining room without blocking the street; the arcades are medieval in origin, and parts of the municipality get over 1,700 mm of rain a year ([Diario de Santiago](https://www.diariodesantiago.es/santiago/por-que-tantas-casas-de-santiago-de-compostela-tienen-soportales-y-que-funcion-cumplian-realmente/), secondary). The 19th century campaigned against them until the early 20th (El Correo Gallego). Arcade depth, height and bay width: (not found).
- Rúa do Vilar runs from the Praza do Toural to the Praza das Praterías; the Casa del Deán there was built 1747-1753 (Compostela Histórica).
- **Praza da Quintana:** built around 1611 and divided by a stair into the upper Quintana de Vivos and the lower Quintana de Mortos ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintana_Square)). The lower level was the burial ground until 1780 (Compostela Histórica). The stair has 18 steps and was built in 1920 by José Torán (search summary, unverified) **(conflict:** Compostela Histórica calls it a great 17th-century stair**)**. The square is closed by the cathedral's east end with the Holy Door, the Casa da Parra, the monastery of San Paio de Antealtares and the Casa da Conga (en.wiki).
- **Casa do Cabido** (1758, Clemente Fernández Sarela): a façade only about 3 m deep, built for scenography to close one side of the Praza das Praterías ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_do_Cabido)).
- **Galleries:** the Galician glazed gallery is 19th-century. Flat glass from the royal works at San Ildefonso was first fitted to galleon sterns at Ferrol in the mid-18th century and then to houses in Ferrol, Pontedeume, Betanzos and A Coruña. They are white-painted timber with large panes; in A Coruña a municipal report of 1875 already criticised the excess of glass ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galer%C3%ADas_de_La_Coru%C3%B1a)). Santiago's old houses also have glazed galleries ([turismo.gal](https://www.turismo.gal/que-visitar/destacados/catedral-e-centro-historico/centro-historico-de-santiago-de-compostela?langId=en_US), secondary). Santiago's own gallery dates: (not found).
- Carved symbols on Rúa do Franco houses mark ownership: a scallop shell for the cathedral chapter, a tree for San Martiño Pinario, five stars for the university (search summary of Santiago tourism pages, unverified).

**Model it**
- Arcade module: a granite pier, a round or lintelled opening and a jettied upper storey; one module per house front, varied arch types along one street. The walkway is a dark, low strip; vertex-paint the soffit nearly black and the street edge wet-dark.
- Granite ashlar texture (256 px) with moss in the joints; the galleries are a white timber grid with dark glass, set on stone corbels.
- The two-level square is a terrace edge with a full-width stair, and a blank monastery wall as one side.
- A Casa do Cabido-style "false front": a 3 m-thick façade block that hides a drop or a service yard behind.

**Play it**
- Arcades are covered shadow lanes beside a lit street: parallel routes with pillars as cover. Rain noise masks footsteps (design: a weather state).
- The two-level square's stair is a choke point and a sightline break: from the upper level you overlook the lower; from the lower, the stair's riser hides you.
- A façade-only building can be climbed and gives a ledge view over a square without any interior.

## 6. Salamanca's fronts and the Cáceres tower-houses (deeper)

**What and why:** the noble stone house at its two extremes: Salamanca's carved "front as altarpiece", and Cáceres' fortified tower-house cut down by a queen. These suit the Old Town's key buildings (the merchant's house, the noble house).

**Features**
- **Casa de las Conchas** (1493-1517, by Rodrigo Maldonado de Talavera and his son): more than 300 carved shells on the front; four large Gothic windows, all different; a patio of about 18.80 x 16.80 m with mixtilinear arches below and Carrara marble columns above; a three-flight stair. Its **tower was cut down on Charles I's orders, losing two-thirds of its height** ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_las_Conchas)).
- **Plateresque** (late 15th century to about 1563) concentrates grotesques, candelabra, medallions and heraldry around portals and windows on a still-Gothic structure, making "façade-altarpieces" ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateresco)). The University front dates from 1529-1533.
- **Cáceres, the cut towers:** Isabella the Catholic had the nobles' towers cut down (*desmochadas*) to curb disobedience, sparing only the Torre de las Cigüeñas. The old town has more than 1,200 inventoried coats of arms; the wall averages 3 m thick and exceeds 10 m in places; the Torre de Carvajal is round (13th c., reformed in the 16th). Of its four gates, the Arco del Cristo is the only Roman one, and the Coria and Mérida gates are gone ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciudad_monumental_de_Cáceres)).
- **Torre de las Cigüeñas** (Casa de los Cáceres-Ovando, late 15th to early 16th c.): the only uncut tower, **25 m** tall, with merlons on corbels; the right to build it was granted in 1480. The house has a Gothic patio ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_las_Cigüeñas)). Heights of the cut towers: (not found).
- **Torre de Bujaco** (Almohad rebuild, 12th c.): about **10 m square and 25 m tall**, an *albarrana* (detached) tower with two side machicolations and one front; a small 16th-century balcony (the Balcón de los Fueros); it held the town clock 1672-1791 ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_de_Bujaco)).
- **Arco de la Estrella** (1726, Manuel de Lara Churriguera): replaced the 15th-century Puerta Nueva, which carriages could not pass. It is a low segmental arch with oblique piers through almost the full wall thickness, keeping the battlements above. A star-shaped lantern sits in a niche 77 x 45 x 62 cm. The towers of Bujaco, Púlpitos and Hierba guard this entrance ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arco_de_la_Estrella)).

**Model it**
- Tower-house kit: a 6-10 m-square plan with one cut-off (flat, parapeted) top variant and one crenellated 25 m variant. Very few openings: a portal with a stone arch and a shield, 1-2 grilled windows, and corner machicolation boxes. Granite ashlar in two tints, with lichen in vertex paint.
- Plateresque front: concentrate detail on one portal and one or two windows as normal-mapped or baked texture panels (512 px allowed for a hero door) on an otherwise plain wall.
- The skewed gate: a thick wall block with a slanted barrel through it.

**Play it**
- A cut-down tower is a flat roof platform above the roofline: a watch post or a rope anchor. The one surviving full-height tower is the district's landmark and overwatch, visible from everywhere (design: use it for orientation).
- Machicolation boxes are perches above a door: drop-in points for the player and drop-attack spots for the guards.
- The skewed gate through a thick wall is a long, dark, sound-carrying tube and the only carriage route between districts.

## 7. Girona's Call, the Onyar houses, and Besalú

**What and why:** Catalonia's version: a Jewish quarter of stepped alleys climbing from a main street to the cathedral, river houses built on the old town wall, and (as an extra) a fortified bridge town.

**Features**
- **The Call:** Jewish presence is documented from 888 (25 families). The quarter consolidated in the 12th century and held up to about 800 people at its peak ([ca.wiki](https://ca.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_de_Girona)). After 1391 the quarter was walled (search summary of [JTA](https://www.jta.org/2014/12/15/ny/my-girona), unverified); the last Jews left in 1492.
- **Carrer de la Força** follows the Roman Via Augusta and bounded the Call. Its houses run from ground plus two to ground plus four storeys (nos. 20 and 10) ([ca.wiki](https://ca.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrer_de_la_For%C3%A7a)). On its east side "picturesque stepped alleys" (Cúndaro, Sant Llorenç) climb toward the cathedral; the third synagogue (after 1416) had courts, wells and ritual baths ([Pedres de Girona](https://www.pedresdegirona.com/separata_forca_1.htm), secondary). Lane widths and step counts: (not found).
- **Pujada de Sant Domènec and Casa Agullana:** the two halves of the Agullana house (the old one 14th c., the new one probably 16th c.) stand on either side of the Sant Domènec stair and were joined in 1631 by a covered bridge on a low segmental arch (search summary of [ca.wiki Casa Agullana](https://ca.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_Agullana), unverified). Number of steps: (not found).
- **Cases de l'Onyar:** 19th-century houses built on the medieval wall, which grew upward, mostly from the 19th century. Many have galleries over the river with timber uprights every 70-80 cm. They were rehabilitated in 1982 (architects Josep Fuses and Joan Maria Viader), with a colour scheme by the artists Enric Ansesa and Jaume Faixó ([ca.wiki](https://ca.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cases_de_l%27Onyar)). The colours are therefore modern; for an earlier period use ochres and lime.
- **Besalú bridge** (extra): documented from 1075, built in the 12th century, Romanesque ([Catalunya Medieval](https://www.catalunyamedieval.es/pont-medieval-de-besalu-la-garrotxa/), secondary). It has a fortified tower, a pointed gate and merlons; two arches were blown up in 1939, and it was restored in the 1960s by Francesc Pons i Sorolla (same). It is 105 m long, with the gate on the first pier near the wall and a hexagonal loopholed tower on the fifth (search summary, unverified). Arch count **(conflict:** 7 per Catalunya Medieval, 8 per the search summary**)**. Height: (not found).

**Model it**
- Stair-lane modules: 1.5-3 m wide (design), steps in flights of 6-12 with landings at house doors; walls of rubble with dressed corners.
- House-bridge: a 1-room box on a low arch spanning a stair-lane (the Agullana pattern), reusable as a "two halves of one house" key building.
- River-wall houses: a sheer masonry base (the old wall) with stacked timber galleries on top, uprights every 70-80 cm (sourced spacing), each storey stepping out a little.

**Play it**
- Stair-lanes are long sightlines up and down but narrow: the watch climbs slowly, the player can drop from balconies to landings.
- A house-bridge over a stair is the ideal key-building link: enter one half, cross above the patrol, exit the other.
- River-wall galleries are a climbable lattice of posts over water: an outside route along the back of a whole row of houses.

## 8. Cuenca's ridge, and Ronda in brief

**What and why:** two Spanish cliff towns. Cuenca shows houses stacked so tall on a gorge that they read as low on the street and as towers from the void, which is exactly what the Old Town's edge against the rock needs. Ronda is the gorge-town link (keep for the gorge district).

**Features**
- Cuenca's old town sits at 956-1,000 m on "a narrow escarpment" between the Júcar and Huécar rivers. UNESCO listed it in 1996 (22.79 ha). The San Pablo bridge (1533-1589) is up to 40 m high; the cathedral was built 1182-1270 ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuenca,_Spain)).
- **Casas colgadas:** the only three surviving are the Casa de la Sirena and the two Casas de los Reyes, built in the 13th-15th centuries, with large projecting timber balconies. Eight still stood in the 1920s restoration; Wyngaerde drew the ensemble in 1565; a museum opened on 5 February 1966 ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casas_Colgadas)). Balcony projection and height above the gorge: (not found).
- **The "skyscrapers" of San Martín:** about 30 buildings along Calle Alfonso VIII (nos. 1-77), probably 13th-15th century, showing 3-5 storeys to the street and more than a dozen to the Huécar gorge, painted in strong colours ([101viajes](https://www.101viajes.com/cuenca/rascacielos) and search summary, secondary/unverified).
- **Ronda:** El Tajo is 100-plus metres deep; the Puente Nuevo was built 1751-1793 and stands 120 m above the canyon floor ([en.wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronda)) **(conflict:** es.wiki gives 98 m, see `iberian.md`**)**. The Casa del Rey Moro (18th c.) holds the "Water Mine", a stair cut in the cliff down to the river (en.wiki).

**Model it**
- A "split-height" house module: a 3-storey street face whose back drops 8-12 storeys down the rock (design), each lower floor smaller and set into the cliff. One mesh, two silhouettes.
- Hanging balconies: timber decks on cantilevered beams with plank railings, bolted to the cliff-face house.

**Play it**
- The cliff face of the split-height houses is a vertical maze: climb down outside from the street, enter on the eighth floor below, and come up through the house (design). Light from tiny windows high on a dark cliff is a striking night landmark.
- Hanging balconies are exposed perches over a drop: great views, lethal falls, and guard-free because no one expects an approach from the void.

## 9. The Spanish house kit, detail by detail

### 9.1 Rejas (window grilles) and cierros
**What and why:** the grille is the Spanish window; it decides whether a window is an entry point.
**Features:** *rejas* close openings in metal or wood, fixed or folding (see `iberian.md`, [es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reja_(arquitectura))). Seville, second half of the 18th century: grilles set in the reveal on the street floor, and balconies or projecting grilles above (Ollero 2022, p. 310). The 1850 ordinances banned projecting grilles on lower floors (p. 320). Bulging "chest" grilles (*rejas de buche, panzonas*) are of Baroque origin; they ventilated the room and held jars of food out in the night air ([El Imparcial](https://www.elimparcial.com/locurioso/2026/06/30/estas-ventanas-que-seguro-has-visto-se-llaman-rejas-panzonas-cual-es-su-historia/), secondary, Mexican). The *cierro* is the Andalusian glazed, grilled bay on a brick base with a sloping top; glass in cierros appears only in the 19th century, its great age (search summary of [Málaga Monumental](http://malagamonumental.blogspot.com/2009/02/cierros-y-balcones.html), unverified). Bar spacing: (not found).
**Model it:** the grille is an alpha quad in the reveal (flush) or a 5-face bulging cage (projecting, about 0.3 m, design); the cierro is a box with a glass texture, a timber grid and a tiled cap.
**Play it:** a flush grille is a sightline but not an entry; a projecting grille is a climbing frame (two handholds high); a cierro is a lit box on the street at night where someone may sit watching.
**Images:** [Reja](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reja-dragones.jpg) · [Courting at the reja, Córdoba](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%22Pelando_la_pava%22.JPG)

### 9.2 Doors, the postigo and knockers
**What and why:** the street door is the house's main lock.
**Features:** a *postigo* is a small door cut in a larger door, and also a minor gate in a town wall (Seville's Postigo del Aceite was *bab al-Qatay* under Muslim rule) ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postigo)). The *aldaba* is a hinged metal knocker (ring, hammer or animal head) of iron or bronze; forged heraldic examples spread from the 15th century, and it doubled as a pull handle ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldaba)). The zaguán's outer door is solid and opaque and is shut only at night (Guerra de Hoyos). Stud patterns and door sizes: (not found).
**Model it:** a two-leaf door module with a separate postigo leaf (its own door actor); studs as a texture with a few geometry bosses; the knocker as a 30-tri prop.
**Play it:** the postigo is the quiet lock (small, picks fast); the big leaves need a bar lifted from inside. A knock with the aldaba is a lure the player can use (design).

### 9.3 Tiles: skirting, street names and shrines
**What and why:** tile is Seville's signature and a wayfinding layer.
**Features:** cuerda seca (15th-16th c.), cuenca/arista (16th c.) and Niculoso Pisano's flat painted tile (from about 1500; his Alcázar Visitation altar dates from 1504); tiled skirtings in houses from the 15th to the 18th c. ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerámica_de_Sevilla)). The Casa de Pilatos tile contract of 1538-1539 ran to 2,000 tiles a week ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_Pilatos_(Sevilla))). **Street names:** under Olavide (1767-1771) Seville was divided into 5 *cuarteles* of 8 *barrios* of 8 *manzanas*, 320 blocks in all (256 on the left bank). Each street got a ceramic name plate, another plate gave its barrio, cuartel and block, and each house got a number ([Tu guía de Sevilla](https://tuguiadesevilla.com/pablo-de-olavide-azulejos-y-el-primer-plano-de-sevilla/), secondary). The 18th-century tiles have a white ground, a thin cobalt frame, blue or manganese-black letters and a small cross at the top centre; a royal despatch of 1771 ordered houses numbered and streets named, and in January 1776 a Seville potter delivered 2,628 such tiles to Jerez (García Lázaro 2016, [retabloceramico.org](https://retabloceramico.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/D00376.pdf)). In 1845 Pickman's Cartuja factory was commissioned for new street plates with black relief letters on white, five and a half inches high (search summary, unverified). **Street shrines (retablos):** documented in Seville from the 14th century and generalised in the 16th. A niche with an attic or broken pediment sat under a projecting little roof (*tejadillo*), lit by oil lanterns or candles, often with an alms box, and kept small so people and carriages could pass. Ceramic shrines spread from the 17th century ([Vela Cruz, *Tabularium Edit* 11](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/9342073.pdf)). In Huelva a priest's endowment of 1758 kept a shrine lantern burning every night (same). About 220 ceramic retablos were counted in Seville in the early 20th century ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerámica_de_Sevilla)). Tile size: (not found).
**Model it:** street-name tiles and house numbers as decals (64 px); a shrine as a 2 x 3-tile decal panel, a 4-face tejadillo and one lantern prop with an omni light; tile skirting as a strip texture.
**Play it:** shrine lanterns are the Old Town's fixed lights. They burn all night, so they are the pools the player must skirt, and dousing one is a loud signal (design). Street tiles let the player navigate by reading corners: a diegetic map.
**Images:** [Retablo cerámico](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Retablo_cerámico_de_la_Virgen_del_Rocío.JPG)

### 9.4 Whitewash, ochre and almagra
**What and why:** colour sets the night palette: lime-white walls catch moonlight.
**Features:** whitewash became predominant in the white towns with the plague waves from the 14th century (`iberian.md`). In Seville lime coats on fronts and patios were promoted after the 1800 epidemic; the Alcázar's walls were whitewashed in 1813, and an attempt to undo it in 1833 failed; the 1850 ordinances required whitewash in rooms where contagious patients had died and twice-yearly whitewash in tenements; in 1875 the whitewashing of a façade on the Plaza Nueva caused complaint (Ollero 2022, pp. 320-321). Eighteenth-century fronts were two-coloured, white or ochre with *almagra* red (p. 311). *Albero* is an ochre calcarenite earth from Los Alcores, its colour coming from goethite ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albero)); its use as the name of a paint for bands round openings: (not found in a scholarly source).
**Model it:** three wall textures (lime-white, ochre, almagra-banded) with vertex-painted grime at the base and streaks below sills; painted bands as a second UV strip around openings.
**Play it:** white walls make silhouettes; ochre and almagra lanes are darker and safer under the same moon (design).

### 9.5 Roofs: teja árabe, azoteas, miradores, chimneys
**What and why:** the roofscape is the second street network.
**Features:** canal tile is 45 cm long, 21 and 16 cm wide at its two ends, 8 cm high and about 2 kg; 27 per m² at a 20% overlap; laid in concave channels (*canal*) and convex covers (*cobija*), mortared only at the edges ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teja_árabe)). After 1755 Seville replaced roofs of "Moorish pieces" with double tiling (Ollero 2022, p. 310). Azoteas and miradores: see section 4. Fireplaces in salons around 1800 (Ollero 2022, p. 315); chimney-stack forms: (not found).
**Model it:** tiled roofs as low-pitch planes with a corrugated texture and a geometry ridge and eaves row; azoteas as flat walkable slabs with parapets. Mix both in each block so routes alternate sloped and flat.
**Play it:** canal tiles are loud and slippery (design: noise surface); azoteas are quiet and fast. A tiled slope between two azoteas is the risk step.

### 9.6 Night lighting and the sereno
**What and why:** the watch and its lights are the Old Town's antagonists.
**Features:** oil-burning street lamps (including reflector *reverbero* lamps) were installed in the main Spanish cities in the second half of the 18th century (search summary of [Muy Interesante](https://muyinteresante.okdiario.com/historia/asi-llego-tarde-como-casi-siempre-el-alumbrado-publico-a-las-calles-de-las-ciudades-espanolas-del-gas-a-la-luz-electrica.html), unverified). Seville's gas contract was held by York y Compañía until 1860 and passed to La Catalana around 1870-1871 (search summary of [ASIHF](https://asihf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/TST_32_52_77.pdf), unverified). Electric lighting was approved in 1901, and the lamplighters fought it in the streets ([La Ruina Habitada](https://laruinahabitada.org/2014/05/05/adios-al-farolero/), secondary). Lamplighters carried a pike, a whistle, a lantern, a ladder, an oil can and cloths, and from 11 pm they had to call to one another every quarter hour ([es.wiki Farolero](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farolero)). **The sereno:** first documented in Valencia in 1715; a corps of serenos was formally created on 12 April 1765. In Madrid the date is 1798 per es.wiki **(conflict:** 1779 per [Revive Madrid](https://www.revivemadrid.com/senas-de-identidad/el-sereno); 1797, with eight officials for eight districts, per a search summary**)**. A decree of 16 September 1834 regulated serenos in the provincial capitals; the sereno and lamplighter roles merged in 1854; the service ran 22:00-06:00; the figure disappeared in 1977. They carried a short pike (*chuzo*), a lantern, a whistle, later a straight staff, and the residents' door keys, and wore a blue greatcoat and a peaked cap; they called the hour and the weather ("Las once y sereno") ([es.wiki](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sereno_(oficio))). Madrid's first force was 100 men. Recruits were at least 20 years old, with a clear voice. Residents clapped and shouted "¡Sereno!", and he answered by striking the pike on the ground or calling "¡Va!". He also fetched firemen, doctors, midwives and priests, and was paid partly by neighbours' tips (Revive Madrid). A rattle for fire alarms is also reported (search summary, unverified).
**Model it:** sereno = one character with lantern (an omni light), pike, key ring (a visible, stealable prop) and greatcoat; wall lamps as iron brackets with a glass box; shrine lanterns as in 9.3.
**Play it:** the sereno holds every door key on his beat: pickpocket him and the street is open (design). His hour call is an audible clock and position ping; claps from a window summon him (a lure the player can fake, design); lamplighters calling every quarter hour give a guard-chatter rhythm. Lamps are dousable but each one has an owner who notices.
**Images:** [A sereno with pike and lantern, about 1912](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sereno.png) · [Category: Serenos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Serenos) · [Category: Torre Tavira](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Torre_Tavira)

## 10. Stealth synthesis: what Spain adds that Porto and Lisbon do not

**What and why:** the design takeaways, gathered in one place. Everything here is my inference from the sourced sections above.

**Features (the Spanish-only devices)**
- An **inward house**: blank walls, one bent passage and a court seen through iron (sections 2 and 9.2), against Porto's tall street-facing fronts.
- **Gated blind alleys** (adarves) and **house-bridges** (cobertizos, Casa Agullana) that make the street network itself lockable and layered (sections 4 and 7).
- **Walkable flat roofs linked house to house** (azoteas, miradores, wash-houses), against Lisbon's pitched tile (sections 2 and 4).
- The **communal court** (corral) as a high-witness building type (section 3).
- A **civic night system**: numbered houses, tiled street names, shrine lanterns burning all night and a key-holding watchman who calls the hours (section 9).

**Model it:** build the district as alternating blocks: 60% patio houses (blank walls, quiet), 15% corrales (noisy hubs), 10% key buildings (tower-house, merchant's house), and the rest squares, stairs and the wall (design ratios).

**Play it**
- **Three layers:** lanes about 1 m wide below (contact and patrols); azoteas and parapets above (the highway); aljibes and the city wall's passages below ground and through the wall (shortcuts).
- **Safe pockets:** a patio at night (street door shut) and a locked adarve are pockets of calm; a corral is the opposite.
- **Light grammar:** shrine lanterns and corner lamps are fixed, all-night pools; a cancela leaks a patio's light into the lane; a cierro is a lit window-box.
- **The watch:** serenos hold keys, call the hours, answer claps; lamplighters chatter every quarter hour. The rules are period-true and readable.

**Images:** [Night scene in Seville (panoramio; found by a Plaza de Doña Elvira search, location not stated)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ambiente_nocturno_en_Sevilla_-_panoramio.jpg) · [Calle Reinoso, second view](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Calle_de_los_besos_2.jpg)

### Lots and patios

- **Lot:** narrow and deep, with one street front between two party walls and the garden or yard at the back (Seville: search summary of a [San Pedro Mártir house study](https://www.archilovers.com/projects/102435/vivienda-en-el-casco-historico-de-sevilla.html), unverified). Córdoba neighbours' houses have fronts of 15-20 m with no more than 15% of the wall open (López Merino p. 117). Design lots: 6 x 18 m (small), 10 x 25 m (merchant), 16 x 20 m (corral), 8 x 8 m (corner).
- **Patio:** at least 25% of the lot (the Córdoba planning rule, López Merino p. 117). Great-house patios run 18.8 x 16.8 m (Conchas) and 324 m² (Pilatos). Ordinary patio sizes: (not found). Design: 4 x 5 m (small), 7 x 8 m (merchant), 10 x 12 m (corral).

### Rooms per floor and their uses

- **Ground floor:** zaguán, summer living room and summer bedrooms around the patio (Guerra de Hoyos; Ollero p. 315), kitchen and wash-place near the well (Ollero p. 314), stable or coach house at the back entrance in grand houses (p. 314).
- **Main floor:** salón de estrado over the portal, bedrooms to the street or garden, winter living rooms, and the kitchen over the kitchen via the false stair (p. 315).
- **Top:** soberado for storage, dovecote and servants; azotea with wash-house and mirador (p. 315).
- **Córdoba single family:** ground floor workshop, store and stables; upper floor living rooms (López Merino n. 7).

### Variants (each a prefab built from the modules)

1. **Small patio house** (design: 6 x 18 m lot, 2 floors): straight zaguán, cancela, a 4 x 5 m patio with one gallery side on timber brackets (a column-less patio, sourced as common by Ollero p. 314), front range of 1 room per floor, back range of 1 room per floor, corner stair, flat azotea at the back and a tile roof at the front. **Rooms:** 2-3. Props: a well, pots, one bed, a table, a chest.
2. **Merchant's patio house** (design: 10 x 25 m, 3 floors with soberado): bent zaguán with a tiled skirting, decorated cancela, a 7 x 8 m arcaded patio (3 x 3 bays, round arches below and a glazed gallery above, 19th c.), a portal-patio-garden axis, salón de estrado over the portal, false stair and light-well, back yard with stable and back door to a lane, azotea with a **mirador tower** (the Cádiz merchant habit, 17th-18th c.). Loot: counting room, strongbox in the salón, ledgers in the mirador.
3. **Corral de vecinos** (design: 16 x 20 m, 2 floors, 3 at most): one street gate with a zaguán; a 10 x 12 m patio with a central well and a pila; galleries on all four sides (timber, or iron for the 19th c.); 16-24 one- or two-room cells of 3 x 4 m (design count; Corral del Conde reached 113 dwellings); a latrine closet and wash patinillo at the back; a bread oven and a small chapel niche (both at Corral del Conde). Many sleepers, laundry lines, pots.
4. **Corner house with a tile shrine** (design: 8 x 8 m, 3 floors, no patio: corner plots in San Basilio take the house without a patio, per the search summary of the Córdoba plan; unverified): a shop or tavern on the ground floor with a postigo door, a living room upstairs, the soberado, a shrine panel on the corner at 2.5 m with a tejadillo and an all-night lantern, and street-name tiles on both faces. The lantern lights both lanes: the corner is a beacon.
5. **Tower-house** (design: 18 x 22 m lot): a patio house plus a 7 x 7 m tower in one corner, either cut down to the house's roof +1 floor (Isabella's order) or full height to 25 m (Cigüeñas, Bujaco). Granite or ashlar ground floor, heraldic shield over the portal, grilled windows, corner machicolation boxes. The tower top is a lookout and a rope anchor. Optional: a Plateresque hero portal.

### Furniture and props

- **Patio:** well-head with pulley and bucket; stone trough (pila); marble fountain (merchant); pots on walls and rails; orange tree in a brick bed; awning or sail cloth (summer); tiled bench; birdcages (Guerra de Hoyos mentions pots and birds in corrales).
- **Zaguán:** tiled skirting, lantern bracket, a chair for the porter, the cancela.
- **Rooms:** bed with curtains, chests, *estrado* platform with cushions (reception room), table and chairs, wall shelves with plates, brazier (no fireplace before about 1800 in most houses; Ollero p. 315), devotional print or small tile panel, writing desk and *chinero* (china cabinet, around 1800, Ollero p. 315).
- **Kitchen:** hearth or cooking niche (in corrales, outside the room per Guerra de Hoyos), water jars, oil jars, hanging pots.
- **Soberado and azotea:** straw bales, grain sacks, dovecote boxes, wash trough, laundry lines, urns on the parapet, a mirador with a telescope (merchant).
- **Street:** wall lamp bracket, shrine with lantern and alms box, street-name and house-number tiles, a grilled window with jars on the sill, mounting step, door knocker, sereno's lantern and key ring.
