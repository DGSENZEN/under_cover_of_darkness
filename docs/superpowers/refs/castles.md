# Castles for the finale: references, parts, ways in, night life, PS2 kit

Research notes for the last act of the night mission: an English-style castle on the summit of a
rocky Iberian city, garrisoned (patrols, squads, alarm bells, chapel, barracks, great hall), with
the target at the top of the keep. The aim is a grand, larger-than-life but controlled sense of
scale. Compiled 2026-09-28 from web sources (read-only research; nothing downloaded).

**How to read the tags**

| Tag | Meaning |
|-----|---------|
| **[V]** | Quoted or read directly from the linked source during this research |
| **[S]** | Single secondary source (blog, tourism page, aggregator). Plausible, but cross-check before relying on it |
| **[U]** | Unverified or conflicting between sources. Treat as a range, not a fact |
| **[D]** | Design suggestion from me, not a historical fact |
| *(derived)* | Arithmetic from verified numbers (unit conversion, step counts) |

Commons API on 2026-09-28 (a deliberately fake title came back "missing", so the check works).

## 0. At a glance

### The three best references for this finale

1. **Harlech (Wales, 1283-89): the whole castle on a rock.** A compact concentric castle on a
   rock "almost 200 feet (61 m) high" [V], with a fortified stair of 127 steps [V] down to a water
   gate that kept it supplied by sea in the 1294-95 siege [V]. The inner ward is only about
   39 m x 47-53 m [V], so it is a realistic footprint for a level, and its great gatehouse is a
   residence as well as a gate: the room over the gate passage held a chapel and the portcullis
   winches [S]. Several ways in, one dominant building, all on a cliff.
2. **Rochester (Kent, keep built 1127-38): the keep where the target lives.** A great tower about 21 m square,
   turrets 38 m above the ground and battlements about 34 m [V/derived], a forebuilding reached
   over a 2.7 m drawbridge gap [V], a cross wall running the full height with a **well shaft and a
   well-head on every floor** [V], two spiral stairs in opposite corners [V], and sockets for
   timber hoardings under the battlements [V]. In 1215 a mine fired with the fat of forty pigs
   brought one corner down, and the garrison fought on behind the cross wall [V]. It is the best
   model for a vertical, layered keep interior.
3. **Alcázar of Segovia (Castile): the Iberian blend.** A castle on the prow of a rock above
   the meeting of two rivers [V], with cliffs "de más de 80 m" [V], a rock-cut moat quoted as
   24 m wide and 26 m deep [V], and the Tower of Juan II crowned by a ring of turrets (12 per the
   city tourist office, 10 per another source) [V/U]. It supplies the silhouette and the
   "cliff city" relationship.

Runner-up for grandeur and hidden routes: **Caernarfon**, with its polygonal banded towers, the
Eagle Tower (28 m to the parapet, 39 m to the tops of its three turrets [V]) and continuous
passages inside the curtain at two levels under the wall-walk [V].

### Five best "ways in" from history (details in section 3)

1. **Edinburgh, 1314.** A man who grew up in the garrison knew a secret path up the north cliff that
   he had used at night to visit a girl in the town. Thirty men climbed it in the dark and put a
   12-foot rope ladder to the wall. A watchman above threw a stone down as a joke and shouted "I see
   you well!"; they froze, and it flew over their heads [V, Barbour].
2. **Roxburgh, 1314.** About sixty men in black cloaks crawled up on hands and knees on the night
   of a feast, and the sentries took them for strayed cattle. They used ladders of hemp rope with
   wooden rungs and a square iron hook. The hook clanked on the battlement and a watchman came
   running, but he was killed on the ladder-head [V, Barbour].
3. **Château Gaillard, 1204.** The attackers got in through a low, unbarred window of a newly
   added chapel beside the latrines. The popular "up the toilet chute" version is a later legend
   [V].
4. **Linlithgow, 1313, and Edinburgh, 1341.** A trusted supply cart stopped in the gateway. At
   Linlithgow the driver's man cut the traces so it could not be moved, then armed men hidden in
   the hay attacked, and the gate could not be closed [V].
5. **Conwy, 1401.** Two brothers and 44 men posed as carpenters on Good Friday while most of the
   garrison was at church in the town. They killed the two watchmen on duty and took the castle [V].

## 1. Castle references

### 1.1 Edwardian concentric and curtain castles (North Wales, 1283-1330)

Built for Edward I under Master James of St George. They are UNESCO-listed together as the
"Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd". All four have unusually rich surviving
building accounts and dimensions.

#### Caernarfon: grandeur, polygonal banded towers, galleries inside the walls

**Why:** the most imperial of the four, with polygonal towers and bands of coloured stone
(read as a reference to the walls of Constantinople [V]). Its curtain wall is honeycombed with
passages, which gives natural stealth routes.

- **Eagle Tower:** 28 m to the parapet, and three polygonal turrets take it to 39 m [V medievalheritage].
  Walls 18 ft (5.5 m) thick [V Cadw]. Ten-sided; basement chamber 9 x 10.5 m internally; the
  first-floor main chamber is nearly 12 m across with 4.5 m walls [V medievalheritage].
- **Curtain wall:** 3.6-4.9 m thick at ground level, thick enough to hold one or two levels of
  wall galleries, with the open wall-walk above as a third fighting level. The galleries are
  roofed with stone slabs on corbels and hold stairs between levels. The walls are about 18 m high
  outside and 12.2 m from the courtyard [V medievalheritage]. There are arrow slits in every
  second merlon [V medievalheritage]. The Granary Tower has a forked loop arrangement that lets
  three archers shoot through one outside opening [V medievalheritage].
- **King's Gate (planned):** two drawbridges, five doors, six portcullises and a right-angle
  turn, overlooked by arrow loops and murder holes [V Wikipedia].
- **Queen's Gate:** raised on the old Norman motte and approached by a stone ramp. It had a
  drawbridge with a counterweight pit, a portcullis and six murder holes in the vault. Guard
  chambers either side have single embrasures and latrines [V medievalheritage].
- **Great hall:** 30.5 m (100 ft) long [V]. The Well Tower well is 15 m deep with a stone tank
  beside it [V]. The Chamberlain Tower has three octagonal rooms 6.7 m across [V].
- **Town walls:** about 800 yards (730 m) with eight towers about 70 yards (64 m) apart
  [S castlewales].
- **Garrison:** "around thirty" during the 1403-04 sieges [V Wikipedia].
- **Stairs:** 5 clockwise and 4 anticlockwise (the Well, Chamberlain, Eagle and Black towers)
  [V Guy 2011-12].

#### Harlech: a castle on a cliff with a sea stair (top pick for the overall layout)

**Why:** a compact concentric castle on a sheer rock, dominated by one great gatehouse, with a
protected stair down to a water gate. This is the closest real analogue to a castle on the
summit of a rocky city.

- **Site:** "Harlech Dome, a spur of rock almost 200 feet (61 m) high" [V Wikipedia]. Sheer on
  the sea side; the two easier sides are cut off by a wide rock-cut ditch [V medievalheritage].
- **Inner ward:** about 39 m wide and 47 m (west side) to 53 m (east side) long, inside a curtain
  about 3 m thick [V medievalheritage].
- **Corner towers:** about 10 m in diameter. The south-west tower is 15.8 m tall and the
  north-west 15.1 m, each topped by a cylindrical watch turret 5.8 m high [V medievalheritage].
- **Outer ward:** the outer wall follows the inner line but is "much lower and thinner"
  [V medievalheritage]. In 1323-24 two square gate towers were added in the ditch in front of the
  entrance, one 18 m and one 12 m high [V medievalheritage].
- **Great gatehouse:** twin D-shaped towers [V].
  - The passage has three portcullises and two or three double-leaf doors barred from inside
    [V medievalheritage; Wikipedia says "three portcullises and at least two heavy doors"].
  - The passage is about 16 m long [S seeingthepast].
  - The drawbridge pit is about 5 m x 3.5 m, enclosed, reachable only by ladder [V AW report 1080].
  - Order of defences: two-leaf door with drawbar, two portcullises, another barred door, a third
    portcullis [S warhistory.org].
  - The chapel sat directly over the gate passage, flanked by vestries, and received the two front
    portcullises when they were raised; the winches were on that floor. A rear door on the first
    floor led by a platform and outside stair to the ground, so the upper floors stayed reachable
    with every gate shut [S warhistory.org].
  - Two upper floors of apartments, with six large windows to the courtyard [V].
- **"Way from the Sea":** a protected stair down the cliff to a water gate. It has 127 steps per
  Wikipedia [V] and 108 per great-castles.com [U]. Clark (1875) describes a rock-cut roadway rising
  10-12 ft to a drawbridge with a pit 12 ft deep; today the water-gate passage is just under 2 m
  wide [V GAT report 774].
- **Garrison:** "36 men: a constable, 30 men, including 10 crossbowmen, a chaplain, a smith,
  carpenter and stonemason" [V Wikipedia].
- **Sieges:** supplied from Ireland by sea through the water gate in 1294-95 [V]. It fell to
  Glyndŵr at the end of 1404 [V]. The English besieged it with cannon, and it fell in February 1409
  when supplies ran out [V]. In 1468 its small garrison surrendered after a month [V].
- **Latrines:** Cadw points visitors to a latrine chute by the south-west tower [V Cadw leaflet].

#### Beaumaris: the "perfect" concentric plan (unfinished)

**Why:** the textbook walls-within-walls plan, with a tidal moat and a dock at the gate. It shows
how many barriers stood between the outside and the inner ward.

- **Inner ward walls:** 36 ft (11 m) high and 15.5 ft (4.7 m) thick [V Wikipedia]; 11 m and
  4.9 m per medievalheritage [V]. The inner ward covers about 0.75 acre (0.30 ha) [V], or
  59 x 54 m [V medievalheritage].
- **Gap between the curtains (outer ward):** about 18 m (60 ft) [V medievalheritage; World History
  Encyclopedia].
- **Outer curtain:** 12 towers [V World History Encyclopedia], but the Welsh government's
  education site says "16 towers and two gates" [U]. Curtains about 1.5 m thick (1.8 m in one
  section) [V medievalheritage].
- **Moat:** "some 18ft wide", tidal [V hwb.gov.wales].
- **Dock:** "vessels of 40 tons laden weight could sail right up to the main gate"
  [V hwb.gov.wales].
- **Barriers:** after the first gate an attacker "would still have to face 11 further obstacles
  ... the barbican, further 'murder holes', three portcullises and several sets of doors"
  [V hwb.gov.wales].
  - South gate passage sequence: outer doorway with double draw-bar sockets, portcullis slot,
    four arches between murder slots, loops in each wall, another portcullis slot, then an arch
    [V Gatehouse Gazetteer].
- **North gatehouse hall:** about 70 x 25 ft (21 x 7.6 m) [V].
- **Firing positions:** "around 300 firing positions for archers, including 164 arrow slits" [V].
- **Latrines:** "as many as 32 of them, 16 on one level" [V medievalheritage], "drained by a
  special system using the water from the moat" [V Wikipedia].
- **Stairs:** 6 clockwise and 4 anticlockwise, arranged as mirror-image pairs in the gatehouses
  [V Guy].

#### Conwy: a linear castle on a rock ridge, with its town walls (best source of kit numbers)

**Why:** a long castle on a rock ridge, split into an outer and an inner ward by a cross wall,
with barbicans at both ends and watch turrets. It was originally **white**. Its town walls give
the cleanest measured kit dimensions anywhere.

- **Towers:** eight towers about 21 m (70 ft) tall [V]; about 30 ft (9 m) in diameter
  [S magiccarpetjournals]. The Prison Tower walls are 3.7 m thick at the base [V medievalheritage].
- **Curtain:** 3-3.3 m thick on average and about 9.1 m high above the courtyards, with a
  breastwork 1.8-2.1 m high [V medievalheritage].
- **Wards:** lower (outer) ward 50 x 30 m; upper (inner) ward 25 x 22 m. The inner ward's gate
  had a drawbridge and portcullis over a dry moat cut in the rock [V medievalheritage].
- **Watch turrets:** "Each tower has an additional watchtower turret, probably intended both for
  security and to allow the prominent display of the royal flag" [V Wikipedia].
- **Battlements:** "originally sported triple finial designs and featured a sequence of square
  holes running along the outside" [V Wikipedia]. The pinnacled merlons are a Savoyard feature,
  shared with Castello di San Giorio di Susa [V].
- **Barbicans:** the west barbican (with three turrets) carries "the earliest surviving stone
  machicolations in Britain" [V Wikipedia]. It was reached over a drawbridge and a masonry ramp
  climbing sharply from the town [V].
- **Colour:** "Originally the castle would have been white-washed using a lime render" [V
  Wikipedia]. Cadw describes "gleaming white walls, heraldic banners, painted shutters and shields
  hanging from the battlements" [V Cadw].
- **Well:** 91 ft (27.7 m) deep [V].
- **Great hall:** about 125 ft (38 m) long; eight stone arches were inserted in 1346 to carry a new
  roof [S].
- **Garrison (1283):** "30 soldiers, including 15 crossbowmen, supported by a carpenter, chaplain,
  blacksmith, engineer and a stonemason" [V Wikipedia].
- **Water access:** a postern led down to a small dock on the river, "allowing key visitors to
  enter the castle in private and for the fortress to be resupplied by boat" [V Wikipedia].
- **Stairs:** only one anticlockwise, in the Bakehouse Tower [V Guy].
- **Conwy town walls (use these for the kit):**
  - 1.3 km long, with 21 towers and 3 gatehouses [V]. Towers are evenly spaced about 45 m apart,
    mostly semicircular, 4-5.2 m in diameter and up to 15 m tall [V medievalheritage].
  - The wall is 1.7 m thick and 9 m high, 5.2 m to the wall-walk from the town side. The
    waterfront wall is over 3.1 m thick; some short stretches are only 0.91 m thick with no
    wall-walk [V medievalheritage].
  - **"Merlons ... 2.1 meters wide, were spaced at 0.9 meter intervals"** [V medievalheritage].
  - The wall-walk is widened on a row of corbels [V Wikipedia].
  - The towers are "gap-backed" (open at the back) and "originally included removable wooden
    bridges to allow sections of the walls to be sealed off from attackers" [V Wikipedia].
  - The gates are pairs of horseshoe towers flanking a **2.9 m wide passage**. The ditch at the
    Upper Gate is just over 5 m wide [V medievalheritage].
  - A row of **12 latrines** is built into the southern wall near the Mill Gate [V].

### 1.2 Great keeps (the building at the top)

#### White Tower (Tower of London, c.1077-1100): the archetype

**Why:** the model Norman keep. It has a first-floor entrance, a cross wall dividing every floor,
a chapel inside the walls, and a well in the basement. It was also the scene of two famous rope
escapes.

- **Size:** 36 x 32 m (118 x 105 ft) not counting the corner turrets, and 27 m (90 ft) high at the
  southern battlements [V Wikipedia]. Heritage Gateway gives 35 x 29 m excluding the apse and
  turrets [V].
- **Walls:** 15 ft (4.6 m) thick at the base, "almost 11 feet" (3.4 m) higher up [S two aggregator sites].
- **Entrance:** on the first floor of the south face, reached by a wooden stair "which could be
  removed in the event of an attack". A 12th-century forebuilding no longer survives [V].
- **Floors:** originally three storeys (basement, entrance level, upper floor), with a top floor
  added in the 15th century [V]. Each floor is split into three chambers, with the chapel of
  St John rising through the entrance and upper floors in the south-east [V]. The basement was an
  undercroft for storage, with a well in one room [V]. Latrines are built into the walls and there
  were four fireplaces [V].
- **Stairs:** the main stair is in the north-east turret: clockwise, a 3.6 m cylinder, 1.4 m tread
  and 20 cm riser, rising through every floor. A smaller anticlockwise service stair in the
  north-west corner runs to the roof (1.21 m winder) [V Guy].
- **Colour:** "In 1240 it was whitewashed giving it the name it still retains" [V Heritage Gateway].

#### Rochester (keep 1127-38, by Archbishop William de Corbeil [V]): the tall keep (top pick for the keep interior)

**Why:** tall and narrow, with a forebuilding, a full-height cross wall with a well shaft, two
spiral stairs and hoarding sockets. It suits the layered, vertical final ascent to the target.

- **Size:** "70 by 70 feet (21 by 21 m) externally with pilaster buttresses at each corner" [V].
  The turrets rise 125 ft (38 m), "12 ft above the battlements" [V], so the battlements are about
  113 ft (34 m) *(derived)*. Wikipedia calls it "the tallest keep in England" [V].
- **Walls:** 12 ft (3.7 m) thick at the base, tapering to 10 ft (3 m) at the top [V].
- **Cross wall and well:** "The keep's interior is divided for its entire height by a cross wall
  ... The cross wall carried a well shaft, with a well-head at each floor" [V].
- **Stairs:** the north-east spiral serves all floors; the south-west spiral runs from the first
  floor to the top [V].
- **Forebuilding:** on the north side. A stone stair starts on the west and turns into the
  forebuilding, which is entered "by crossing a drawbridge across a gap 9 feet (2.7 m) wide" [V].
- **Floors:** the ground floor is storage, with three storeys of accommodation above [V]. The
  second floor is 27 ft (8.2 m) high (a two-storey space with a gallery) and has a chapel of about
  28 x 15 ft (8.5 x 4.6 m) [V].
- **Hoarding:** "rectangular holes, marking where wooden hoarding would have been attached" sit
  below the battlements [V].
- **Curtain (Bishop Gundulf's wall):** 4.5 ft (1.4 m) thick at the base, narrowing to 2 ft (0.6 m)
  at the top, and about 22 ft (6.7 m) high. A later round tower is 30 ft (9.1 m) in diameter [V].
- **1215 siege:** see section 3.

#### Dover (Great Tower 1180s): the palace keep inside a concentric castle

**Why:** a keep used as a palace, with a three-towered forebuilding holding the grand stair and
two chapels. It sits inside the earliest large concentric castle, and it has a written night
routine and medieval tunnels.

- **Great Tower:** 83 ft (25.3 m) high and "just under 100 feet (30m) square", with walls up to
  21 ft (6.5 m) thick [S dover-kent.co.uk]. The corner towers rise about 12 ft higher [S].
  A 19th-century account gives uneven sides of 108, 103 and 123 ft [S].
- **Forebuilding:** "three-towered fore-building, carrying the entry staircase and two chapels";
  the upper chapel was reserved for the royal family [S dover-kent.co.uk].
- **Stairs:** the widest English spirals include Dover's, with a 1.9 m winder [V Guy].
- **Water:** lead pipes carried water from the keep's well to other floors; two 3.5-inch pipes were
  found in 1930 [V Kent Archaeological Society]. The well depth is quoted as 289 ft (88 m) in one
  source [S] and 400 ft (122 m) in a Victorian guide [S]. Treat it as [U].
- **Defences:** Henry II's engineer Maurice built the inner bailey and part of the outer
  [V English Heritage]. The inner curtain has 14 square towers and two gatehouses [S].

- **Night routine:** see section 4.

#### Hedingham (c.1140): the great hall arch

**Why:** a compact keep with a two-storey banqueting hall spanned by a single huge arch. It is a
good model for a dramatic hall inside the final tower.

- **Size:** 53 ft (16 m) on the east and west sides and about 58 ft (18 m) on the north and south
  [V]. The main part is "more than 70 ft (21 m) tall", with turrets rising 15-25 ft

- **Walls:** about 11 ft (3.4 m) thick at the base and 10 ft (3.0 m) at the top [V].
- **Floors:** five, including the Great or Banqueting Hall "with a large fireplace and a central
  arch extending two storeys" [V]. The arch spans 28 ft (8.5 m) [S].
- **Stairs:** the principal stair cylinder is 4.25 m in diameter [V Guy].

### 1.3 Later English castles (1300s-1400s)

#### Warwick: machicolated tower tops, gate and barbican

**Why:** its 14th-century tower tops show machicolations that carry the silhouette. Caesar's
Tower is quatrefoil in plan with a double parapet, and the gate has a proper barbican.

- **Caesar's Tower:** "one of only two Quatrefoil towers in the country" [V official]. "Both
  towers are machicolated and Caesar's Tower features a unique double parapet" [V Wikipedia].
- **Plan:** a perimeter of about 130 x 82 m [V Wikipedia].
- **Gatehouse and barbican:** "murder holes, two drawbridges, a gate, and portcullises"; the

#### Bodiam (1385): the moated quadrangle

**Why:** the compact courtyard castle. The walls, domestic ranges and gatehouse form one block in
a lake, approached by a causeway with an island barbican, and there is a postern in the back.

- **Moat:** 155 x 115 m overall and about 2 m deep, fed by springs [V Gatehouse Gazetteer citing
  Historic England]. Wikipedia gives an average of 5 ft (1.5 m) and 7 ft (2.1 m) in the SE corner [V].
- **Approach:** a stone causeway projects halfway into the northern arm of the moat, holding the
  remains of an outer barbican and ending in an octagonal plinth [V].
- **Gatehouse:** three storeys. The passage "would originally have had three wooden portcullises",
  a vaulted ceiling "pierced with murder holes", a machicolated top and gun-loops. The medieval
  outer portcullis of iron-clad oak survives [V].
- **Postern:** under a square, three-storey postern tower in the middle of the southern range [V].
- **Inside:** the great hall is 24 x 40 ft (7.3 x 12.2 m) [V]. The chapel pushes the curtain
  9 ft (2.7 m) out into the moat [V]. The well is about 2.75 m in diameter [V]. The towers are
  three storeys; the curtains and internal ranges are two [V].
- **Curtain height and thickness:** no reliable figure found [U]. One search summary claimed
  18 m high and 5.5 m thick, but no primary page confirmed it.

#### Alnwick: the long barbican and figures on the battlements

**Why:** one of the finest barbicans in Britain, and stone figures standing on the crenellations,

- **Barbican:** "fortified passage over dry moat to vaulted gateway flanked by polygonal towers"

### 1.4 Iberian cousins (for the blend)

#### Alcázar of Segovia: the rock prow (top pick for the Iberian blend)

**Why:** the model for a castle ending a rock city. It sits on the point of a rock above the
Eresma and Clamores rivers, with a deep rock-cut moat on the city side, a massive keep facing the
town, and slate spires.

- **Site:** above the confluence of the Eresma and Clamores [V en.wikipedia]. "Dos enormes tajos
  de más de 80 m. de altura" (two cliffs over 80 m high) [V castillosricsol]. The walls converge
  on "la aguda punta" at the old keep, which gives the prow [V castillosricsol].
- **Moat:** "profundo foso de 24 m. de ancho y 26 m. de profundidad", cut in the rock; "Felipe II
  ordenó ahondar el foso hasta darle 72 pies de profundidad" [V castillosricsol]. The drawbridge
  was replaced by a stone bridge with one great arch under Philip II [V].
- **Tower of Juan II:**
  - Built 1440-65 on a 13th-century tower [V es.wikipedia].
  - The height is widely quoted as 80 m [V Turismo de Segovia; es.wikipedia], but **treat that as
    measured from the valley floor [U]**. The stair has 152 steps [V es.wikipedia] (156 per
    en.wikipedia [U]); at 20-21 cm per step that is only about 30-32 m of climb *(derived)*.
  - Ring of turrets: "doce torrecillas" [V Turismo de Segovia], or "diez enormes garitones que
    flanquean el adarve" [V arteguias] [U].
  - Decorated with esgrafiado (sgraffito) plaster and used as a state prison [V].
- **Roofs:** the slate spires and roofs date from Philip II's works [V]. A fire on 6 March 1862
  destroyed the roofs and spires; restoration began about 20 years later [V].
- **Courtyards and terrace:** two courtyards [V]. A walled terrace along the whole front is the
  "Terraza de Moros, así llamada porque sobre ella hacía centinela la guardia mora de Enrique IV"
  (called that because Henry IV's Moorish guard stood sentry there) [V es.wikipedia].
- **Perimeter:** 728 m [U single search summary].

#### Castelo de São Jorge (Lisbon): the hilltop citadel above a city

**Why:** a citadel on the highest hill of a city, with a Traitor's Gate (a postern) and the
Martim Moniz legend of a knight jamming a door open with his body.

- **Citadel:** about 6,000 m² (roughly 77 x 77 m) *(side derived)*, quadrangular, with 10 towers.
  "Muralhas mostram uma altura média de 10m e uma espessura que varia entre os 2,5m e os 5m"

- **Towers:** square in plan [S], with square merlons without arrow slits [S]. The Torre de Ulisses
  (possibly the keep) is "10m de face por 20m de altura" (10 m a side, 20 m high) [V e-cultura],
  and has held a camera obscura since 1998 [V].
- **Porta da Traição:** "Na muralha norte abre-se a Porta da Traição protegida por um pano de muro
  exterior" (the Traitor's Gate opens in the north wall, shielded by an outer wall) [V e-cultura].

- **Barbican:** a barbican (in Portuguese usage a low outer wall) protects the south and east
  faces, with a dry ditch between it and the wall [V e-cultura; Wikipedia].
- **1147 legend:** "the knight Martim Moniz, noticing that one of the doors to the castle was
  open, prevented the Moors from closing it by throwing his own body into the breach" [V Wikipedia].
- **City wall (1373):** 77 towers, 34-46 gates, 5.4 km [V].

## 2. The parts: what they are, how they work, typical sizes

### 2.1 Site and plan types

| Type | Examples | How it shapes play [D] |
|---|---|---|
| **Concentric** (walls within walls, the inner wall higher so both can shoot) | Beaumaris, Harlech; Dover was "one of the first examples of its kind in western Europe" (Pastscape, quoted on the Images of Dover blog [S]) | An outer ward about 18 m wide acts as a killing ground between the curtains (Beaumaris, [V]), which suits patrol loops and exposed crossings |
| **Linear on a ridge** (wards in a row, cross walls between) | Conwy, Caernarfon, Château Gaillard, Peñafiel | Natural act structure: each ward is a step closer to the keep |
| **Rock prow** (sheer on three sides, a cut ditch on the neck) | Segovia, Harlech, Château Gaillard | One obvious front; the cliffs are the stealth sides |
| **Quadrangle in water** | Bodiam | A single compact block with a postern at the back |

### 2.2 Barbican

- **What it is:** an outwork in front of a gate that controls the choke point. Often a walled
  approach called the "neck" connects it to the main gate [V Wikipedia].
- **Portuguese usage:** "barbacã" means any lower outer wall in front of the main wall [V]; see
  São Jorge.
- **Examples:**
  - Conwy has barbicans at both ends, each with three turrets [V].
  - Alnwick has a long walled passage over a dry moat [V].
  - Bodiam's is on a causeway island [V].
  - Dover's northern barbican was badly damaged in 1216 and later reached by tunnel [V].
  - Warwick and Harlech have remains [V].
- **Design use [D]:** a walled, turning approach under the gatehouse; the second checkpoint.

### 2.3 Twin-towered gatehouse and its passage

- **Layout:** two D-shaped or polygonal towers flank a vaulted passage. Guard chambers sit either
  side at ground level (Caernarfon's Queen's Gate: "irregular chambers for guards or gatekeepers,
  equipped with single embrasures and latrines" [V]). Apartments are above: the constable's lodging
  or high-status rooms at Harlech [V].
- **Sequence of defences:**
  - Harlech: two-leaf barred door, portcullis, portcullis, barred door, portcullis [S].
  - Beaumaris south gate: door with double drawbar sockets, portcullis, four murder-slot arches,
    loops in both walls, portcullis [V].
  - Caernarfon King's Gate (planned): two drawbridges, five doors, six portcullises and a
    right-angle turn [V].
- **Portcullis:** a wood and/or iron grille sliding in grooves in the jambs, "raised or lowered
  quickly by using chains or ropes attached to an internal winch" [V Wikipedia]. At Harlech the
  winches sat in the chapel room above the passage [S]. Bodiam's iron-clad oak portcullis
  survives [V]. The Tower of London, York's Monk Bar, Hever and Edinburgh keep working ones [V].
- **Drawbridge:** usually a turning bridge. The front rises into a recess and the back drops into a
  pit [S warhistory]. Harlech's pit is about 5 x 3.5 m [V]. Caernarfon's Queen's Gate pit took the
  counterweight [V].
- **Murder holes:** holes in the passage vault for dropping "rocks, arrows, scalding water, hot
  sand, quicklime, or hot oil (rarely used because of its cost)". They may also have been used
  simply to talk between levels [V Wikipedia]. Examples: six in the Queen's Gate vault
  (Caernarfon) [V]; Bodiam [V].
- **Sizes:**
  - Passage about 2.9 m wide (Conwy town gates) [V]; Harlech's pit is 3.5 m wide [V].
  - Harlech's passage is about 16 m long [S].
  - Beaumaris's north gatehouse hall is 21 x 7.6 m [V].
- **The door that stays open at night:**
  - At Harlech a rear first-floor door with an external stair gave access when all gates were
    shut [S].
  - At Dover after sunset only the postern was used [S], and even the king came in that way.

### 2.4 Curtain wall, wall-walk and battlements

- **Height and thickness:** see the kit table in section 0. Walls were usually rubble-cored with two
  dressed faces [D general]. They often taper: Rochester's curtain goes from 1.4 m to 0.6 m [V].
- **Wall-walk:** on thin walls it was widened on corbels (Conwy town walls) [V]. Walks are
  interrupted by towers. At Conwy the open-backed towers were crossed by removable timber bridges
  so a lost section could be cut off [V]. Parapet walks drained through the wall by through-stones
  (Bew Castle) [V OA North].
- **Merlons and crenels:**
  - Conwy town walls: merlons 2.1 m wide with 0.9 m gaps [V].
  - Carcassonne merlons: 1.60-1.80 m high [V Viollet-le-Duc].
  - Conwy castle breastwork: 1.8-2.1 m high [V].
  - Rule of thumb: "the crenel comprised one-third of the width of the merlon" [V Wikipedia],
    which matches Conwy (0.9/2.1 is about 0.43, so roughly).
  - Merlon thickness is reported as 38-58 cm by Viollet-le-Duc [S StoneKeep Atlas].
  - Variants:
    - Merlons pierced with loops (every second merlon at Caernarfon [V]).
    - Hinged timber shutters hung between merlons [V Wikipedia].
    - Pinnacled merlons at Conwy [V].
    - A battlement with holes for fixing wooden shields for crossbowmen, at Caernarfon [V].
- **Crenel sill height:** not measured in any source found. Use about 0.9-1.1 m above the walk,
  so a crouching guard is hidden and a standing one shows [D].

### 2.5 Arrow loops

- **Shape:** a narrow outer slit with the wall splayed back to an inner recess (embrasure) where
  the archer stands or sits.
  - Carcassonne: the wall is reduced to 0.7 m at the niche and the field of fire is 35 deg
    [V Wikipedia caption]. The outer slit is 6 cm wide [S StoneKeep citing Viollet-le-Duc].
  - Typical slits are 10-12 cm wide and 1.0-1.1 m high, though some are several metres tall
    [S Anatolian typology]. Longbow loops are tall; crossbow loops are lower, for kneeling [V].
- **Variants:**
  - Fishtail foot for seeing the wall base; cross slits; round oillets [V].
  - Displaced cross slits at White Castle [V].
  - Gun-loops at Bodiam's gatehouse [V].
- **Multiple loops:** at Dover "defenders from three embrasures can shoot through the same
  arrowslit" [V Wikipedia]. Caernarfon has a similar three-way fork [V].
- **Arrow lockers:** "Some arrowslits, such as those at Corfe Castle, had lockers nearby to store
  spare arrows" [V].
- **How to tell a firing position:** Viollet-le-Duc reportedly judged that a stone bench in the
  niche marks a real firing position [S StoneKeep].
- **Numbers:** Beaumaris had about 300 firing positions, including 164 slits [V].

### 2.6 Hoardings and machicolations

- **Hoardings (hourds, brattices):** timber galleries bolted onto the wall top in wartime.
  - Beams went into square holes at wall-walk level, "0,30 c. × 0,30 c. environ", and hoardings
    "avaient rarement plus de 1m,95 c. de saillie (une toise)" (rarely projected more than 1.95 m,
    one toise) [V Viollet-le-Duc].
  - Coucy's keep had "quarante-huit consoles de pierre de 1m,07 de saillie" (48 stone corbels
    projecting 1.07 m), with the foot of the hoarding 40 m above the ditch [V].
  - Rochester keeps the rectangular holes under its battlements [V].
  - They were probably stored as prefabricated parts in peacetime [V Wikipedia]. Surviving or
    restored examples: Stokesay, Laval, Carcassonne, Castell Coch [V].
- **Machicolations:** the battlement pushed out on corbels, with openings between the corbels for
  dropping and shooting straight down [V].
  - The idea came from the Levant after the crusades and was "especially prevalent in Southern
    Europe". In England they are "usually restricted to the gateway" [V Wikipedia].
  - That makes machicolated tower tops the signature of the Iberian or French look, while plain
    parapets read English [D].
  - Examples: Conwy's barbican (earliest in Britain) [V]; Château Gaillard (among the first in
    Europe) [V]; Warwick's towers [V]; Bodiam's gatehouse [V].

### 2.7 Towers

- **Drum (round) towers:** Conwy's are about 21 m tall and about 9 m in diameter [V/S]. Harlech's
  corners are about 10 m across and 15-16 m tall, plus 5.8 m watch turrets [V].
- **D-shaped towers:** round outward and flat to the courtyard (Harlech gatehouse, Beaumaris
  sides) [V].
- **Polygonal:** Caernarfon [V].
- **Quatrefoil:** Warwick's Caesar's Tower [V].
- **Open-backed wall towers:** Conwy town walls, 4-5.2 m across, with timber bridges [V].
- **Watch turrets:** small turrets rising from the tower tops (Conwy, Harlech, the Eagle Tower's
  three) [V]. They give the silhouette its spikes [D].
- **Albarrana towers (Iberian):** "A defensive tower detached from the curtain wall and connected
  to it by a bridge or an arcade", with "a removable wooden section allowing the tower to be
  isolated". Built mostly from the 12th century in southern Spain and Portugal; the Torre de
  Espantaperros at Badajoz (c.1170, octagonal) may be the first [V Wikipedia]. São Jorge's Torre
  de Ulisses is labelled a "Torre Albarrã" [V e-cultura].
- **Image:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cordoue_-_Tour_de_la_Malmuerta_2.JPG

### 2.8 Spiral (newel) stairs

- **Sizes (Neil Guy, Castle Studies Group Journal 25, 2011-12) [V]:**
  - Principal keep stairs: cylinders up to 3.7 m (Orford), 4.25 m (Hedingham) and 5.0 m
    (Colchester), "sufficiently wide for two persons to walk up abreast".
  - Treads are normally 1.4-1.9 m wide; Colchester is exceptional at 2.15 m.
  - White Tower main stair: 3.6 m cylinder, 1.4 m tread, 20 cm riser.
  - Narrowest: Goodrich at 0.70 m. Norwich 0.86 m. Newark gatehouse 1.57 m with a 21 cm riser.
  - Tonbridge gatehouse risers are 28+ cm, "probably the steepest stair treads in England".
  - "The step or riser height is the Norman (unwritten) regulation 20 to 21 cms. ... a remarkably
    consistent height throughout the 12-15th century."
- **Useful arithmetic:** a 21 m tower is about 100-105 steps; 152 steps is about 30-32 m
  *(derived)*.
- **Direction and the myth:**
  - The "clockwise so right-handed defenders win" story traces to Theodore Andrea Cook's
    *Spirals in Nature and Art* (1902/1903) [V Newcastle Castle; talesoftimesforgotten].
  - Guy catalogues "more than 85 examples of anticlockwise (ACW) spiral stairs", rare before 1200
    but common from the 1240s "especially with the introduction of the twin-towered gatehouse",
    where they come in **mirror-image pairs** [V].
  - Edwardian counts: Caernarfon 5 clockwise / 4 anticlockwise, Conwy 6/1, Beaumaris 6/4 [V].
  - Stephen Friar's practical reading: stairs used for carrying loads went clockwise, "allowing the
    load to be carried in the right hand while climbing on the broad outer sections", and "stairs
    were used in pairs, one for ascending and one for descending" [V quoted by Guy].
  - The Newcastle Castle blog adds that the wider side is nearest the light [V].
- **Design use [D]:** pick stair direction by layout, not lore. Give the keep two stairs in opposite
  corners (Rochester, White Tower) so guards and the player can loop up and down separately.

### 2.9 Inner and outer bailey

- "The most important and prestigious buildings, such as the great hall and the keep ... were
  usually located in the inner bailey"; "the lower or outer bailey often held less important
  structures, such as stables" [V Wikipedia].
- Buildings lean against the curtain: Caernarfon has rows of corbels on the courtyard face where
  ranges once stood [V WRENN].
- **Design use [D]:**

  | Ward | Put here |
  |---|---|
  | Outer | stables, smithy, barracks, stores, the well if it is shared |
  | Inner | hall, chapel, kitchen, lord's lodgings |
  | Keep | treasure and target |

### 2.10 Great hall, solar, chapel, kitchen

- **Hall:**
  - Winchester (1222-35) is a "double cube" of 110 x 55 x 55 ft (33.5 x 16.8 x 16.8 m) [S].
  - Caernarfon is 30.5 m long [V]. Conwy is about 38 m long with stone transverse arches [S/V].
    Bodiam is 12.2 x 7.3 m [V].
  - Layout: the lord's private rooms (the solar) lie beyond the high (dais) end. The kitchen,
    buttery and pantry lie on the other side of the screens passage [S].
- **Kitchen:** often a separate building joined by a covered way [S]. Kenilworth's was 19 x 8 m [V].
- **Chapel:**
  - Harlech: over the gate passage [S].
  - Conwy: a chapel tower with a "watching chamber" (with its own latrine) for watching services
    in private [V Cadw].
  - Bodiam: projects into the moat [V].
  - Rochester: inside the keep, 8.5 x 4.6 m [V]. White Tower: St John's [V]. Dover: two in the
    forebuilding [S].
  - Château Gaillard's new chapel with an undercroft is how the middle ward fell [V].
- **Servants' sleeping:** most domestic servants slept in shared chambers in cellars or attics
  [S World History Encyclopedia].

### 2.11 Wells, cisterns and water

- **Wells:** Caernarfon 15 m [V]; Conwy 27.7 m [V]; Beeston 113 m, "the deepest castle well in
  England" [V]. Rochester has well-heads on every floor of the cross wall [V]. Dover piped water
  through the keep in lead pipes [V].
- **Iberian water structures:**
  - The **aljibe** (cistern): Olite's Torre del Aljibe [V].
  - The **coracha**: "Un lienzo de muralla que protege la comunicación entre una fortaleza y un
    punto concreto" (a stretch of wall protecting the route from a fortress to a nearby point),
    often ending in a "torre del agua" over a well or spring. It can have a parapet on both sides
    because it can be attacked from either flank [V es.wikipedia].
  - Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Coracha_de_Buitrago_sobre_el_río_Lozoya.jpg
- **Design use [D]:** a coracha is a ready-made secret route. It is a walled path from the cliff-top
  castle down to a spring or river gate.

### 2.12 Garderobes (latrines) and chutes

- **How they work:** "a simple hole discharging to the outside into a cesspit ... or the moat"
  [V Wikipedia]. Chutes run down inside the wall or discharge from corbelled boxes on the face.
- **Examples:**
  - Beaumaris: 32 latrines flushed by moat water [V].
  - Langley: a south-west tower with 12 garderobes, four per floor, whose shafts drop into a pit
    that a diverted stream could flush [S].
  - Conwy town walls: 12 in a row [V].
  - Harlech: a chute by the SW tower [V Cadw].
  - Caernarfon: most tower chambers have their own garderobe [V WRENN].
- **Design use [D]:** chutes are a classic "way in". Make them narrow and foul, and make the history
  honest: see Château Gaillard in section 3.
- **Image:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Garderobe,_Peveril_Castle,_Derbyshire.jpg

### 2.13 Sally ports, posterns and water gates

- **Definition:** a small, secure secondary door used for sorties, messengers or escape; often
  called a postern [V Wikipedia]. Much later military dictionaries give about 6 ft wide and
  8.5 ft high [V].
- **Real ones useful here:**
  - Harlech's water gate and stair [V].
  - Conwy's postern to the river dock [V].
  - Beaumaris's "Gate next the Sea" and dock [V].
  - Bodiam's south postern tower [V].
  - São Jorge's Porta da Traição, shielded by an outer wall [V].
  - Corfe's sally gate, used by traitors in 1646 [S].
  - Dover's postern, the only way in after sunset [S].
- **Image:** https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Old_West_Sally_Port,_Edinburgh_Castle.JPG

### 2.14 The keep (great tower)

- **Entrance and forebuilding:** the entrance is at first floor "to prevent the door from being
  easily broken down"; the entrance stair is protected by walls and a door, making a forebuilding
  [V Wikipedia].
  - Rochester: stair plus a 2.7 m drawbridge gap [V].
  - Dover: a three-towered forebuilding with two chapels [S].
  - White Tower: a removable timber stair [V].
- **Basement:** storage and the well [V].
- **Cross wall:** in larger keeps; the smaller ones have one room per floor [V].
- **Vaulting:** "Usually only the first floor would be vaulted in stone, with the higher storeys
  supported with timbers" [V].
- **Walls** could reach 24 ft (7.3 m) [V].
- **Inside the walls:** mural passages, galleries around double-height halls, garderobes and
  chapels in the wall thickness (White Tower gallery, Rochester second floor) [V].
- **Roof:** a parapet with turrets at the corners rising above it (Rochester's by 12 ft, Hedingham's
  by 15-25 ft) [V]. Hedingham's top floor may have replaced a pyramid-shaped roof [V].
- **Alternatives:** the keep-gatehouse (Harlech) and the Iberian torre del homenaje (Peñafiel
  34 m, Olite 40 m, Segovia's Juan II).

## 3. How real castles were broken into, or out of

### 3.1 Stealth and escalade

- **Edinburgh, 14 March 1314 (Thomas Randolph).**
  - William Francis's father had been keeper of the castle. As a youth Francis "loved a wench here in
    the town ... I made a ladder of ropes, and therewith slipped over the wall. Then I went down a
    narrow way I had spied in the crag" [V Barbour, *The Bruce* Book X, prose translation on
    electricscotland].
  - He offered "a place where with a short ladder of twelve foot we may easily climb the wall" [V].
  - Thirty men climbed on a dark night. The crag was "high and dreadful". A watchman above
    "cast a stone" and cried "Away, I see you well!", but "he saw them not a bit, and the stone
    flew over their heads, and they sat still" [V]. The watch then moved off talking, and the party
    climbed the last stretch. The wall was "very nigh twelve foot in height" [V].
  - Edinburgh's Castle Rock cliffs rise about 80 m above the surrounding land [V Wikipedia].
  - **Game use [D]:** an NPC-given secret path, a watch that passes on a timer, and a false-alarm
    bark that tests the player's nerve.
- **Roxburgh, night of 19 February 1314 (James Douglas).**
  - Sim of the Leadhouse made "ladders of hempen ropes with wooden steps ... a hook of iron, strong
    and square, which if once fixed on a battlement ... should hold securely" [V Barbour]. A
    Victorian retelling says iron steps [S].
  - About sixty men in black frocks over their armour crawled "on hands and feet, as if they were
    cows and oxen" [V]. The sentries remarked that some farmer had left his oxen out [V].
  - "One made a sound when the hook fastened hard in the battlement." A watchman came, and Leadhouse
    killed him at the top [V].
  - The garrison was feasting in the hall on Shrove Tuesday eve. The warden held out in the great
    tower until an arrow wound in the face made him surrender [V].
  - A modern analysis notes that captured rope ladders were put on display at Berwick as
    "ladders of wonderful construction" [V Scottish Historical Review].
- **Château Gaillard, 6 March 1204.**
  - Philip II's men got into the middle ward through John's new chapel. The French Wikipedia,
    following the eyewitness William the Breton, says it was "non pas par les latrines comme le veut
    une légende tenace, mais par l'une des fenêtres basses de la chapelle" (not by the latrines, as
    a stubborn legend has it, but through one of the chapel's low windows) [V].
  - English Wikipedia still gives the "garderobe (toilet chute)" version [V].
  - Once inside they set fire to buildings and lowered the bridge [V Wikipedia]. One French
    retelling has the defenders lighting a fire that the wind blew back at them [S lesandelys.com].
  - The outer ward fell to a mine under its great tower, dug after the ditch was filled [V/S].
    The inner ward fell to mining under the cover of a rock bridge [V].
  - The squire usually named is "Bogis" [S].
  - **Game use [D]:** the unbarred low window next to the latrines of a recently added building.
- **Antioch, 3 June 1098.** An insider, Firouz, "lowered a rope ladder" from the Tower of the Two
  Sisters. Bohemond's men climbed in through a window at night and more were hauled up by ropes [S].
- **Ideas with no documented case found:** climbing ivy or drainpipes. The real analogues are
  latrine chutes, a known crag path, and rope ladders with hooks. Treat ivy as a game invention [D].

### 3.2 Trickery at the gate

- **Linlithgow, 1313.**
  - The farmer William Bunnock (Binnock), who sold hay to the garrison, drove his wain into the gate
    with eight armed men hidden in the load [V Barbour summary].
  - "His man dropped his whip, and quickly hewed in two the trace. With that Bunnock nimbly dealt the
    porter a knock" [V]. Cutting the harness left the heavy cart stuck under the gate so it could
    not close.
  - Some tellings add that the portcullis ropes were cut [S].
- **Edinburgh, 17 April 1341 (William Douglas).** His men "disguised themselves as merchants from
  Leith bringing supplies to the garrison. Driving a cart into the entrance, they halted it there
  to prevent the gates from closing" [V Wikipedia]. A hidden force rushed in and the garrison of
  100 was killed [V].
- **Conwy, Good Friday 1 April 1401.** "The brothers and 44 men infiltrated Conway Castle. They
  pretended to be carpenters, and entered the castle while the guard were in church" [V Wikipedia].
  They "killed the two watchmen on duty" [V Conwy article], then held the castle for about two
  and a half months.
- **Lisbon, 1147 (legend).** Martim Moniz jammed an open door with his own body [V].
- **Corfe, 1645-46 (treachery).** Colonel Pitman offered to bring a hundred Royalist
  reinforcements; "the troops he returned with were Parliamentarians in disguise". They attacked
  from inside as the besiegers attacked from outside [V Wikipedia]. Other tellings say they came in
  by a sally gate [S].
- **Berwick, 1318 (bribery).** Peter Spalding, a burgess responsible for a section of the town
  wall, was bribed to let Douglas's men in [S].
- **Kildrummy, 1306 (inside job with fire).** The castle smith Osbourne, paid by the besiegers, set
  fire to the great hall, where grain was being stored. The legend says he was paid in gold poured
  molten down his throat [S].

### 3.3 Force: mining, tunnels, water

- **Rochester, 1215.**
  - King John wrote: "send to us with all speed by day and night forty of the fattest pigs of the
    sort least good for eating to bring fire beneath the tower" [V].
  - The burning props collapsed the mine and brought down one corner of the keep, "for such was the
    structure of the stronghold that a very strong wall separated the half that had fallen from the
    other" [V].
  - The garrison ate horse flesh and surrendered on 30 November [V].
- **Dover, 1216-17.** The barbican and north gate were badly damaged. During the siege "the English
  defenders tunnelled outwards and attacked the French" [V Wikipedia]. The tunnel system afterwards
  is described in section 1.2.
- **St Andrews, 1546-47.**
  - "Europe's only surviving mine and countermine" is cut through solid rock. "The defenders dug
    three mine tunnels before they reached the attackers" [V Wikipedia].
  - The tunnels were rediscovered in 1879 and visitors can still crawl through them [V].
- **Kenilworth, 21 June-13 December 1266.**
  - The water defences of the Great Mere defeated mining. "Barges were sent from Chester to attempt
    an attack via the lake; this did not work" [V].
  - A wooden siege tower held "two hundred crossbowmen or more" [V].
  - Hunger and disease ended the siege [V].
  - **Game use [D]:** water means no tunnels, so the ways in are a boat and a night crossing.

### 3.4 Escapes (for the exit)

- **Ranulf Flambard, 3 February 1101 (White Tower).** "Friends smuggled to Ranulf a rope in a
  flagon of wine, he gave the drink to his guards, and after they were drunk and asleep, climbed
  down the rope" [V Wikipedia].
- **Gruffydd ap Llywelyn, 1244 (White Tower).** He knotted sheets into a rope, which broke, and he
  fell to his death. One source says about ninety feet, from a top-floor window on the south side
  [S].
- **Empress Matilda, December 1142 (Oxford).** The Thames was frozen. Dressed in white against the
  snow, she was lowered from a tower on ropes (or left by a postern; accounts differ), crossed the
  ice and walked to Abingdon [S].
- **Alexander Stewart, Duke of Albany, 1479 (Edinburgh).** He "escaped by getting his guards
  drunk, and then lowering himself from a window on a rope" [V Wikipedia].
- **John Gerard, 4 October 1597 (Tower of London).** He "escaped on a rope strung across the Tower
  moat". It is often told with a cord thrown over the moat with a lead ball, and letters in orange
  juice as invisible ink [V/S].

### 3.5 Design takeaways [D]

- Real surprise attacks cluster on **feast nights and church services**: Shrove Tuesday at
  Roxburgh, Good Friday at Conwy. A garrison at mass in the chapel, or feasting in the hall, is a
  historically honest window.
- Most successful entries depended on **local knowledge** (Francis), an **insider** (Firouz,
  Spalding, Pitman, Osbourne) or a **trusted routine** (the hay cart, carpenters, merchants), more
  than on climbing skill. The level can offer each as a different route: the cliff path, the bribed
  guard, and the supply cart.
- Towers that seal off sections of wall (removable bridges, doors) punish a single climb. The player
  has to pick the right section of wall, not just any wall.

## 4. Life and light in a castle at night

### 4.1 Locking up and the watch (documented routines)

- **Dover (Constable Stephen de Pencester, 1260s-90s).** "At sunset the bridge shall be drawn, and
  the gates shut; afterwards the guard shall be mounted by twenty warders on the Castle walls." The
  rule was so strict that a king arriving after sunset "had to go to a postern where the Constable
  personally admitted him with only two or three of his suite, the rest of his company having to
  remain outside until full day" [S Bavington Jones, *Annals of Dover* 1916].
- **Dover castle-guard.** Knights' service was organised in 13 groups of four or five, each serving
  four weeks a year. Towers were named after the baronies that manned them [V Kent Archaeological
  Society].
- **Tower of London.**
  - Formal locking began after Edward III walked in unchallenged one night in December 1340. He
    "decreed that the castle should be locked at sunset and unlocked at sunrise" [V HRP].
  - Under Mary I (1555), nine Yeoman Warders patrolled by day and six at night. The keys were kept
    in a locked coffer "in the sight of the constable, the porter and two of the Yeoman Warders"
    [V HRP].
  - The ceremony is still done by candle lantern, with the challenge "Halt, who comes there?" /
    "The keys." / "Whose keys?" [V/S].
- **The royal household's night watchman (Black Book of Edward IV, 1478).** "A wayte, that nyghtely
  from Mychelmas to Shreve Thorsdaye pipe the watch within this courte fowere tymes; in the somere
  nightes three tymes and maketh bon gayte at every chambre doare and offyce, as well as for feare
  of pyckeres and pilfers" [V 1911 Britannica "Waits"]. In short: four piped rounds a night in
  winter and three in summer, checking every door, explicitly against thieves.
- **Real garrison sizes were small:**

  | Castle | Garrison | Tag |
  |---|---|---|
  | Harlech | 36 in all: a constable, 30 men including 10 crossbowmen, a chaplain, smith, carpenter and mason | [V] |
  | Conwy | 30 soldiers including 15 crossbowmen, plus a carpenter, chaplain, blacksmith, engineer and mason | [V] |
  | Caernarfon (1403-04) | about 30 | [V] |
  | Edinburgh (1341) | 100 | [V] |

  **[D]:** a big patrolling garrison is itself the "larger than life" exaggeration. Keep the named
  trades: chaplain, smith, carpenter.

### 4.2 Light sources and where they belong

- **What was actually used:**
  - Rushlights (rush pith dragged through fat), tallow candles for most people, beeswax for church
    and aristocracy [V Chadwick].
  - Oil lamps; lanterns ("simply candles in a metal frame"); torches in brackets; the hall's hearth
    [V/S].
  - **Cressets:** a stone block with hollows for fat or oil and a floating wick, or a metal basket
    on a pole used as a beacon [V].
  - Durham's monks had a stone with "twelve cressetts ... everye night one of them was lighted ...
    to give light to the monks at midnight" [V Wikipedia quoting a 1593 account of Durham].
  - Torches "have not survived well in the archaeological record" [V Chadwick]. The idea of a torch
    every ten metres is a "Hollywood-ism"; people carried their light with them [S Tastes of History].
- **Allowances:** a household officer was entitled to "one small wax candle and 24 candle ends" a day
  [V Chadwick].
- **Curfew:** from "couvre-feu", cover the fire. The tradition is a law of William the Conqueror that
  all fires and lights be covered at an eight o'clock bell, because of fire in timber buildings. A
  "curfew" was also the lid for banking embers overnight [V Wikipedia].
- **Chapel:** a sanctuary lamp, usually red, burns constantly near the reserved sacrament [V
  Wikipedia]. In a castle chapel at night this is the one light that is always on [D].
- **Placement plan [D, based on the above]:**

  | Place | Light at night |
  |---|---|
  | Gatehouse and porter | lantern at the gate; the brightest spot outside the keep; the keys go in with the watch |
  | Wall-walks | dark; watchmen keep their night vision; a cresset or brazier only at a few fixed watch posts and tower tops |
  | Great hall | embers of the hearth; sleepers; a single rushlight; bright only during a feast |
  | Kitchen and bakehouse | banked fire under a curfew lid; the ovens relit before dawn |
  | Smithy (outer bailey, for fire safety [S]) | banked forge glow |
  | Stables | a shielded lantern only; grooms and hay upstairs (loft and quarters likely [V Exeter]) |
  | Chapel | red sanctuary lamp, plus candles when a service is on |
  | Keep | candles in the lord's chamber (the target's light seen from far below) |

### 4.3 Stables, smithy, bell

- **Stables:** Kenilworth's Tudor range was 180 x 21 ft with 30 stalls for great horses and 20 for
  geldings; the survivor is about 49 x 10 m [V]. Typical tie stalls are 4-5 ft wide by 9-10 ft long
  (for modern horses), in rows with drains, partitions and a hayloft above [V Exeter].
- **Smithy:** a castle smith was on the permanent staff at Harlech and Conwy [V].
- **Bells:** the curfew bell [V]. Alarm and chapel bells are standard design [D]. At Dover the Roman
  pharos served as the church's bell tower [V English Heritage].

### 4.4 Iberian night detail

- Segovia's Terraza de los Moros was where Henry IV's Moorish guard kept watch [V].
- Almourol, Segovia and Olite all have tower-top walks made for looking out: atalaya (watch tower),
  garitas (bartizans) [V/D].

## 5. PS2-detail modelling notes and a castle kit [D unless tagged]

### 5.1 What carries the silhouette

These read against a moonlit, fogged sky at 128-256 px textures:

1. **Tower tops that break the wall line:** watch turrets (Conwy, Harlech 5.8 m, the Eagle Tower's
   three), keep turrets 3.7-7.6 m above the parapet (Rochester, Hedingham) [V]. This is the single
   biggest "castle" read.
2. **Crenellation rhythm:** real geometry, because the sky shows through the crenels. A 3.0 m bay
   (2.1 + 0.9) reads well at distance [V Conwy numbers].
3. **Machicolation bands** under tower parapets, mainly on Iberian-looking towers (Segovia,
   Warwick). A row of corbels is a strong horizontal shadow line. Model it on hero towers and
   texture it on the rest.
4. **Roofs:** conical slate spires say Segovia and Iberia [V Philip II]. English towers read better
   with flat roofs behind parapets. Use spires on only a few towers so they stay special.
5. **Chimneys and banners:** Caernarfon's Queen's Tower has three octagonal chimney shafts behind the
   parapet [V WRENN]. Conwy had heraldic banners and shields hung on the battlements [V Cadw].
   Banners are cheap animated silhouette.
6. **The keep's mass:** a single tall block 25-35 m high [V] that is visible from everywhere in the
   level. Keep it the tallest object, with nothing else near its height.

### 5.2 Texture versus geometry

| Texture (baked or painted) | Geometry (keep it) |
|---|---|
| Masonry coursing; Caernarfon-style colour bands [V]; putlog holes (scaffold sockets) | Crenels and merlons |
| Arrow loops beyond about 15 m (a dark slit with an alpha fishtail) | Arrow-loop embrasures the player can use as peepholes (a real splayed recess 0.7-1.0 m deep) |
| String courses, plinth batter lines | The keep's forebuilding stair, gate passage vault and murder holes |
| Lime render patches over stone (Conwy was white [V]); moss and soot streaks under spouts | Corbels of machicolations and hoarding holes near the play space |
| Hoarding beam holes on distant walls | Turrets, stair turrets, chimneys |

- **Putlog-hole note:** Master James's Savoyard masons set putlog holes "in a sloping line up flat
  and curved walls" (helical scaffolds) [S]. A spiral of dark dots up a tower is a cheap, authentic
  texture detail.
- **Vertex shading:** bake darkening into the wall foot, the merlon undersides, and the insides of
  crenels and loops. Render (white or ochre) versus bare stone is the main material variation, and
  whitewashed walls under moonlight give strong value contrast for readability [V history + D].

### 5.4 Scale: grand but controlled [D]

- **Keep real vertical sizes and compress horizontal ones.** Real wards are small: Conwy's inner
  ward is 25 x 22 m, Harlech's 39 x 47-53 m, Beaumaris's about 55-59 m square [V]. Real towers are
  far apart (45 m or more). Pull towers closer and keep the walls tall, and the castle feels bigger
  without growing the map.
- **Put the keep on the highest rock and shift the approach sideways.** The player sees it from
  many angles while climbing. That is the Segovia prow and Harlech's cliff stair.
- **Let the cliffs do the height.** Segovia's "80 m" is mostly rock [U/derived]. A 25-35 m keep on a
  40-60 m rock reads as enormous and stays affordable to build.
- **Players notice repeated clutter faster than repeated architecture** (Burgess, on Oblivion
  dungeons [V]). Spend the unique-asset budget on props: banners, carts, the hay wain, the
  chapel's lamp. Repeat walls freely.

## 6. Myths and surprises worth knowing

- **Castles were often white.** Conwy was lime-rendered white with banners and painted shutters
  [V Cadw]. The White Tower was whitewashed in 1240 [V]. A moonlit white castle is historically
  justified.
- **Clockwise stairs "for the defender" are a modern myth.** More than 85 anticlockwise stairs are
  catalogued, and Edwardian castles mix both [V Guy]. The claim goes back to a 1902/1903 book on
  spirals [V].
- **Château Gaillard was probably not taken "up the toilet".** The eyewitness chronicler says a
  chapel window [V].
- **Garrisons were tiny:** about 30 men, plus a chaplain and a smith [V]. At Dover twenty warders
  held the walls at night [S].
- **The night watch was explicitly anti-thief.** Edward IV's household watchman checked every door
  "for feare of pyckeres and pilfers" [V].
- **At Harlech the chapel sat over the gate passage** and housed the portcullis winches [S].
- **Famous silhouettes are partly later work.** Segovia's slate spires are 16th century and were
  rebuilt after the 1862 fire [V]. Almourol's even merlons are 19th-20th-century restoration [V].
- **Tall-tower figures often include the rock:** Segovia's "80 m" [U], and Warwick's towers at
  39/45 m in books versus 29/40 m on the official site [U].
- **Conwy's town-wall towers had removable bridges.** Losing one wall section did not lose the next
  [V].
- **Rochester's keep had a well-head on every floor** in its cross wall [V].
- **Murder holes rarely poured oil.** Hot oil was "rarely used because of its cost"; water to
  quench fires and stones were likelier, and the holes may also have been used to talk between
  floors [V Wikipedia].
