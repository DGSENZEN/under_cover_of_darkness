# The Old Town: Porto references (Oct 2 2026)

## How to read this

This report gathers Porto references for the Old Town of "the city on the rock": the dense, stair-laced hill between the harbour wall and the cathedral square. It goes beyond `houses_ribeira.md` (Ribeira quay, house fronts, balconies, roofs; read that file for facade fine detail) and covers the quarter behind and above the quay: Barredo, the Sé hill, the merchants' lanes and the culverted stream, the Fernandine wall and its gates, Miragaia, Rua das Flores, Vitória and its Jewish quarter, the landmark towers and tiled churches, and above all the Porto house, inside and out. Each place has five parts: **What and why**, **Features** (facts with numbers; the source link follows each claim or group of claims), **Model it** (a PS2-budget modular kit), **Play it** (what a Thief-style player gets) and **Images** (Wikimedia Commons pages). Facts carry their date, because the game mixes periods on purpose: the medieval house (13th-15th c.), the mercantile house (16th-17th c.), the Almada-era Enlightenment house (c.1760-1800) and the Liberal 19th-century house are all usable. Numbers I could not find are marked **(not found)**. Every dimension in a "Model it" block that is not cited is a **design value**: my proposal for the kit, not a historical claim. Portuguese terms are kept where the user may meet them in references: *casa burguesa* (burgher house), *loja* (ground-floor shop or store), *sobrado* (an upper timber floor; "a house of two sobrados" has a ground floor plus two floors), *claraboia* (roof skylight over the stair), *trapeira* or *mirante* (dormer or rooftop lookout room), *tabique* (timber-framed lath-and-plaster wall), *quintal* or *logradouro* (back yard), *chafariz* (public fountain), *alminhas* (wayside shrines to the souls in purgatory).

## 1. Barredo and its stairs

**What and why:** Barredo is the residential slope stacked between the Ribeira quay and the Sé, behind the coloured riverfront, the densest and steepest part of old Porto. It is the closest real model for the Old Town's lower terraces: stairs instead of streets, arches, houses grown on medieval ground floors.

**Features**
- The oldest link between the river settlement and the hilltop ran up Rua de D. Hugo, through the Porta das Verdades of the old wall, then down the Escadas do Barredo. It was the shortest route and the steepest; a gentler route via Rua dos Mercadores, Bainharia and Rua Escura came later ([pt.wikipedia: Ribeira](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribeira_(Porto))).
- The Escadas das Verdades were once called the Escadas das Mentiras ("stairs of lies") ([pt.wikipedia: Escadas das Verdades](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escadas_das_Verdades)). Their gate, the Porta das Mentiras, was renamed for Nossa Senhora das Verdades in the 14th c. ([pt.wikipedia: Muralha Primitiva](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muralha_Primitiva)). The route runs from Rua de D. Hugo by the 18th-c. chapel of Nossa Senhora da Verdade, past the Romanesque wall and the Arco do Colégio, down to Rua dos Canastreiros ([All About Portugal](https://www.allaboutportugal.pt/en/porto/monuments/escadas-do-barredo)).
- The **Arco das Verdades** is a house built over the passage between the Escadas do Barredo and the Escadas das Verdades. The city archive dates the houses at nos. 48 and 50 to the 16th c., nos. 42-44 to the 15th-16th c., no. 139 to the 16th-17th c. and no. 18 to the 18th c. ([Gisa: Escadas do Barredo](https://gisaweb.cm-porto.pt/places/11055/documents/)). Barredo also has a wall niche, the Nicho do Senhor da Boa Fortuna ([Gisa document 916303](https://gisaweb.cm-porto.pt/units-of-description/documents/916303/)).
- **Escadas do Codeçal:** 270 m long, medieval ([pt.wikipedia: Escadas do Codeçal](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escadas_do_Code%C3%A7al)), with more than 400 steps ([Porto Secreto](https://portosecreto.co/escadas-do-codecal/)). In the Middle Ages this stair was the wall-walk of the Fernandine wall, linking the Santa Clara convent to the Postigo da Areia by the river. Halfway down stands the Recolhimento do Ferro (begun 1752), a house of enclosure for women, from 1729 on land given on condition that it take in "repentant" women; later it also held brides placed there by court order ([pt.wikipedia: Escadas do Codeçal](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escadas_do_Code%C3%A7al)). Building the Luís I bridge in the 1880s cut away part of the stair ([same](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escadas_do_Code%C3%A7al)).
- **Torre da Rua de Baixo** (no. 5): a five-storey tower-house fronting two parallel streets. Its tympanum and the arch of one original lancet suggest the 13th c.; the square windows and perhaps the top floor are later ([All About Portugal](https://www.allaboutportugal.pt/pt/porto/monumentos/torre-da-rua-de-baixo)). It is the oldest surviving civil building in Barredo ([Visit Porto](https://visitporto.travel/pt-PT/poi/5cd04b50f979e0000196beb7)).
- The lanes include Rua da Lada, Travessa do Barredo, Rua and Travessa dos Canastreiros (photographed 1965) ([Gisa](https://gisaweb.cm-porto.pt/places/11055/documents/)), Rua de Baixo, Largo do Terreiro and the dead-end Rua do Barredo ([FAUP route study](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/70428/2/23693.pdf)).
- **Density:** Távora's 1969 renewal study split Barredo into 14 blocks and found whole families living in one room, buildings in ruin and no basic sanitation ([Flores, "The Barredo's urban renewal study"](https://exa.ai/library/publication/pk0spcg75lw)). A 1950s FAUP diploma study of the Sé slope estimated about 1,329 inhabitants per hectare in the blocks it surveyed, with houses averaging about three storeys ([CODA 136](https://repositorio-tematico.up.pt/bitstream/10405/48070/1/CODA_136_pe.pdf)). The municipal architect who rebuilt Barredo (CRUARB, 1974 onward) explains that most houses rest on a medieval ground floor with later floors on top. Some cleared plots were deliberately left empty to let light into the quarter. Stone bases that once held the poles for drying fish survive ([Oporto Encanta](https://www.oportoencanta.com/2016/08/a-historia-e-arquitetura-do-barredo.html)).
- **Lane widths:** for Barredo itself (not found). As a general medieval guide, a Porto thesis cites Leguay: streets of 10-12 m were rare, most were 2-5 m, and alleys of 1-2 m were normal ([Santos 2010, p. 27](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/56102/2/TESEMESMARIAHELENASANTOS000128424.pdf)). The rise from the river to the cathedral terrace is about 80 m; the hilltop's height is given in a blog ([Porto d'Honra](https://www.portodhonra.com/2024/07/um-passeio-pela-pena-ventosa-o-morro-da.html)).

**Model it:** Build Barredo as **terraces with stair-lanes, not streets**. Kit (design values): `StairFlight_1.5w` and `StairFlight_2.5w` (risers 0.18 m, treads 0.30-0.35 m outdoors, granite steps as geometry and their wear as vertex paint); `Landing_2x2` and `Landing_3x4` for turns and doorsteps; `ArchHouse_Over` (a one-room house bridging a 2.5 m stair, with a 2.2 m clear arch: one mesh with its underside lit dark by vertex paint); `RetainingWall_3h`, the stone wall that holds each terrace, with doors cut into it (the "medieval ground floor" read). Houses: the `Medieval_4.5` and `Mercantile_6` lots from the kit at the end, set on stepped plinths so that a house's front door is on one lane and its back door two storeys higher on the next (the Torre da Rua de Baixo fronts two parallel streets). Keep lanes 1.2-3 m; open one empty "light plot" per block as a small yard. Laundry lines, flower pots and the fish-pole bases are cheap alpha cards and props.

**Play it:** The stair-lane is a vertical corridor: guards see you from above and below, so sightlines are long along the stair and nil across it. Arch-houses give an overhead crossing (go through the room over the stair and the patrol below never sees you). Houses with doors on two levels are shortcuts between terraces. Empty light plots are drop points and hiding yards. A tower-house like Rua de Baixo is a natural "key building": five storeys, two street fronts, old lancets for an entry. The Recolhimento do Ferro (a walled women's house on the stair) makes a tense social stealth space with a gatekeeper and a single door.

## 2. The Sé hill (Penaventosa): the cathedral precinct

**What and why:** The hilltop of Pena Ventosa holds the cathedral, the bishop's palace, the old town hall tower and the canons' street, all inside a Romanesque ring wall. It is the model for the Old Town's crown, the cathedral square. The key lesson: **the big open square is a 1940s creation**; before that the cathedral stood in a tight knot of lanes, which is better for stealth.

**Features**
- **The old ring wall (Cerca Velha):** of Roman origin (3rd c.), rebuilt in the 12th c.; about 750 m around, enclosing less than 4 ha; only one small tower and a short stretch survive, rebuilt in the mid-20th c. It had four gates: the Porta de Vandoma, the widest and the only one for carts (demolished 1855); the Porta de São Sebastião by the old town hall (demolished 1819); the Porta de Sant'Ana (demolished 1821); and the Porta das Mentiras/Verdades ([pt.wikipedia: Muralha Primitiva](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muralha_Primitiva)). The hill was planned radially and concentrically, with the cathedral as its centre ([SIPA: Zona histórica](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/Site/APP_PagesUser/SIPA.aspx?id=6144)).
- **The cathedral (Sé):** a Romanesque cathedral of the 12th-13th c. with an aisled nave under a barrel vault, among the first Portuguese buildings with flying buttresses; a Gothic cloister (14th-15th c.); a Baroque side loggia by Nasoni (c.1736); a new main portal (c.1772) ([pt.wikipedia: Sé do Porto](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9_do_Porto)). The facade has two square towers, each with two buttresses and a cupola, and a rose window under a crenellated arch that makes it read as a fortified church. The cloister's tiles (1729-1731) are by Valentim de Almeida ([en.wikipedia: Porto Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porto_Cathedral)). A silver altar was hidden from the French in 1807 behind a hastily built plaster wall ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9_do_Porto)).
- **Paço Episcopal (bishop's palace):** rebuilt in the 18th c. with Nasoni's influence on the west and south fronts. On the ground floor are five panelled doors and seven low barred windows. The piano nobile has 24 windows, 12 on each side, paired vertically, each opening onto a small wrought-iron balcony. The grand stair at the back of the vestibule rises under a lantern. The palace stands on huge rocks, and its main front is the lowest. It housed the town hall from 1916 to 1956 ([pt.wikipedia: Paço Episcopal](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pa%C3%A7o_Episcopal_do_Porto)).
- **The Terreiro da Sé is a 1940s clearance.** Demolished then were the Capela dos Alfaiates (rebuilt elsewhere in 1953), the old bishop's prison (Cadeia do Bispo), a count's house with a coat of arms, the Largo do Açougue (butchers' square), and Rua das Tendas, Rua do Faval and Rua da Francisca. The pillory in the middle was put up by the city in 1945 ([pt.wikipedia: Terreiro da Sé](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terreiro_da_S%C3%A9)). The medieval bishop also had a pillory, the "Picota do Bispo", at the foot of the cathedral stairs before 1422 ([Vias Romanas: Rua Escura](https://www.viasromanas.pt/docs/SePorto_Escura.html)).
- **Casa dos 24 (old town hall):** a 15th-c. tower built against the old wall only 7 m from the cathedral. It was of granite, 100 palmos high (about 22 m), crenellated, with several floors and a gilded ceiling in its upper hall. After the council left in the mid-16th c. it served as a prison and as a shelter for prostitutes and the homeless ([pt.wikipedia: Antiga Casa da Câmara](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiga_Casa_da_C%C3%A2mara_(Porto))).
- **Rua de D. Hugo** (the street behind the cathedral, first recorded before 1221, once Rua de Redemoinho and Rua de Trás da Sé): 325 m long ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_de_D._Hugo)). It was a canons' street: by 1493 the chapter owned about 65 houses there, two of them tower-houses, plus walled yards with orange, lemon, plum and fig trees. The bishop's palace and its garden took much of the west side. A 1551 lease describes a front room 9 varas long and 6 wide with a shop of the same size below, and a kitchen in a balcony 5 by 3 varas ([Viela dos Cónegos study, UCP](https://repositorio.ucp.pt/bitstreams/53001d34-7231-4b6a-af96-02bcec5a7463/download)). At no. 5, digs under a house rebuilt inside Gothic walls found layers down to the 4th c., 3 m deep ([en.wikipedia: Casa da Rua de D. Hugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_da_Rua_de_D._Hugo)). The Casa do Dr. Domingos Barbosa (now the Guerra Junqueiro museum) was built in 1730-1746 for a cathedral dignitary, a Baroque granite house with an entrance court ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa-Museu_Guerra_Junqueiro)).
- **Rua das Aldas / Sant'Ana:** the old Jewish quarter ran along today's Rua de Sant'Ana to Rua Escura and Rua Chã ([Rede de Judiarias](https://www.redejudiariasportugal.com/index.php/pt/cidades/porto)). A medieval lease swapped houses on Rua das Aldas for a stable, half a yard and a house with an upper floor ([UCP](https://repositorio.ucp.pt/bitstreams/53001d34-7231-4b6a-af96-02bcec5a7463/download)).
- **Rua Escura and the Aljube:** the clergy prison (Aljube) faced the Fonte de São Sebastião. The fountain was moved in 1636 to rest against a terrace wall, and again in 1940. The Escadas da Rainha climbed from here to the cathedral yard ([Vias Romanas: Rua Escura](https://www.viasromanas.pt/docs/SePorto_Escura.html)). The Chafariz de São Miguel, probably by Nasoni, has a long curved backrest, a column topped by Saint Michael, and a spout in a niche ([DGPC](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=73486)).

**Model it:** Build the precinct at its **pre-1940 density**: the cathedral and palace as two big hero meshes (cathedral at about 1,500-2,500 tris, design value, its towers as simple boxes with buttress slabs and cupola caps; crenellation as an alpha-tested strip), a small irregular square in front, and lanes closing in. Modules (design values): `RingWall_Romanesque` (4 m high, 1.5 m thick, rubble texture), `Gate_Vandoma` (a cart-wide arch about 3.5 m), `Gate_Postern` (1.2 m), `TowerHouse_22m` for the Casa dos 24 (6 x 6 m footprint, crenellated top; footprint not found), `Palace_Front_Bay` (one 3.6 m bay with a ground window, a paired piano-nobile window, an iron balcony and an eaves cornice: repeat 12 times per side). Canon houses: the `Mercantile_6` lot with a walled `Quintal_Fruit` (an orange tree card). The pillory is an optional landmark; it is 1945, so use it only if you want the later look.

**Play it:** A palace whose main front is lowest because it sits on rock is a gift: cliff-foot entries, high balconies over a drop, barred ground windows (pick or lever them), and a grand stair under a lantern (a lit hub that every route crosses). A prison tower 7 m from the cathedral, with a gilded hall at the top, is a vertical heist. The canons' street with walled orchards gives garden-hopping routes behind the houses. Hide the small Terreiro among butchers' stalls and tailors' chapel walls so that the open square stays small and patrollable.

## 3. The merchants' lanes below the Sé: Mercadores, Souto and the Rio da Vila

**What and why:** Between the cathedral hill and the river runs the medieval merchants' quarter: stone tower-houses, a back alley along the old wall, tanneries cut into the rock, and a stream with mills that was later vaulted over. This is the Old Town's "middle layer" and its underworld.

**Features**
- **Rua dos Mercadores:** houses mostly of stone, of two, three and four storeys, on the tower-house pattern. A 1317 contract orders stone houses with the first floor jettied over the street (a *sobrado ladrom*). In 1355 a builder was paid 20 morabitinos to raise houses of three storeys. A tower-house at nos. 156-158, drawn in 1866, had a ground floor plus three, two openings per floor, and Gothic windows at the top. Nos. 182-184 are a 16th-c. house with a gargoyle; only its ground floor is stone, the rest is taipa, and its timber balconies imitate stone ones. Alminhas stand at nos. 89 and 131 ([Vias Romanas: Mercadores](https://www.viasromanas.pt/doc/SePorto_Mercadores.html)).
- **The blocked back alley:** a 1438 lease says these houses backed onto "the alley that goes round the old wall". The alley ran behind the houses at the foot of the Romanesque wall to Rua de Sant'Ana. A 1802 inspection found it walled off as a "blocked alley" and left it closed ([Vias Romanas: Mercadores](https://www.viasromanas.pt/doc/SePorto_Mercadores.html)).
- **Rua do Souto and the Cruz do Souto:** the longest medieval street, recorded from 1234, running from the Cruz do Souto crossroads across the Rio da Vila stream. A lime kiln stood in an alley (1259). A 1446 exchange mentions upper-floor stone houses with a stone tower behind them. Water mills stood on the Rio da Vila (1467). There was a heavy cluster of tanning pits (*aloques* and *pelames*) cut into the rock: 31 municipal ones in the early modern period, 23 of them by Rua dos Pelames. A stone well by the tanneries served as a public wash place and to fight fires (1731). In 1731 houses were licensed to be rebuilt "on top of an arca" (a vault) by the Rio da Vila, with the water pipe for the Fonte do Souto secured outside. The Cruz do Souto was paved by the late 15th c., and tower-houses at its back faced Rua Escura ([Vias Romanas: Rua do Souto](https://www.viasromanas.pt/docs/SePorto_Souto.html)).
- The Rio da Vila now runs culverted under Rua Mouzinho da Silveira, laid out in 1875 ([SIPA: Zona histórica](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/Site/APP_PagesUser/SIPA.aspx?id=6144)). A Roman road ran through Mercadores, Bainharia and Pelames ([SIPA: Centro Histórico](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/Site/APP_PagesUser/SIPA.aspx?id=6143)).
- **Rua Nova** (now Rua do Infante D. Henrique, begun 1395): the wide royal street, built to a uniform pattern, mostly ground floor plus two. Stone went up only to the first floor (the shop), and the upper floors were timber, adobe and taipa. Some houses had to carve the royal arms in stone on their fronts and paint them. Leases give houses of 7.10 x 5.19 m and 12.56 x 3.73 m (Oliveira Marques' braça = 10 palmos = 1.82 m). Kitchens were on the upper floor. Latrines (*privadas*) appear in only some leases, and where they stood is unknown. A fire burned the area in 1491/92 ([Santos 2010](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/56102/2/TESEMESMARIAHELENASANTOS000128424.pdf)).

**Model it:** Kit (design values): `TowerHouse_Merchant` (5 x 8 m footprint, ground plus three storeys at 3.2 m, two openings per floor, Gothic paired lancets on the top floor as a decal on a flat recess); `Jetty_Strip` (the upper floor oversailing by 0.4 m on a beam row, timber vertex-coloured); `BackAlley_1m` (a 1 m slot between a house rear and the old-wall mesh, ending in a bricked `BlockedArch`); `TanPit_Cluster` (round and square pits 1-1.5 m across, sunk into a rock floor, dark water as a flat unlit plane plus decal staining); `Stream_Culvert` (a 2 m wide barrel vault over the stream, 1.6 m clear, houses sitting on top); `Mill_Wheel` prop. Royal-arms decal on Rua Nova-type fronts.

**Play it:** The tannery yard is a smelly, low-light, noisy zone (water and scraping cover footsteps). The culverted stream is the thief's sewer route under the merchants' houses, with trapdoors into cellars built "on the arca". The walled-off back alley is a forgotten path between house rears and the old wall: open it with a crowbar, and it threads behind a whole street. Tower-houses are loot vaults with narrow stairs and one room per floor.

## 4. The Fernandine walls, gates and posterns

**What and why:** The 14th-c. wall joined the Sé and Vitória hills to the river and fixed the old town's limits for four centuries. It is the model for the harbour wall the Old Town climbs from, and for houses built against and over a wall.

**Features**
- **Dates:** begun 1336 under Afonso IV; the Postigo do Carvão bore an inscription dated 1348; finished c.1376 under King Fernando ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5461)). Another source gives c.1370 ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muralhas_fernandinas_do_Porto)).
- **Size:** perimeter nearly 2,500 m; about 10 m high; inner and outer parapets, the outer with thick merlons; numerous square towers (30, according to João de Barros in the 16th c.) of strong ashlar, some hollow and some solid, 14-21 m high; walls 2.5 m wide, at least in the stretches near Santa Clara ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5461)). Wikipedia gives 2,600 m around, 9 m high (30 feet), 44.5 ha enclosed and 17 gates and posterns ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muralhas_fernandinas_do_Porto)); a walking guide gives 14-16 m high ([WalkinPorto](https://walkinporto.com/muralha-fernandina-o-reflexo-do-grande-desenvolvimento-do-burgo/)). Within 200 years about 10,000 people lived inside ([WalkinPorto](https://walkinporto.com/muralha-fernandina-o-reflexo-do-grande-desenvolvimento-do-burgo/)). Tower footprints: (not found).
- **Gates and posterns (17):** Porta Nova/Nobre, Postigo dos Banhos, da Lingueta, da Alfândega, do Carvão (the only survivor), Porta da Ribeira, Postigos do Pelourinho, da Forca, da Madeira, da Lada, Porta do Sol, Porta de Cimo de Vila, Porta de Carros, Porta de Santo Elói, Porta do Olival, Porta das Virtudes and Postigo de São João Novo ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muralhas_fernandinas_do_Porto)). The river front had many posterns. Gates grew from posterns over time: Carros became a gate in 1521, Santo Elói in 1764, the Penedo postern became the Porta do Sol in 1768. A round fort was built at the Porta Nobre corner in 1580. Demolitions ran from the Ribeira gate in 1774 to the Porta do Sol in 1875 ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5461)).
- **Houses against and over the wall:** in 1529, 360 braças of wall fell between Santo Elói and the Olival. In the first half of the 19th c. the river stretch in front of Barredo was pierced with a row of round arches separated by small oculi; this is the Muro da Ribeira or dos Bacalhoeiros ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5461)). Good stretches survive "behind the houses" of Rua das Taipas and Rua Dr. Barbosa de Castro. In 1989-90 two buried stretches and an attached turret were found more than 2 m under 19th-c. demolition rubble at the Cordoaria ([SIPA](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/site/app_pagesuser/sipa.aspx?id=5461)). Back yards in Rua Barbosa de Castro are divided by panels of the wall ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/historia.htm)). A model of a house in Rua Dr. Barbosa de Castro has its rectangular skylight on the rear roof slope "against the Fernandine wall" ([Coimbra 2024, annex](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). At the Caminho Novo, houses are built directly against the masonry ([Nomado](https://www.nomadotravel.app/en/attractions/muralha-fernandina)). The Escadas do Codeçal were the wall-walk ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escadas_do_Code%C3%A7al)).
- The curfew bell hung in the tower of the Porta do Olival until 1583 ([JN](https://www.jn.pt/arquivo/artigo/que-policiamento-havia-na-cidade-de-quinhentos/453486)).

**Model it:** Kit (design values from the cited sizes): `CityWall_Run_8m` (10 m high, 2.5 m thick, a 1.6 m wall-walk with an inner parapet and outer merlons, ashlar texture at 256 px tiling every 2 m, vertex-painted moss low down); `WallTower_Square` (7 x 7 m, 14 / 17 / 21 m high variants, `Hollow` and `Solid` versions; footprint is a design value); `Gate_Main` (a 3.5 m arch in a tower), `Postern` (1.2 x 2.2 m arch with stairs through the wall), `WallArcade_Bacalhoeiros` (round arches with an oculus between each pair, shops inside). Houses: a `House_AgainstWall` variant whose rear wall *is* the city wall (no rear windows, skylight on the rear slope, a top-floor door onto the wall-walk).

**Play it:** The wall-walk is a guarded highway; houses built against it give a ladder of roofs up to it and a door out of the top floor. Posterns are low, dark choke points (the river-front ones open straight onto the quay). Hollow towers are climbable interiors with stairs; solid ones are only roofs. A stretch of wall hidden behind house yards, or buried under rubble, is a forgotten route.

## 5. Miragaia

**What and why:** The fishermen's and shipwrights' suburb outside the wall to the west, a riverfront of arcaded houses and the parish church of the fishermen. It is the model for an extramural, rougher waterside quarter at the foot of the Old Town.

**Features**
- In 1258 the royal inquiry noted 75 houses built near Monchique in under 15 years ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/historia.htm)). Miragaia sits outside the walls, on an old river beach that served as a medieval shipyard. The houses are now behind a big flood wall, and their upper floors are built over arches ([LocalPorto](https://www.localporto.com/pt/miragaia-bairro-historico-porto/)). Its famous arcades were the fronts of houses facing the sand. Floods could still bring boats up Rua de Miragaia in 1966 ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/historia.htm)). A 1824 plan shows the "covered spaces" (*cobertos*) of the beach houses ([Gisa: Igreja de Miragaia](https://gisaweb.cm-porto.pt/topics/5733/documents/)).
- Miragaia had its own Jewish quarter with a synagogue and cemetery. The Igreja de São Pedro de Miragaia was almost entirely rebuilt from 1740 ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/historia.htm)). It is the fishermen's parish ([Vai Ver](https://www.vaiver.com/porto/miragaia-igreja-de-sao-pedro-de-miragaia/)) and has tiles from the Vale da Piedade factory, laid 1863-1876. A fountain of 1821 by the church spouts from a dolphin-like beast. The Rio Frio stream crossed the parish and was used to wash clothes ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/patrim.htm)).
- The **Chafariz da Colher**, built of granite in 1491, was paid for by a food tax called the "colher" (spoon); it was rebuilt in 1629 and is now in a hidden spot on Rua de Miragaia ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chafariz_da_Colher)). It is sheltered under a first-floor balcony ([Junta de Miragaia](http://www.j-f.org/miragaia/historia.htm)). The **Chafariz das Virtudes** (1619) has a tall pediment with pyramids and the royal arms, an empty niche, and two giant stone masks spouting into two deep tanks ([Visit Porto](https://visitporto.travel/pt-PT/poi/5cd04b46f979e00001234af2)); it stood before the Postigo das Virtudes ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chafariz_das_Virtudes)).

**Model it:** Kit (design values): `Arcade_Bay_River` (a 4 m round arch, 3 m clear, three storeys above in tabique or stone, a deep dark undercroft as a separate unlit material); `FloodWall_River` (low granite parapet with boat steps); `Chafariz_Wall` (backrest plus a tank: 300-500 tris), `Chafariz_Virtudes` (pediment, two masks, two tanks: one hero mesh). Paint flood tide-lines on lower walls with vertex colour.

**Play it:** Arcades are a long covered gallery with shadow along the river front, ideal for slipping past the quay watch. The undercrofts double as boat stores and smugglers' rooms. Fountains are gathering points where NPCs chatter (noise cover) and water lights the scene with reflections.

## 6. Rua das Flores and Largo de São Domingos

**What and why:** The 16th-c. "noble street" of merchants' and gentry houses with coats of arms, cut through the bishop's gardens. It is the model for the Old Town's rich street of shops below, homes above, and yards behind.

**Features**
- Opened 1521-1525 through the bishop's flower gardens, from the Largo de São Domingos to the Porta de Carros. For the first time in the city, precise rules set the type of house to be built, regularising both sides of the street. Merchants (often ennobled), city and crown officials and some New Christians lived there; craftsmen (shoemakers, coppersmiths, locksmiths, masons, smiths) lived at the upper end. The Misericórdia church was begun in 1559. The Casa dos Sousa e Silva bears a coat of arms dated 1703 ([pt.wikipedia: Rua das Flores](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_das_Flores_(Porto))).
- **Corner marks:** each house was marked at its corners with the sign of its landlord: Saint Michael the Archangel for the cathedral chapter, Saint Catherine's wheel of knives for the bishop ([Casa dos Maias record](https://www.municipiosefreguesias.pt/ponto-de-interesse/4735/casa-dos-maias)).
- **Casa dos Maias** (16th c.): a U-plan house with two floors and a mezzanine. Eight balcony windows with wrought-iron railings and low triangular pediments face the street, above five oval windows and six doors; two wide doors carry identical coats of arms; the eaves sit on granite corbels. At the back, a paved patio at first-floor level is held up by a terrace wall with a dolphin and a basin (once a fountain) and an octagonal chapel. Inside, a broad stair of two side flights and one central flight is lined with granite columns ([municipiosefreguesias](https://www.municipiosefreguesias.pt/ponto-de-interesse/4735/casa-dos-maias)).
- **19th-c. changes:** 146 building permits from 1837-1916 show shops on the ground floor and homes above, back yards with gardens and also workshops and warehouses, extra storeys, regularised windows, and new glazed balconies (*marquises*) and porches. In 1838 one owner asked to replace a wide door with two regular doors and widen the slits to line up with the balcony windows of the piano nobile ([FLUP chapter](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/160448/2/681283.pdf)).
- The Largo de São Domingos got the city's first round fountain: a granite basin fountain of 1544 with two bowls on a fluted column, inscribed COMMVNI, removed in 1845 ([Águas do Porto](https://portowatercity.aguasdoporto.pt/pt/saber/saber-historico-de-fontes/)).

**Model it:** Kit (design values): `Flores_House_3bay` (6-7 m front, ground shop with two arched doors, mezzanine slits, a piano nobile of three balcony windows with pediment caps, a top floor of plain windows); `CornerStone_Mark` decals (Saint Michael and the knife-wheel, 256 px each); `CoatOfArms_Granite` (a 1 x 1.2 m relief: a normal-mapped quad or a 20-tri bump); `Patio_Terrace` (a walled yard one floor up, with a chapel box and a wall fountain); `Fountain_Bowls` (two-bowl basin, about 400 tris).

**Play it:** The corner marks tell the player who owns which house (chapter or bishop) and so whose watchmen respond. Rear patios one floor up connect over yard walls into the neighbours' upper floors. Shops on the ground floor mean shutters, counters, strongboxes and a night clerk.

**Images:** [Category: Rua das Flores (Porto)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Rua_das_Flores_(Porto)) · [Category: Casa dos Maias](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Casa_dos_Maias)

## 7. Vitória and the Judiaria do Olival

**What and why:** The second hill, settled later, held the Jewish quarter of 1386-1496, then a Benedictine monastery and the prison-court. It is the model for an Old Town quarter with its own gates and curfew.

**Features**
- In 1386 King João I gathered Porto's Jews into a walled quarter inside the city wall, on land of 30 courelas rented for 200 maravedis a year (contract of 1388). It had a fence with two gates, one to the Largo da Porta do Olival and one to the "Escadas da Esnoga" (today Escadas da Vitória). Its spine was Rua de São Miguel, with cross-lanes off it ([U.Porto](https://sigarra.up.pt/up/pt/web_base.gera_pagina?p_pagina=1006469)). Rua de São Miguel is 120 m long, opened in 1386. Jews moved freely by day but had to be back at night when the Trindades bell rang in the Olival gate tower ([pt.wikipedia: Rua de São Miguel](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_de_S%C3%A3o_Miguel_(Porto))).
- Two gates of solid iron controlled contact; houses had no entrances onto the "Christian streets", reinforced where needed by high walls ([Associação Comercial do Porto](https://cciporto.com/2020/07/31/sabia-que-o-cemiterio-israelita-ficava-na-encosta-das-virtudes/)).
- After the 1496 edict, converts' houses were marked with crosses cut into door jambs, lintels and bases ([Rede de Judiarias](https://www.redejudiariasportugal.com/index.php/pt/cidades/porto)). A hekhal (Torah ark niche) of a secret synagogue was found in 2003 at Rua de São Miguel no. 9 ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_de_S%C3%A3o_Miguel_(Porto))).
- On the hilltop, the monastery of São Bento da Vitória (from 1598) and the Tribunal e Cadeia da Relação, the prison-court of the later 18th c., reshaped Vitória ([SIPA: Zona histórica](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/Site/APP_PagesUser/SIPA.aspx?id=6144)).

**Model it:** Kit (design values): `QuarterGate_Iron` (a 2.5 m iron-plated door in a masonry arch with a small wicket); `BlindWall_HighFence` (4-5 m wall with no openings to the outer street); `Cross_Mark` decal set for jambs and lintels; a `HiddenNiche` interior prop (a wall cupboard that reveals an ark niche).

**Play it:** A quarter that locks at the evening bell becomes an inside job: get in before the bell, or come over the roofs. Houses that open only inward make the cross-lanes the only real streets, and walls that face outward are sheer. Cross marks are environmental storytelling and a breadcrumb to a hidden room.

## 8. Casa do Infante (the old customs house)

**What and why:** The crown's customs house and mint on the riverfront, the largest civil building of the medieval town. It is the model for a royal warehouse-and-counting-house "key building" where harbour and Old Town meet.

**Features**
- Begun in 1325 by Afonso IV as the royal store, against the bishop's wishes. The first building had two tall towers and a central courtyard; the upper floors of the north tower were a dwelling (the royal steward's), and tradition puts Prince Henry's birth there in 1394. In the 15th c. a forward block was added with an inscribed lintel and a niche for the Virgin. East of it stood the Mint (14th-c. origin), with the treasury accounts office and the notaries' hall nearby. In the 17th c. the front was pushed out to the street, the tower tops were cut down into two large covered halls (inscription 1677), and the Mint was partly demolished during a pause in coining (1607-1688). It served as customs until the 19th c., then as a private warehouse ([pt.wikipedia: Casa do Infante](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_do_Infante)).

**Model it:** A `Customs_Courtyard` set: two stump towers (8 x 8 m, design value) under hipped roofs, a courtyard (15 x 12 m, design value) with an arcade, a forward block with an inscription decal and a Virgin niche; interior halls with scales, sealed bales and a counting room. Hero budget about 2,000 tris (design value).

**Play it:** Customs seals and ledgers are mission targets; the Mint wing is the vault; the courtyard is lit and watched while the tower-top halls under the roof are dark storage reachable over the roofs.

## 9. Tower-houses: Rua de Baixo, D. Pedro Pitões, Pedro Sem and the Casa dos 24

**What and why:** Porto's medieval elite lived in stone tower-houses (*casas-torre*); several survive or were rebuilt. They are the Old Town's vertical "key buildings".

**Features**
- Tower-houses were among the largest houses; until the mid-14th c. building one needed the bishop's licence ([UCP: Viela dos Cónegos](https://repositorio.ucp.pt/bitstreams/53001d34-7231-4b6a-af96-02bcec5a7463/download)). One tower-house was split between two tenants "along the wall that carries the hall chimney", with a heated room (*estufa*) ([same](https://repositorio.ucp.pt/bitstreams/53001d34-7231-4b6a-af96-02bcec5a7463/download)).
- **Torre de D. Pedro Pitões** ("Torre Medieval"): hidden for centuries among the houses facing the cathedral, beside the old butchers' stalls. In the 1940s clearance it was kept, moved about 15 m and rebuilt by Rogério de Azevedo, who added a Gothic-style stone balcony ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_Medieval_do_Porto)).
- **Torre de Pedro Sem:** built in the first half of the 14th c. on a country estate outside the walls, by a chancellor of Afonso IV. The legend: a rich merchant watching his ships from the top boasted that not even God could make him poor, and a storm sank his fleet. A third floor was added after 1909 ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_de_Pedro_Sem)).
- **Torre da Rua de Baixo:** five storeys, 13th c. (Section 1). **Casa dos 24:** about 22 m, crenellated (Section 2).

**Model it:** One `TowerHouse` kit in three heights (3, 4 and 5 storeys at 3.2-3.5 m, design values), a 6 x 7 m footprint (design value), walls 1 m thick (design value), one room per floor, a straight stair against one wall or a spiral in a corner, crenellated or hipped top, lancet windows and one machicolated balcony as optional modules.

**Play it:** One room per floor means each floor is a puzzle: the stair is the only link unless you climb the outside. Tops give overwatch of the square. A tower-house buried among later houses can be reached from a neighbour's attic.

Pedro Pitões](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Torre_de_D._Pedro_Pit%C3%B5es) · [Category: Torre Pedro-Sem](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Torre_Pedro-Sem) · [Category: Torre da Rua de Baixo](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Torre_da_Rua_de_Baixo) · [Category: Arco de São Sebastião](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Arco_de_S%C3%A3o_Sebasti%C3%A3o)

## 10. Azulejo churches, the Casa Escondida and São Bento (tile reference)

**What and why:** Porto's tiled church fronts are the strongest single visual signature to borrow for the Old Town's churches and chapels, and the Carmo pair hides a gem for stealth: a house 1.5 m wide between two churches.

**Features**
- **Igreja do Carmo** (1756-1768, Rococo, by José Figueiredo Seixas): its side front carries a huge tile panel of the Carmelite founding and Mount Carmel, designed by Silvestre Silvestri, painted by Carlos Branco and made in Gaia, dated 1912. Because two churches could not stand wall to wall, the **Casa Escondida** ("hidden house"), only a little more than 1.5 m wide, was built between the Carmo and the Carmelitas church ([pt.wikipedia: Igreja do Carmo](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igreja_do_Carmo_(Porto))). The city museum dates it to 1768: several floors joined by one stair, with a bedroom, sitting room and kitchen. It housed chaplains, artists and craftsmen, later the order's hospital doctors, and by tradition secret meetings during the French invasions and the civil war ([Museu da Cidade](https://museudoporto.pt/en/recurso/um-objeto-e-seus-discursos-a-casa-escondida/)).
- **Capela das Almas** (Chapel of Santa Catarina; built from the late 18th c., enlarged 1801): until 1929 its outside was plain whitewash. In 1929 it was clad in 15,947 tiles over about 360 m², designed by Eduardo Leite and made by Viúva Lamego in Lisbon ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_Santa_Catarina)).
- **Santo Ildefonso** (1709-1739, granite, two bell towers): about 11,000 tiles by Jorge Colaço, laid in November 1932 ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Saint_Ildefonso)).
- **São Bento station** (built from 1904, opened 1916, on the site of a convent founded 1518 and demolished 1892): about 20,000 tiles over 551 m² by Jorge Colaço ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Bento_railway_station)).
- Note the dates: the great figurative panels are 1900s-1930s. For an earlier look, use plain 18th-c. church granite with tiles only inside (the cathedral cloister's 1729-1731 panels) and patterned 19th-c. tiles on houses (Section 12).

**Model it:** A `ChurchFront_Tiled` uses one granite frame mesh plus a large panel texture. At 256 px, use a 2x2 or 4x4 atlas of 256 px "panel scenes" with a separate tiling border strip, so a 7 m wall carries 4-8 unique 256 px tiles plus the border. Blue-white palettes compress well. The Casa Escondida is a `Slot_House_1.5` module: a 1.5 m wide stack of rooms with a ladder-stair, wedged between two big meshes.

**Play it:** The hidden house between two churches is a secret passage: in at street level, up four tiny floors, out through a door into either church's tribune or roof. Tiled walls are bright under moonlight, a "lit wall" that silhouettes the player.

## 11. Torre dos Clérigos (the landmark tower)

**What and why:** Porto's symbol and the tower seen from everywhere. Use it as the Old Town's orientation landmark (a bell tower or watch tower).

**Features:** The church was built 1732-1750 by Nicolau Nasoni; the tower was built 1754-1763 behind the church. It is 75.6 m high, with 240 steps to the top of its six floors ([en.wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cl%C3%A9rigos_Church)). It dominates the Rio da Vila valley between the two hills ([SIPA: Zona histórica](http://www.monumentos.gov.pt/Site/APP_PagesUser/SIPA.aspx?id=6144)).

**Play it:** A navigation anchor in a maze; a sniper and lookout post; a bell that can be rung to pull every guard in the district.

## 12. Context: 19th-century azulejo fronts and shopfronts (Flores, Santa Catarina, Aliados)

**What and why:** How whole house fronts came to be clad in tile, and what the tiles were like, for the Old Town's "Pombaline-to-19th-c." layer.

**Features**
- Between the late 18th and mid-19th c., Porto and Gaia had six semi-industrial tile works (Massarelos, Miragaia, Carvalhinho, Devesas, Cavaquinho and Santo António do Vale da Piedade), probably the first in Portugal to design patterns specifically for house fronts. Smooth tiles measured about 14.5-15.5 cm, usually set as a 2x2 pattern repeat. High-relief tiles (mainly Massarelos) had a big central rosette and four corner sprays, and each was a stand-alone piece; thinner half-relief tiles were typical of Devesas ([Vitruvius](http://vitruvius.com.br/revistas/read/arquitextos/06.069/379)). Half-relief tiles were about 15 mm thick, half the thickness of full relief ([LM Conservação](https://luismariz.wordpress.com/arte-e-tecnica/tecnica/)).
- In Porto the borders framing windows and doors used the same square module as the field tiles ([LNEC/Mariz](http://azulejos.lnec.pt/AzuRe/links/01%20Primeiras%20fachadas%20azulejadas.pdf)). In the North, pronounced relief and a taste for light-and-shade prevailed, while the South kept flat patterns ([Hayashida summary](https://exa.ai/library/publication/jxsh2th0hpc)). Devesas (founded c.1865) dominated the trade until about 1915 ([Patrimónios](https://patrimonios.pt/azulejo-de-fachada/)).
- Before mass production, fronts were rendered with sand-lime mortar and painted. Tile became the commonest cladding once it was mass-produced in the mid-19th c. ([Coimbra 2024, quoting Teixeira 2004](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Massarelos made smooth and relief tiles in 1873-1895, and in 1888 it clad the Lordelo church in about 13,000 tiles ([DGPC: Massarelos](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=71229)).
- **Shopfronts:** a typical early-1900s shop house (Rua Cândido dos Reis 75-79) has a ground-floor shop with a separate door to the stairs, two main floors and two recessed mansard floors. Its three bays at street level are the stair door and two wider shop openings ([DGPC: Cândido dos Reis](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=74507)). Rua das Flores permits (1837-1916) show shopfronts being "beautified" and windows regularised ([FLUP chapter](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/160448/2/681283.pdf)).

**Model it:** At 256 px a 15 cm tile is too small to draw per tile on a whole front. Use a **tiling azulejo texture of 8x8 tiles** (1.2 m repeat, 32 px per tile, design value) for the field, a **border strip** of one tile width (15 cm) around openings as separate UV islands, and a few **relief** variants faked with a baked normal or bevelled vertex-shaded tile sheet. Keep granite surrounds (lintels, sills, jambs) as geometry or a separate 256 px trim sheet. Shopfront module: `Shop_Ground_3bay` (stair door plus two wide shop doors with fanlights and iron grilles).

**Play it:** Tiled fronts reflect moonlight and lamplight: brighter walls, worse hiding. Plain rendered fronts are darker. Mixing them by period gives "light" and "dark" streets for free.

**Images:** [Category: Azulejos in Porto](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Azulejos_in_Porto) · [Category: Rua de Santa Catarina (Porto)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Rua_de_Santa_Catarina_(Porto))

## 13. The Porto house (casa burguesa) and its ancestors

**What and why:** The narrow, deep, tall house is the cell of the whole Old Town. "A big city made of small houses", as Veiga de Oliveira put it (quoted in [Teixeira, FAUP](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/77052/2/94625.pdf)). This section collects the facts; the kit specification follows.

**Features: lot and size**
- **Medieval / mercantile (to 17th c.):** irregular single-front lots 10-15 m deep and never more than 4.5 m wide, filling the whole lot. Regular two-front lots 20-30 m deep and up to 6 m wide also appear ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Another thesis gives a minimum of 3 m, a maximum of 6 m and usually 4.5 m, with two-front houses about twice as deep ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). Rua Nova leases give 5.19 x 7.10 m and 3.73 x 12.56 m (Section 3).
- **Almada era (later 18th c.):** a 6 m front, depth near 12 m or near 22 m, always with a back yard ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). Frontages were 5-7 m, the buildings had two fronts and three to five storeys, and almost always three openings per front ([Coimbra & Romão 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/by0jhdtkptk)). The Almada lot was 25-30 palmos wide ([Mota, "At home"](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/10716.pdf)). A surveyed example in Rua Pinheiro is 6 x 21 m with four storeys plus an added one ([Estratégias thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/76956/3/33052.1.pdf)).
- **19th c.:** lots stay 5.5-6 m wide and 15-20 m deep, up to 30 m ([Teixeira 2004 via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Five surveyed houses measure (on the party-wall axes) 4.23-8.42 m wide and 15.55-21.18 m long, with four or five storeys plus a recessed top ([Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).
- **Storeys:** in 1788 Rebelo da Costa wrote that houses ordinarily had three storeys, many had four and some five, besides upper kitchens and underground shops, and that their doorways, windows, corners and balconies were of fine dressed granite ([quoted in the Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). The two classic types are the **narrow tall house** (shop below, home above) and the rarer **broad low house** (ground floor plus a piano nobile, the noble house) ([Coimbra & Romão 2011](https://exa.ai/library/publication/by0jhdtkptk)).
- **Storey heights:** Teixeira's model house is about 11 m high for three storeys, and one thesis takes 3.20 m per storey as its working value ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). Five-storey houses have party walls 19.4-21.6 m high ([Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)), which implies about 3.5-4 m per floor (my derivation). Storey heights rose in the 19th c. ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Medieval storey heights: (not found).

**Features: structure**
- **Party walls** carry the floors. After the 17th c. they were granite rubble 30-60 cm thick, replacing older mixed tabique ([Teixeira 2004 via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)); surveyed ones are 0.30-0.46 m. Front walls do not carry the floors; surveyed front walls are 0.60-0.75 m, with window openings of 1.25-1.36 m ([same](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Of a wall framing a 1.25 m opening, about 20 cm holds the window frame and 40-50 cm the inner shutters, usually three leaves that fold into the reveal ([same](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).
- **Floors:** round logs 20-30 cm across, trimmed flat on two faces, 50-70 cm apart, spanning between the party walls and never more than 7 m long. Cross-pieces (*tarugos*) run about every 1.5 m, and floorboards are about 3 cm. Ceilings were boarded at first and plastered with ornaments from the mid-18th c. ([same](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Wealthier houses had chestnut ceilings shaped as a trough or coffers ([FEUP facade thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)).
- **Medieval fronts:** often only the ground floor was stone and the upper floors were tabique, slightly jettied for more room ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Many old fronts were simply tabique, jettied or flush, with timber window frames, balcony entablatures and eaves ([Coimbra & Romão 2011, after Oliveira & Galhano](https://exa.ai/library/publication/by0jhdtkptk)). Fear of fire in the 18th c. replaced tabique outer walls with stone ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Added top floors were mixed tabique on the old walls, and from about the mid-18th c. simple tabique set back from the front ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).

**Features: stair and light**
- **Single-front medieval house:** a straight flight against a side wall in houses of two sobrados, or two flights across the back in houses of three or more floors. **Two-front house:** two or three flights across the middle, which splits front from back ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). Usually two or three flights per storey with intermediate landings, ending under a skylight ([FEUP facade thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)). In the Rua Dr. Barbosa de Castro model the first two flights are stone and the top floor all timber ([Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).
- **Claraboia:** from the 18th c. the stair is lit from above by a conical, oval or round skylight ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Skylights come in three kinds: flat (one or two panes in the roof slope), simple structural ones (segmented or round, the commonest in central Porto) and complex structural ones that stand up like an umbrella ([same](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). An 18th-c. description says interior rooms were lit by "tall and large" skylights ([quoted in the Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). In deep houses the skylight also lit interior alcoves through windows cut in the stair walls ([same](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)).
- **Attic:** dormers (*trapeiras*), attic rooms and rooftop lookouts (*mirantes*) were added later to light and air habitable attics ([FEUP facade thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)).

**Features: room uses by period**
- **17th c. (mercantile):** a broad ground floor used as workplace or shop, with a separate street door to the floors above. Rooms were undivided: one space served as bedroom, dining room and sitting room. The kitchen, usually the only defined room, sat on the top floor by the roof to vent smoke ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). A general Portuguese pattern (from a Viseu study): in two-storey houses the kitchen is the middle room upstairs, the hall faces the street and the chambers are at the back by the yard; in houses of three or more storeys the kitchen is on the top floor ([Viseu study](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/13662.pdf)).
- **18th c. (Almada era):** sometimes a mezzanine between the shop and the first floor; the street-facing room now receives visitors ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). The kitchen stays at the back of the top floor ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). In the Rua Pinheiro house, the shop has its own entry to the family home above. A central skylit stair divides front and back halls, each reached through an antechamber. The kitchen is on the top floor (identified by its sink and oven), and a column of privies outside the rear wall is reached by long balconies ([Estratégias thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/76956/3/33052.1.pdf)).
- **19th c. (Liberal):** sanitary rooms became compulsory against the rear wall. The kitchen moved down to the ground floor at the back by the yard, the floors above held the bedrooms, and the attic held the servants ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Purely residential houses appear, with a raised half-basement for service rooms, a very tall front door, and a central balcony over three openings ([Protótipo thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/80258/2/23654.pdf)). Veiga de Oliveira: after about 1825 home and shop separate, but houses kept the narrow tall type, with an office instead of the shop, its street windows often barred ([Mota, "At home"](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/10716.pdf)). Late-19th-c. houses add a vestibule, an office by the entrance, a parlour, a dining room toward the yard, small sitting rooms for the women, and billiard or smoking rooms for the men ([same](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/10716.pdf)).

**Features: openings, balconies, roofs, yards, water**
- **Windows:** wooden sash windows (*guilhotina*) and casements (*batente*), both as half-height *janelas de peito* and full-height balcony doors (*sacada*) ([FEUP facade thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf); [OERN manual contents](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/67832/2/46399.pdf)). Ground floors had painted wooden doors of one or two leaves, closing against the dressed granite jambs ([FEUP](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)). See `houses_ribeira.md` for the sash date (after 1703) and rótula lattice shutters.
- **Balconies:** first timber, later stone. The slab is about 50 cm deep (few exceed it) and 15-20 cm thick, on granite corbels (*cachorros*), sometimes a single slab counterweighted inside. From the 19th c. slabs grew thicker and lost their corbels, which then survive only at the back under the balconies leading to the privies. Railings are wrought or cast iron ([FEUP](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)). Iron railings replaced timber in the 18th c. ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)). Rear balconies were often enclosed as timber *marquises* ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).
- **Roofs:** mostly hipped (four slopes): two main slopes over the party walls and two short hips (*tacaniças*) over the fronts. Trusses sit on the party walls about every 3 m, with rafters every 50 cm. Tiles were first canal (Roman) tiles set in mortar on boarding; from the mid-19th c. flat interlocking Marseille tiles allowed two-slope roofs and attic rooms ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Eaves are two or three courses of tiles, with long first-course tiles of up to 80 cm. Bare gable walls are hung with slate or tiles, later corrugated zinc ([FEUP](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/130062/2/428600.pdf)). English residents brought the parapet in the 18th c. ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). Roof pitch: (not found).
- **Yards:** medieval houses had walled yards with orange, lemon, plum and fig trees ([UCP](https://repositorio.ucp.pt/bitstreams/53001d34-7231-4b6a-af96-02bcec5a7463/download)). The 19th c. organised yards as gardens and vegetable plots ([IPP report](https://recipp.ipp.pt/bitstreams/2313d4db-5ba0-4a0d-b2c5-6d430120596f/download)), sometimes with workshops or warehouses ([FLUP](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/160448/2/681283.pdf)). Because building depth stays constant while yard depth varies, the street, not the block, was the planning unit ([Coimbra & Romão](https://exa.ai/library/publication/by0jhdtkptk)).
- **Latrines and water:** medieval privies were rare and their location is unknown (Section 3). From the early 19th c. a privy block stood at the back, reached by balconies (above). Water came from public fountains fed by five supply systems, Paranhos being the main one ([Diogo Teixeira thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/57345/2/TESEMESDIOGOTEIXEIRA000147897.pdf)), and was carried by Galician water-carriers ([Mota, "At home"](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/10716.pdf)). Private wells in houses: (not found).
- **Cellars:** "underground shops" in 1788 (above). Raised basements in the 19th c. Foundations are shallow on rock and on timber piles near the rivers ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)).

**Features: furniture and objects (dates; mostly Portuguese, not Porto-specific)**
- **17th-c. merchants' houses** (Inquisition statements): chairs (low, high-backed, leather, gilded), stools (*tamboretes*), folding beds (*catres*) and curtained beds (*leitos*), chests (*arcas*, cedar or leather-covered), Indian coffers (*caixões da Índia*), cabinets (*contadores*), writing desks (*escritórios*), sideboard-tables with drawers (*bufetes*), folding tables, a candle-stand (*velador*) and a domestic oratory ([Braga 2009](https://exa.ai/library/publication/113tl2n36j8)).
- **A 1780 Lisbon judge's house, room by room:** in the first hall, 12 walnut armchairs in crimson damask, 12 stools, two card tables (one with a draughts board), seven taffeta pelmets and a six-light crystal chandelier. Elsewhere: bathtubs, single beds, and chests measured in palmos; the attic (*águas-furtadas*) held old portraits, a mattress chest 8 palmos long and a travel box ([Madureira inventory, transcribed](https://www.yumpu.com/pt/document/view/31062183/a-casa-senhorial-em-portugal-nos-seculos-xv-ao-xix/149)). An aristocratic inventory (1749-50) adds gilt tables, 22 paintings in an antechamber and corner shelves for china ([Conde de Pombeiro](https://acasasenhorial.org/acs/index.php/pt/fontes-documentais/inventarios/365-conde-de-pombeiro-1749-1750)).
- **Late-18th-c. Porto under British influence:** stools and chairs sent from Porto in 1742, floor mats, desk-cabinets (*papeleiras*) with gilt fittings, wall oratories, English-style armchairs, and Indian tea services ([Arouca cell study](https://exa.ai/library/publication/r24lfvnfth6)). Local faience came from Massarelos (from 1766) and Miragaia ([DGPC: Massarelos](https://imovel.patrimoniocultural.gov.pt/detalhes.php?code=71229)).

## 14. The night city: lights, watch, bells, fountains and shrines

**What and why:** The systems that make Thief-style night play feel Porto-real: when and how streets were lit, who patrolled, the curfew, and the water and shrine points that structure a route.

**Features**
- **Lighting:** the first street lamps went up in 1771-72 on the Rua Nova, paid for and kept by the residents. In 1816 the 800 lamps the city needed were judged too expensive. Government-run oil lighting came in 1824; lamps were lit only on dark nights, never at full moon, and walkers carried their own lantern or had servants carry one ([PortoArc](http://portoarc.blogspot.com/2016/07/companhia-do-gas-e-companhia-de-luz.html)). Porto and Gaia had public lighting from 1824 ([Bracara Augusta](http://revistabracaraaugusta.cm-braga.pt/index.php/RBA/article/download/13/13)). Gas came from 1855: 1,157 lamps in 1862, more than 2,500 in 1890 and more than 3,500 in 1900 ([Porto de Antanho](http://portodeantanho.blogspot.com/2025/06/25279-iluminacao-publica-e-companhia-do.html)). Alberto Pimentel described streets lit by dim lamps of purging-nut oil, and the Arco de Vandoma by the cathedral as a place so dark it suggested ambushes ([JN](https://www.jn.pt/arquivo/artigo/que-policiamento-havia-na-cidade-de-quinhentos/453486)).
- **Curfew and watch:** an old custom had the alcaide and his men guard the town at night after the bell had rung three times; anyone found abroad late was arrested, disarmed and taken to the tower prison. In 1392 the council ordered the bell rung so the city would be guarded, people would go home, and fires would be covered (lights put out). The bell hung in the Porta do Olival tower until 1583, then at the Porta de Cima de Vila, then in a cathedral tower ([JN](https://www.jn.pt/arquivo/artigo/que-policiamento-havia-na-cidade-de-quinhentos/453486)). The **quadrilheiros** (from 1383, in Lisbon first) were citizen-watchmen with a green staff bearing the royal arms. In 1570 the king ordered quadrilheiros for every quarter of Lisbon and of the other cities. Under Lisbon's 1603 rules each street chose a man who could call on 20 neighbours; they watched inns, taverns and gambling houses and were nicknamed "bats" or "night men" ([GEO Lisboa](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/o-quadrilheiro); [PSP history](https://www.psp.pt/Pages/sobre-nos/quem-somos/historia.aspx)). In Lisbon after 1755 every household had to supply an armed man for night rounds ([GEO Lisboa](https://geo.lisboa.pt/atividades-e-difusao/investigacao/detalhe/o-quadrilheiro)). Porto's Guarda Real da Polícia appeared in 1808. Before 1832 it moved its post to the Largo do Corpo da Guarda by the cathedral, in a former bishop's tower-prison, and the Guarda Municipal replaced it in 1835 ([Porto de Antanho](http://portodeantanho.blogspot.com/2022/07/)). Its Lisbon model (1801) wore a short blue coat with red facings and a black waxed-leather cap; the Porto uniform itself: (not found) ([AHM](https://ahm-exercito.defesa.gov.pt/details?id=87955)).
- **Fountains:** 17th-18th-c. Porto had few large fountains because water was scarce; the supply came from five systems with stone water chambers (*arcas*) ([Diogo Teixeira thesis](https://repositorio-aberto.up.pt/bitstream/10216/57345/2/TESEMESDIOGOTEIXEIRA000147897.pdf)). Examples: Colher (1491), Virtudes (1619), São Domingos (1544), São Miguel (Nasoni era), São Sebastião (Sections 2, 5, 6).
- **Alminhas:** small shrines to the souls in purgatory, set in wall niches or house fronts, at crossroads and bridge ends. People left candles, flowers or an oil lamp; they were painted reliefs, canvases or stone, and later mostly tile ([pt.wikipedia: Alminhas](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alminhas)). The Alminhas da Ponte in the Ribeira (a bronze of 1897) recall the 1809 collapse of the bridge of boats; candles are still left there ([pt.wikipedia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alminhas_da_Ponte)). Rua dos Mercadores has two alminhas, one of wrought iron ([Vias Romanas](https://www.viasromanas.pt/doc/SePorto_Mercadores.html)).
- **Covered passages and blocked alleys:** the Arco das Verdades (Section 1), the 19th-c. arches in the wall (Section 4), the Miragaia arcades (Section 5), the blocked Mercadores back alley (Section 3), and houses over the culverted stream (Section 3).

**Model it:** Light sources (design values): `Lamp_Oil_Bracket` (a wall bracket every 30-40 m on main lanes only, small warm radius, off on "full moon" nights); `Shrine_Alminha` (a 0.6 x 0.9 m niche with a votive oil lamp: a tiny flicker light that marks routes); `Lantern_NPC` (a carried light for watchmen and late walkers). The watch: a `Quadrilheiro` (citizen with green staff, lantern, low alertness) and the later `Guarda` (uniformed, armed). A bell in the Old Town's gate tower rings the curfew; after it, anyone abroad is "suspicious".

**Play it:** The pre-1824 city is dark: light comes only from shrines, windows, fountains' reflections and the watch's own lanterns, which suits Thief's lightgem perfectly. Full-moon nights could be the hard difficulty, with the moon lighting tile fronts and no lamps lit. The curfew bell changes AI rules mid-mission. Shrines are safe-looking light pools the player must cross or snuff (snuffing one could alert a pious neighbour).

## The Porto house: a kit specification

All values below are **design values** unless a Section 13 source is named. The aim: a few modules that combine into hundreds of believable houses at PS2 budgets (about 300-900 tris per house exterior, one 256 px atlas per material set, vertex-painted light and grime).

### Grid and core dimensions
- **Plan grid:** 0.5 m. **Lot widths:** `W3` = 3.0 m, `W45` = 4.5 m (medieval norm), `W6` = 6.0 m (Almada norm), `W75` = 7.5 m (rich 19th-c.). Sources: 3-6 m, usually 4.5 m (medieval); 6 m (Almada); 5.5-6 m (19th c.); up to 8.4 m (survey).
- **Depths:** `D10`, `D12`, `D15`, `D20`, `D22` (Section 13: 10-15 m single-front; about 12 or about 22 m Almada; 15-20 m 19th c.).
- **Storey heights (floor to floor):** ground/shop `H_G` = 3.8 m (taller shop floor, per `houses_ribeira.md`); upper floors `H_U` = 3.2 m (working value from the Protótipo thesis); 19th-c. piano nobile `H_N` = 3.6 m; attic knee wall 1.2 m.
- **Walls:** party wall 0.40 m (survey 0.30-0.46 m); front wall 0.60 m stone (survey 0.60-0.75 m) or 0.15 m tabique for medieval upper floors and added top floors; interior partitions 0.10 m tabique.
- **Openings:** standard bay 1.25 m wide (survey 1.25-1.36 m). Window sill 0.9 m above the floor, head 2.4 m. Balcony door 1.25 x 2.8 m. Shop door 1.6-2.2 m wide, arched or with a granite lintel. Three bays per 6 m front (Almada rule); two bays per 4.5 m front.

### Modules (names, size, what is geometry vs texture)
1. `Wall_Party_*`: a shared side wall per lot, never textured on its hidden face. Geometry: one box. Texture: granite rubble 256 px; where it rises above a neighbour, slate or tile hanging (vertex-coloured dark).
2. `Front_Stone_Bay_[G|U|N|A]`: one 1.25 m opening in a 2 m wide wall panel per floor type (G ground, U upper, N noble, A attic). Geometry: opening reveal with a 0.20 m frame recess (outside) and an inner reveal for folding shutters; granite surround as a 4-quad bevel. Texture: render or azulejo field (tiling) plus a granite trim sheet. About 40-60 tris per bay.
3. `Front_Tabique_Jetty`: medieval upper floor, 0.4 m oversail on a joist-end row. Geometry: a slab lip plus a flat front. Texture: lime render over timber, with visible posts as a decal (Oliveira & Galhano's fachwerk-like tabique).
4. `Balcony_Slab_[Single|Run]`: 0.50 m deep, 0.15 m thick granite slab on 2-3 corbels (single), or a full-width run. The railing is an alpha card (wrought-iron or cast-iron pattern, 256 x 128 px). Rear variant: `Balcony_Rear_Long` on big corbels, leading to `Privy_Tower`.
5. `Privy_Tower`: a 1.2 x 1.2 m tabique box stacked up the rear wall, one per floor, 19th c. only.
6. `Door_Street_[1leaf|2leaf]` + `Fanlight_Iron` (painted wood; the iron fanlight is an alpha card).
7. `Stair_Core_[Straight|TwoFlight|ThreeFlight]`: Straight = one flight along a party wall (medieval, two sobrados). TwoFlight = two flights across the house mid-depth with a half landing (Almada standard, a 3.2-3.5 m deep stair box; Coimbra's macro uses 0.18 m risers and 0.23 m treads). ThreeFlight for 3.8 m storeys. The first two flights are stone, the rest timber. The open well (*bomba*) is 0.6-1.0 m.
8. `Claraboia_[Flat|Round|Oval|Umbrella]`: over the stair box. Flat = a glazed quad in the roof slope. Round/Oval = an 8-12-segment drum and cone (60-90 tris). Umbrella = a raised lantern with its own little roof. Inside, a `SkyCeiling` glass panel at the top floor, emissive at day, dark blue at night.
9. `Roof_Hip_[W][D]`: four-slope roof with hips over the fronts; canal tiles (pre-1850) or Marseille (post-1850). Eaves = a 2-3 course tile overhang strip (a separate mesh row with an alpha edge). Pitch: (not found); use 25-30 degrees for canal tile, 30-35 for Marseille (design values). `Roof_Gable_Marseille` for 19th-c. two-slope roofs.
10. `Trapeira` (a dormer, 1.0 m wide) and `Mirante` (a glazed rooftop lookout box, 2 x 2 x 2.2 m, zinc-clad).
11. `Parapet_Granite` (18th-c. English-style, 0.8 m high, with balusters as an alpha strip) and `Cornice_Granite`.
12. `Azulejo_Field` (8x8 tile repeat over 1.2 m; flat, relief and bevel variants), `Azulejo_Border` (one-tile strip around openings), `Azulejo_Panel_Saint` (a 4x6-tile devotional panel above a door; the street shrine of the 19th c.).
13. `Shopfront_[Medieval|Almada|19c]`: medieval = a wide arched opening with timber boards. Almada = two arched doors plus a stair door with small square windows above. 19th c. = a stair door plus two wide glazed doors with iron grilles.
14. `Quintal_[Short|Long]`: a walled yard (2.5 m walls) with a fruit tree, a well-head prop (private wells: not found, so use it sparingly), a workshop shed (19th c.), or a vine trellis.

### Room list per floor (by period)
- **Medieval (to 1500), 4.5 x 12 m, ground plus 2:** G = *loja* (shop/store or workshop; animals in poorer houses), stone; 1 = one undivided room (sleeping, eating, sitting) with a chimney; 2 = kitchen at the back under the roof, plus a store; yard with a privy only sometimes.
- **Mercantile (1500-1700), 4.5-6 x 15-25 m, ground plus 2-3:** G = shop at the front, separate stair door, store behind; 1 = hall to the street, chambers to the yard; 2 = chambers; top = kitchen at the back (smoke).
- **Almada (1760-1820), 6 x 22 m, ground plus mezzanine plus 3:** G = shop, stair door, store; M = shop office/storage (mezzanine slits); 1 (noble) = front *sala* for visitors with an antechamber, stair hall, back *sala*, interior alcoves lit from the stair; 2 = front and back chambers with alcoves; 3 = kitchen at the back (hearth, oven, stone sink), pantry, maids' corner at the front; attic = storage and servants.
- **Liberal (1830-1900), 6-7.5 x 20 m, raised basement plus 3 plus attic:** basement = kitchen, laundry, coal and wine; 1 = vestibule, office (barred street window), parlour at the front, dining room at the back by the yard stair; 2 = bedrooms front and back with a dressing room; 3 = bedrooms or a study; attic = servants' rooms under dormers; privies on the rear balconies.

### Plan variants
1. **`Narrow_SingleBay` (W3-W45 x D10, medieval):** single front, no yard; a straight stair along one party wall; one room per floor; a jettied tabique front over a stone shop; kitchen at the top; a ladder to the roof hatch. Use: Barredo and the stair-lanes. Play: one stair, one room per floor; climb the front instead.
2. **`DoubleBay_Almada` (W6 x D22, 18th c.):** two fronts (street and yard), three bays per front, a two-flight stair across the middle under a round claraboia, alcoves lit from the stair, kitchen on the top floor, long rear balconies. Use: Rua das Flores-type streets. Play: front/back halves with the stair as the hub; the skylight is a roof entry straight into the stairwell.
3. **`Corner_House` (W6 x D12, two street fronts, any period):** on a corner the house is more square ([Viseu study](https://ler.letras.up.pt/uploads/ficheiros/13662.pdf)), and the front walls carry floor ends ([Teixeira via Coimbra 2024](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstreams/5c227e23-5209-458e-81f5-0696c40f0f2e/download)). A corner balcony wraps both fronts; a corner shrine (alminha) on the cornerstone; a three-flight stair in the inner corner. Play: two streets watched from one balcony; the shrine light marks the corner.
4. **`House_AgainstWall` (W45-W6 x D12, rear wall = city wall):** no rear windows; skylight on the rear slope (the Barbosa de Castro model); top-floor door onto the wall-walk; a cellar cut into the wall foot. Play: the house is a ladder onto the wall and a back door out of the city.
5. **`ShopHouse_Merchant` (W6-W75 x D20, ground plus 3 plus a mirante):** a vaulted stone shop with a counting room at the back, a separate stair door, the owner's rooms above, a rooftop *mirante* for watching the river, and a yard warehouse. Play: strongbox in the counting room, ledgers in the office, and the mirante as an overwatch and roof entry.
6. **`Slot_House_1.5` (W1.5, between two big buildings):** the Casa Escondida type, several floors with a ladder-stair and one room each. Play: a secret connector between two key buildings.
